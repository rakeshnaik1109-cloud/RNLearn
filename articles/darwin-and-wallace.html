Darwin and Wallace: Evolution by Natural Selection, and How a Theory Is Built

A wealthy gentleman naturalist and a self-taught specimen collector hit on the same idea, half a world apart. Their theory could not be proved by one experiment, so they built it from a mountain of evidence.

1. How Is a Theory Built from Evidence?

Newton’s laws could be tested by calculation and measurement (Part 6). But how do you establish a theory about events that took millions of years and that no one could ever watch? Charles Darwin, a wealthy English naturalist, and Alfred Russel Wallace, a self-taught collector who earned his living selling specimens, independently discovered how species change: evolution by natural selection. Their theory explained the diversity of life without any designer, and placed human beings within nature. [1, 2]

It was not proved by a single experiment. Darwin called the Origin of Species “one long argument”: a vast web of evidence from breeding, geography, fossils, anatomy and embryology, all explained by one idea. Their story shows how a historical science builds its case, and what Hume’s problem of induction (Part 7) looks like in practice.

3. Darwin and the Voyage of the Beagle

Charles Darwin was born in Shrewsbury in 1809, the son of a prosperous doctor and grandson of the potter Josiah Wedgwood and of the physician-poet Erasmus Darwin, who had himself speculated about evolution. He abandoned medicine in Edinburgh, unable to bear surgery without anaesthetic, and studied for the Church at Cambridge, where his real passions were beetle collecting and geology. In 1831, aged 22, he joined HMS Beagle as a gentleman naturalist on a survey voyage that lasted almost five years. [1]

He read Charles Lyell’s Principles of Geology on board, with its picture of the Earth shaped by slow, ordinary processes over immense time, and saw it confirmed: raised beaches in Chile after an earthquake, fossils of giant extinct mammals resembling living South American animals. In the Galápagos Islands in 1835 he noticed that mockingbirds differed from island to island; back in London, the ornithologist John Gould told him that his small brown birds, now called Darwin’s finches, were a group of distinct but closely related species. By 1837 Darwin was convinced that species are not fixed, and sketched in his notebook a branching tree, headed “I think”. [1, 2]

4. Wallace: A Naturalist Who Paid His Own Way

Alfred Russel Wallace was born in 1823 in Usk, Monmouthshire, into a family of modest means, and left school at 13 to work as a surveyor. Inspired by travel books, in 1848 he sailed to the Amazon with his friend Henry Walter Bates to collect specimens for sale, hoping to solve “the problem of the origin of species”. After four years, the ship carrying him home caught fire and sank with almost all his collections. [3] Undeterred, he spent 1854–62 in the Malay Archipelago, travelling some 22,000 km and collecting more than 125,000 specimens. In 1855 he published a paper arguing that every species has come into existence close in space and time to a pre-existing, closely allied species, which hinted strongly at descent. [4] He also noticed a sharp boundary, running between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi, separating Asian animals from Australian ones: the Wallace Line, which we now know marks a deep-water channel that ancient land bridges never crossed.

5. The Struggle for Existence

In September 1838, reading Thomas Malthus’s Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), Darwin found the missing piece. [5] Malthus argued that populations tend to grow geometrically while food grows only arithmetically, so most of those born must die before reproducing. Darwin saw that in nature, where every species produces far more offspring than can survive, individuals with variations that help them survive and reproduce would leave more descendants, and those variations would accumulate. He called this natural selection, by analogy with the artificial selection that breeders use to create new varieties of pigeons, dogs and crops.

The argument has three ingredients: individuals vary; much of the variation is inherited; and more are born than can survive, so variants that improve survival or reproduction spread. Given enough time, small changes add up to new species. Wallace, lying ill with malaria in the Moluccas in 1858, remembered Malthus and reached the same idea. [6]

6. 1858: Two Papers, One Theory

Darwin spent twenty years gathering evidence and writing privately, including an essay in 1844 that he instructed his wife to publish if he died. He also spent eight years on a monumental study of barnacles. He feared the reaction to a materialist theory, as the anonymous evolutionary book Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation (1844) had been savaged by scientists. In June 1858 a letter arrived from Wallace, enclosing an essay that set out natural selection so closely that Darwin wrote to Lyell: “I never saw a more striking coincidence.” [2]

Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker arranged for Wallace’s essay and extracts from Darwin’s unpublished writings to be read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Neither author was present: Wallace was in the Moluccas, and Darwin was burying his infant son. The society’s president remarked, at the end of the year, that it had not been marked by any striking discoveries. [6] Wallace, when he learned what had happened, was gracious; he later called his own book on the subject Darwinism.

7. On the Origin of Species

Darwin then wrote what he called an “abstract” of his big book, published on 24 November 1859 as On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection. [7] It assembled evidence of many kinds:

  • Domestication: breeders had produced dramatic changes in a few centuries.
  • Geography: island species resemble those of the nearest mainland, not those of islands with similar climates elsewhere.
  • Fossils: extinct species in each region resemble the living ones there, and the record shows change over time, though with gaps he attributed to its imperfection.
  • Anatomy and embryology: the same bones arranged differently in a human arm, a bat’s wing and a whale’s flipper; vestigial organs; and similar embryos in very different animals.

No single item proved the theory; their combined weight did. The philosopher William Whewell had called this a consilience of inductions: a theory gains credibility when it unexpectedly explains many different kinds of facts. [8] The book closed with an image of an “entangled bank” full of life, and the words: “from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.”

8. Selection in Action: The Peppered Moth

Could natural selection actually be seen? One of the first examples was the peppered moth in industrial England. A dark form was first recorded near Manchester in 1848; by the end of the century it made up the great majority of moths around industrial cities, where soot had blackened tree trunks and killed pale lichens. In the 1950s Bernard Kettlewell released marked moths and found that birds ate the conspicuous ones more often. [9] After clean-air laws, the pale form returned. Critics later questioned aspects of Kettlewell’s experiments, but a seven-year study by Michael Majerus, published after his death in 2012, confirmed that bird predation drives the change. [10]

9. Reception and Objections

The idea that species evolve was accepted by most naturalists within a decade or two; natural selection as its main mechanism was much more controversial. Serious objections came from science, not only religion. The engineer Fleeming Jenkin argued in 1867 that a favourable variation would be diluted away by interbreeding, if, as most believed, inheritance blends the parents’ traits. The physicist William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) calculated from the cooling of the Earth that it was perhaps 100 million years old or less, too little time for evolution. Darwin had no answer to either. [1] Both objections were later removed: by Mendel’s particulate inheritance, rediscovered in 1900, and by the discovery of radioactive heating, which also gave a way to date rocks (Part 9).

The theory’s implications for humans, set out in Darwin’s Descent of Man (1871), were deeply disturbing to many. Wallace himself came to argue that natural selection could not explain the higher human mental faculties, and was drawn to spiritualism, to Darwin’s dismay. [3]

10. Evolution Observed: Darwin’s Finches

Darwin assumed that selection was too slow to watch. Since 1973, Peter and Rosemary Grant have studied the finches of the tiny Galápagos island Daphne Major, measuring and tagging almost every bird. In the drought of 1977, most small, soft seeds disappeared and the population of medium ground finches crashed; survivors had, on average, larger, deeper beaks able to crack the remaining large, hard seeds. Because beak depth is strongly inherited, their offspring had measurably deeper beaks. [11, 12] Later droughts and wet years pushed the population back and forth. Evolution by natural selection, it turned out, can be measured in a single generation.

11. The Modern Synthesis and Beyond

Gregor Mendel’s 1866 work on peas showed that inheritance is carried by discrete units, later called genes, that do not blend. When it was rediscovered in 1900, it at first seemed to conflict with Darwin’s gradual change; in the 1920s and 1930s, Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright showed mathematically that Mendelian genetics and natural selection fit together perfectly, and naturalists such as Theodosius Dobzhansky and Ernst Mayr built the modern synthesis. [13] Wright and later Motoo Kimura emphasised that chance, genetic drift, also changes gene frequencies, especially in small populations. Since the 1950s, DNA has provided overwhelming new evidence: the genetic code is shared by all life, and family trees built from DNA match those built from anatomy and fossils. As Dobzhansky put it in 1973, “nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.” [14]

12. Two Lives After 1858

Darwin, often ill with a mysterious chronic illness, worked at his country home, Down House in Kent, writing books on orchids, climbing plants, insectivorous plants, the expression of emotions and earthworms, many based on careful experiments in his garden. He died in 1882 and was buried in Westminster Abbey, near Newton. [1] Wallace returned to England in 1862 and wrote The Malay Archipelago (1869), one of the great travel books, founded the science of biogeography, campaigned for land reform, women’s suffrage and against vaccination, and lived until 1913. For a century he was overshadowed; today he is recognised as Darwin’s co-discoverer.

13. A Historical Science

Evolutionary biology, like geology and cosmology, studies past events that cannot be repeated. It is tested not by rerunning history but by predictions about evidence not yet found: that fossils intermediate between fish and land animals should exist in rocks of the right age (found in 2004 as Tiktaalik), that rabbits should never be found in Precambrian rocks, that DNA trees should match anatomical ones. Each such test could have failed. Combined with direct observations of selection in moths, finches, bacteria evolving antibiotic resistance and viruses evolving in a pandemic, the theory is among the best-supported in science.

14. Try It Yourself: Watching Selection

Try it yourself: be Darwin for an afternoon
  1. Variation: measure the length of 30 leaves from one tree, or 30 peas from one pod packet. Plot a histogram. How much do “identical” individuals vary?
  2. Selection game: scatter 50 red and 50 green paper dots on grass. Give a friend 20 seconds to pick up as many as possible. Which colour survives better? Let each survivor “reproduce” by adding a dot of the same colour and repeat.
  3. Drift: put 5 red and 5 white beads in a cup, draw 10 with replacement to make the next generation, and repeat. How many generations until one colour disappears?
  4. Homology: compare pictures of the arm bones of a human, a cat, a whale and a bat. Which bones correspond?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: “monkey on his grandmother’s side”

The legend: at Oxford in June 1860, Bishop Samuel Wilberforce asked Thomas Henry Huxley whether he was descended from a monkey on his grandfather’s or his grandmother’s side; Huxley replied that he would rather have an ape for a grandfather than a man who used his gifts to obscure the truth, and science triumphed over religion.

The record: no transcript exists, and contemporary reports are brief and inconsistent; the famous exchange was written down in its polished form decades later. [15] Some present thought Wilberforce had the better of the debate, and Hooker, writing to Darwin, claimed he himself had made the most effective reply. Wilberforce’s published review of the Origin raised serious scientific points that Darwin acknowledged.

The grain of truth: there was a heated exchange that day, and Huxley did make a sharp retort about apes. The meeting became a symbol of the struggle over evolution, but the real story is less one of science versus faith than of a contested scientific question argued in public.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: can a historical theory be tested?

Karl Popper (Part 12) once suggested that Darwinism was not a testable scientific theory but a “metaphysical research programme”, and that “survival of the fittest” was a tautology: the fittest are simply those who survive. Biologists objected that fitness is defined independently, by features such as beak strength in a particular environment, and that the theory makes many risky predictions. In 1978 Popper publicly withdrew his criticism. [16]

The episode shows that science is not only about controlled experiments. Historical sciences reason like detectives, seeking the explanation that best accounts for many independent clues, and testing it against new clues. Darwin’s achievement is also a lesson in persistence: twenty years of gathering evidence before publishing, and honest discussion of the difficulties of his own theory in a chapter devoted to them.

Questions to think about: Is “the survival of the fittest” a tautology? What observation would refute the theory of evolution?
