The Milky Way and Other Galaxies: Leavitt’s Law, Shapley’s Clusters and Hubble’s Cepheid

A woman measuring dots on glass plates at Harvard found a law that turned pulsating stars into yardsticks. Within thirteen years it had pushed the Sun to the edge of the Galaxy and revealed a universe of galaxies.

1. What Is the Milky Way?

On a dark night, far from city lights, a faint glowing band arches across the sky. Every culture has a name for it: a river, a road, a spill of milk. Part 8 showed how parallax finally measured the distances to the nearest stars. But parallax only reaches the stars around us. To find the size and shape of the Milky Way, and to learn whether anything lies beyond it, astronomers needed a new kind of measuring stick.

They found it in a discovery made by Henrietta Swan Leavitt, who worked at the Harvard College Observatory measuring stars on photographic plates. Her law for a class of pulsating stars led, within thirteen years, to two revolutions: the Sun is not at the centre of our Galaxy, and our Galaxy is only one of a vast number. [1]

3. A River of Stars

In 1610 Galileo turned his telescope on the Milky Way and found that its glow comes from “a mass of innumerable stars”, too faint and too close together to see individually. In 1750 the English astronomer Thomas Wright suggested that the stars form a vast flattened layer, and that the band is what we see when we look along it. Immanuel Kant took the idea further in 1755: the Milky Way is a rotating disc of stars, and the faint elliptical glows called nebulae might be other such systems, so far away that their stars blur together. [2] The phrase “island universes” was later attached to this idea. It was a brilliant guess, but for 170 years there was no way to test it.

4. Herschel Counts the Stars

William Herschel, whom we met hunting parallax in Part 8, tried to map the Milky Way by counting. In 1785 he published counts of the stars visible in his telescope in hundreds of directions, assuming that all stars are roughly equally bright and that he could see to the edge of the system. [3] Where he saw more stars, the system must extend farther. The result was a flattened, irregular disc, several times wider than it is thick, with the Sun near the middle.

A century later, the Dutch astronomer Jacobus Kapteyn repeated the exercise with photographic plates and statistics, and in 1922 he too placed the Sun near the centre of a disc about 40,000 light-years across. Both maps had the same hidden flaw: dust between the stars dims distant light, so in every direction along the Milky Way we see only a limited distance. Wherever you stand in a fog, you seem to be at the centre of the clear patch.

5. How Bright Is a Star?

The key to distances beyond parallax is the difference between how bright a star is and how bright it looks. Light from a star spreads out over the surface of an ever-growing sphere, whose area increases with the square of the radius. Twice as far away, the same light is spread over four times the area, so the star looks a quarter as bright. This is the inverse-square law:

$$\text{apparent brightness} = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2},$$

where L is the star’s true power output, its luminosity. If you know L and measure the apparent brightness, you can solve for the distance d. An object of known luminosity is called a standard candle. Astronomers usually express brightness as magnitudes, a backwards logarithmic scale inherited from the Greeks, on which 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100 and larger numbers are fainter. The absolute magnitude M is how bright a star would look from 10 parsecs.

The difficulty is finding stars whose luminosity you know without already knowing their distance. Stars come in an enormous range of luminosities, from dim red dwarfs to blazing giants a million times brighter.

6. Henrietta Leavitt’s Discovery

At the Harvard College Observatory, the director Edward Pickering employed a group of women, known as “computers”, to examine and measure the thousands of photographic plates taken by the observatory’s telescopes. Henrietta Swan Leavitt, who joined in 1895 and later became head of the department for measuring the brightness of stars, specialised in variable stars. Studying plates of the Magellanic Clouds, two hazy patches visible from the southern hemisphere, she identified 1,777 variable stars, and in 1908 she noted that the brighter ones seemed to have longer periods. [4]

In 1912 she published the periods of 25 of these stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. Plotted against the logarithm of the period, their brightnesses fell on a straight line: “A straight line can readily be drawn among each of the two series of points … showing that there is a simple relation between the brightness of the variables and their periods.” [5] Because all the stars in the Cloud are at about the same distance from us, their differences in apparent brightness are real differences in luminosity. A star’s period would tell you its luminosity.

Leavitt’s stars belong to a class called Cepheid variables, named after Delta Cephei, whose variations John Goodricke discovered in 1784. All that remained was to calibrate the relation: to find the distance of at least one Cepheid by another method, which would turn the relative scale into an absolute one. None was close enough for parallax at the time, so in 1913 Ejnar Hertzsprung used a statistical method based on the motions of several Cepheids, and others refined the calibration in the following years.

7. Why Cepheids Pulsate

A Cepheid is a giant star, typically several times as massive as the Sun and thousands of times as luminous, that rhythmically swells and shrinks. Arthur Eddington argued in 1917 that Cepheids are pulsating stars, and suggested that some layer of the star must act like a valve, trapping heat when compressed and releasing it when expanded. In the 1950s Sergei Zhevakin identified the valve as a layer of partly ionised helium. Larger, more luminous Cepheids pulsate more slowly, just as a large bell rings at a lower pitch than a small one. That is the physical reason for Leavitt’s law. Polaris, the Pole Star, is a Cepheid with a period of about four days.

8. Shapley Moves the Sun

Harlow Shapley, working at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California from 1914, applied the Cepheid scale to globular clusters: dense balls of hundreds of thousands of old stars. Using variable stars in the clusters, and for the faintest clusters their apparent sizes and brightest stars, he estimated the distances to about 70 of them. Their arrangement was striking. The clusters were not spread evenly around the sky; they crowded towards the constellation Sagittarius, forming a roughly spherical system whose centre lay far from the Sun. [6]

Shapley argued that the globular clusters outline the true Galaxy, and that the Sun lies far out in its disc. He estimated the distance to the centre as about 15 kiloparsecs and the size of the Galaxy as about 300,000 light-years: ten times larger than Kapteyn’s system. His distances were too large, for reasons that would only emerge later, but his conclusion was right. Just as Copernicus had removed the Earth from the centre of the universe, Shapley removed the Sun from the centre of the Galaxy.

9. The Great Debate

On 26 April 1920, at a meeting of the National Academy of Sciences in Washington, Shapley and Heber Curtis of the Lick Observatory presented opposing views on “the scale of the universe”. Their expanded papers were published the following year. [7]

Shapley defended his huge Galaxy, about 300,000 light-years across. If it was that large, he argued, the spiral nebulae must be relatively small objects within it or near it. Curtis defended a smaller Galaxy, about 30,000 light-years across, with the Sun near the centre, and argued that the spiral nebulae are separate galaxies like ours. He pointed to the novae seen in spirals, which, if they were like novae in our Galaxy, implied great distances, and to the dark lanes seen across edge-on spirals, which resembled the dark band of dust along our own Milky Way.

Shapley had support from a set of measurements by his colleague Adriaan van Maanen, who claimed to have seen spiral nebulae rotating on photographs taken years apart. If they were rotating that fast and were far away, their outer parts would be moving faster than light. Van Maanen’s measurements were later shown to be wrong. [8]

10. Hubble Finds a Cepheid in Andromeda

The question was settled not by argument but by a telescope. The 100-inch (2.5-metre) Hooker telescope at Mount Wilson, completed in 1917, was then the largest in the world, and from 1919 Edwin Hubble used it to photograph the Andromeda nebula. On a plate taken on the night of 5–6 October 1923, he found a faint star that he first marked as a nova. Comparing it with earlier plates, he realised that it was a Cepheid variable with a period of about a month. He crossed out the “N” and wrote “VAR!”.

Using Shapley’s own calibration of the Cepheid scale, Hubble found a distance to Andromeda of about 285,000 parsecs, about 900,000 light-years: far beyond even Shapley’s giant Galaxy. He found more Cepheids in Andromeda and in two other nebulae, NGC 6822 and M33, and his results were read at a meeting of the American Astronomical Society on 1 January 1925. [9] His full paper on Andromeda appeared in 1929. [10] The spiral nebulae were galaxies, “island universes” after all, and the universe was suddenly far larger than anyone except Kant’s followers had imagined. Shapley, on reading Hubble’s letter announcing the discovery, is reported to have said: “Here is the letter that has destroyed my universe.” [1]

11. Two Corrections

Two problems remained, and fixing them changed the numbers on both sides of the debate. The first was dust. In 1930, Robert Trumpler compared the distances of star clusters found from their apparent sizes with those found from their brightness, and showed that distant clusters look systematically too faint: light is absorbed by dust between the stars, by roughly a magnitude for every few thousand light-years along the Milky Way. [11] Kapteyn’s and Herschel’s small Galaxy with the Sun near the centre was an illusion created by dust, and Shapley’s distances were too large partly because he had not allowed for it.

The second was the Cepheids themselves. In the 1940s Walter Baade, using the 100-inch telescope in skies darkened by wartime blackouts in Los Angeles, resolved the stars in the centre of Andromeda and recognised two different populations of stars: young stars in spiral arms and older stars in the central regions and globular clusters. In 1952 he announced that there are two kinds of Cepheid, one about four times (1.5 magnitudes) more luminous than the other. [12] The calibration had mixed them up. When it was corrected, the distances to the galaxies roughly doubled.

12. Our Place in a Universe of Galaxies

Today the Milky Way is known to be a barred spiral galaxy with a disc about 100,000 light-years across, containing a few hundred billion stars. The distance from the Sun to the centre has been measured directly by following stars orbiting the black hole at the centre of the Galaxy: about 8.2 kiloparsecs, or 26,700 light-years, with an uncertainty of less than one percent. [13] The Andromeda galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away, measured with Cepheids observed by the Hubble Space Telescope, [14] and is similar in size to our own or slightly larger. The two galaxies, with dozens of smaller ones, form the Local Group.

Leavitt’s law is still central to astronomy. Cepheids are now calibrated with Gaia parallaxes (Part 8), and used to calibrate brighter standard candles visible across hundreds of millions of light-years (Part 10). Each rung of this ladder depends on the one below.

14. Try It Yourself: The Inverse-Square Law

Try it yourself: a torch and graph paper

You need a small torch (a phone torch works), a sheet of graph paper and a dark room.

  1. Cover the torch with card that has a small square hole cut in it, so that the beam spreads as a square.
  2. Hold the graph paper 10 cm from the hole and count how many squares of the paper are lit.
  3. Move the paper to 20 cm, then 30 cm, and count again. The lit area should grow four times and nine times.
  4. Since the same light is spread over more squares, each square gets 1/4 and 1/9 as much. If you have a light meter app on a phone, check that the reading falls in the same way (stay away from other light sources).

15. How Did They Know? Measuring Brightness on Glass

How did they know? Photographic photometry

Leavitt worked with glass photographic plates, each showing thousands of stars as tiny black dots in the negative image. A brighter star makes a larger and darker dot. To measure brightness, she compared each star’s dot by eye with a sequence of reference stars of known magnitude, often viewed through a magnifier, and judged which it matched. [1]

To find a variable star, she laid a negative plate over a positive plate of the same field taken on a different night. Stars that had not changed cancelled out, while a variable showed up as a mismatched pair of dots. Repeating this across dozens of plates taken over years gave each star’s brightness at many moments, from which she worked out its period.

The method had limits. Photographic plates are more sensitive to blue light than the eye, and the size of a dot is not proportional to brightness, so the magnitude scale had to be built up carefully from standard stars. Leavitt herself spent years establishing a standard sequence of stars near the North Pole of the sky. Today electronic detectors count photons directly.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: the debate that settled nothing

The legend: The Great Debate of 1920 settled whether the spiral nebulae are other galaxies, and Curtis won.

The record: the meeting settled nothing. The talks were pitched at a general audience, and Shapley’s concentrated on the size of the Galaxy, saying little about the spirals; the printed papers of 1921, which gave both sides at greater length, are what people now read. [7, 8] The question of the spirals was answered by Hubble’s Cepheids in 1925, and on the size of our Galaxy and the Sun’s position, Shapley was closer to the truth than Curtis.

A second legend: Hubble’s “VAR!” plate is a myth. The record: it is real. The plate, known as H335H, survives in the archives of the Carnegie Observatories with the “N” crossed out and “VAR!” written in Hubble’s hand.

A third: Leavitt received no recognition. In 1925 the Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler wrote to her about nominating her for the Nobel Prize in Physics, unaware that she had died of cancer in 1921, at the age of 53. [1]

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: are stars the same everywhere?

Hubble’s distance to Andromeda rested on an assumption that no one could check directly: that a Cepheid in another galaxy, millions of light-years away, obeys the same law as Cepheids near us. This is a case of what philosophers call the uniformity of nature, the assumption that the same laws hold everywhere and at all times. Without it, no distance beyond parallax could be measured at all.

The assumption turned out to be almost right, but not quite. There were two kinds of Cepheid, and the stars of different populations differ in their chemical composition, which slightly affects their brightness. Astronomers still correct for such effects. How should we treat a crucial assumption that cannot be tested directly? One answer is to look for independent methods that rest on different assumptions and check whether they agree, as later astronomers did with supernovae, eclipsing binary stars and the masers in other galaxies.
