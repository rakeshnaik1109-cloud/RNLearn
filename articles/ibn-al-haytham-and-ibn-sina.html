Ibn al-Haytham and Ibn Sina: Experiment Against Authority

One proved that light enters the eye and told scholars to be the enemy of everything they read; the other wrote the medical textbook of six centuries and asked how we know a remedy works.

1. Experiment Against Authority

Around the year 1000, the most advanced science in the world was being done in Arabic. Two scholars of that world, born fifteen years apart, stand out. Ibn al-Haytham, known in Latin Europe as Alhazen, overturned a thousand years of thinking about how we see, and insisted that claims, even those of the great Ptolemy, must be tested by careful experiment. Ibn Sina, known as Avicenna, wrote the most influential medical textbook in history and set out rules for deciding whether a remedy really works. [1, 2]

Both were steeped in Aristotle and the Greek tradition (Parts 1 and 2), and both asked the question that would define modern science: when observation and authority disagree, which should we trust? Their answers were not identical. Ibn al-Haytham leaned towards controlled experiment; Ibn Sina built a vast system of reasoned philosophy in which experience had a carefully defined place.

3. The Translation Movement

After the Abbasid caliphs founded Baghdad in 762, an extraordinary effort began to translate the science and philosophy of Greece, Persia and India into Arabic, sponsored by caliphs, courtiers and wealthy families for more than two centuries. Translators such as Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq rendered Aristotle, Galen, Euclid and Ptolemy, often improving the texts by comparing manuscripts. [3] (The famous “House of Wisdom” was probably a palace library and translation bureau rather than the grand academy of popular legend.) Indian numerals and astronomy arrived by the same route (Part 3).

Translation was only the beginning. Scholars writing in Arabic, among them Persians, Arabs, Jews, Christians and Central Asians, criticised and extended what they received, producing original work in algebra, optics, astronomy, medicine and philosophy. [4]

4. Ibn al-Haytham: From Basra to Cairo

Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥasan ibn al-Haytham was born in Basra around 965 and spent much of his later life in Cairo, under the Fatimid caliphs, where he died around 1040. He wrote nearly a hundred works on mathematics, astronomy and optics. Biographers writing two centuries later tell a dramatic story: he boasted that he could regulate the floods of the Nile, was summoned by the unpredictable caliph al-Ḥākim, realised on seeing the river at Aswan that the project was impossible, and feigned madness to escape punishment, living under house arrest until the caliph’s death in 1021 (see Legend and Record). [5, 6] What is certain is that in Cairo he wrote his masterpiece, the seven-book Kitāb al-Manāẓir (Book of Optics), probably between about 1011 and 1021.

5. How Do We See?

Ancient thinkers offered two kinds of answer. In extramission theories, held by Euclid, Ptolemy and the great Arabic philosopher al-Kindī, the eye sends out rays that touch objects; this made vision a matter of geometry, with rays travelling in straight lines. In intromission theories, held by the atomists and by Aristotle, something travels from the object into the eye; this made physical sense but had no geometry. [1]

Ibn al-Haytham combined the strengths of both. He argued, from experience, that bright light can hurt the eye and leaves after-images, so something must enter it; and that it is absurd for rays from the eye to fill the whole sky instantly when we open our eyes to see the stars. Light, he said, travels in straight lines from every point of a visible object in all directions. The difficulty is that rays from every point reach every part of the eye, which should produce a meaningless blur. His solution was that only the ray from each point that strikes the eye perpendicularly enters without refraction and is registered, so each point of the object corresponds to one point in the eye. The answer was wrong in detail, but the framework, point-by-point rays entering the eye and treated with geometry, was correct, and it is the foundation of all later optics. [5, 1]

6. The Camera Obscura

Ibn al-Haytham studied what happens when light from several lamps passes through a small hole into a darkened room: each lamp makes its own separate spot of light on the opposite wall, inverted relative to the lamps, and covering the hole removes the spot of the corresponding lamp. This proved that light from different sources passes through the same opening without mixing and travels in straight lines. [5] He also discussed the crescent-shaped images of the Sun formed through small openings during an eclipse. The camera obscura (“dark room”) became a tool for astronomers watching eclipses, a drawing aid for painters, and the ancestor of the photographic camera. It is also a model of the eye, as Kepler realised six centuries later.

7. Reflection, Refraction and Illusions

The Book of Optics treats reflection from flat, spherical, cylindrical and conical mirrors, including a difficult geometrical problem, finding the point on a curved mirror where light from a given point is reflected to the eye, still known as Alhazen’s problem. He studied refraction with a graduated copper instrument, showing that the angle of refraction is not simply proportional to the angle of incidence, as Ptolemy had implied. [7] A few decades earlier, the mathematician Ibn Sahl, working on lenses and mirrors that focus sunlight, had described refraction by a constant ratio equivalent to the sine law that Europeans attribute to Snell and Descartes. [8]

Ibn al-Haytham also studied how the mind interprets what the eye receives. He catalogued visual illusions and explained, for example, why the Moon looks larger near the horizon: we unconsciously judge it to be farther away when it is seen across the intervening landscape, so we perceive it as bigger, though its image in the eye is the same size. [5]

8. Doubts About Ptolemy and the Method of Verification

In a work called al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs (Doubts Concerning Ptolemy), Ibn al-Haytham listed contradictions in the models of the most respected astronomer of antiquity, arguing that some of Ptolemy’s devices could not correspond to any physical arrangement of the heavens. He opened with a statement of method that could serve as a motto for science: [6]

“The duty of the man who investigates the writings of scientists, if learning the truth is his goal, is to make himself an enemy of all that he reads, and, applying his mind to the core and margins of its content, attack it from every side. He should also suspect himself as he performs his critical examination of it, so that he may avoid falling into either prejudice or leniency.”

In the Optics he repeatedly appeals to iʿtibār, a word that can be translated as “testing” or “verification”: setting up an arrangement, often with specially built apparatus, to check a claim by experience. Historians debate how close this comes to the modern experimental method, but his practice of designing controlled observations to decide between theories was new in its systematic character. [5, 1]

9. Ibn Sina: A Prodigy in Bukhara

Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn Sīnā was born in 980 near Bukhara, in Central Asia. In an autobiography completed by his pupil al-Jūzjānī, he says he had memorised the Qurʾan by the age of ten, studied logic, mathematics and medicine as a teenager, and read Aristotle’s Metaphysics forty times without understanding it until a commentary by al-Fārābī made it clear. At about eighteen he successfully treated the Samanid ruler, which gave him access to the royal library. [9] He spent the rest of his life moving between courts in Persia, working as a physician, adviser and sometimes vizier, writing at night, and occasionally in prison. He died at Hamadan in 1037.

His philosophical encyclopedia, the Kitāb al-Shifāʾ (Book of Healing), reworked Aristotle’s logic, physics and metaphysics into an original system that shaped both Islamic and European philosophy. In physics, he developed the idea of an impressed force (mayl) that keeps a thrown stone moving after it leaves the hand, an important step away from Aristotle towards later ideas of momentum (Part 5). [10]

10. The Canon of Medicine

Ibn Sina’s al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb (The Canon of Medicine), completed around 1025, organised the medicine of Galen and Hippocrates, together with Persian and Indian knowledge and his own observations, into five books: general principles, simple drugs, diseases of each part of the body, diseases affecting the whole body, and compound remedies. Translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona in the twelfth century, it was a standard textbook in European universities, at places such as Montpellier and Louvain, until the seventeenth century. [10, 2]

The second book contains a remarkable passage on how to establish the power of a drug from experience. Ibn Sina lists conditions: the drug must be free of contamination; it must be tested on a simple disease, not one complicated by others; it should be tried on contrasting cases; its strength must be matched to that of the disease; the time of its action must be observed; its effect must be seen consistently in many cases, not just once; and it must be tested on the human body, because effects on animals may differ. [11] These rules address the central problem of medical evidence: many patients recover anyway, and many things happen at once.

11. The Floating Man

Ibn Sina is also remembered for one of the most famous thought experiments in philosophy. Imagine a person created fully formed, floating in the air, eyes covered, limbs spread so that they touch nothing, unable to perceive anything at all. Would that person know that they exist? Ibn Sina argued that they would: they would be aware of their own self, even with no awareness of their body or of anything else. He concluded that the self, or soul, is something distinct from the body. [12] Six centuries later, Descartes’ “I think, therefore I am” would make a strikingly similar argument. The thought experiment shows Ibn Sina’s confidence in reason: some truths, he believed, can be known without the senses at all.

12. A Debate by Letter: al-Bīrūnī and Ibn Sina

Around 997, the young polymath Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī sent Ibn Sina a set of challenging questions about Aristotle’s physics. Why must the heavens move in circles? Could there be a vacuum? Could other worlds exist? Why does ice float if water contracts when it cools? Ibn Sina defended Aristotle; al-Bīrūnī replied sharply, pressing observations against arguments. [13] Al-Bīrūnī went on to measure the Earth’s radius by observing the dip of the horizon from a mountain, to compile careful tables of the densities of metals and gems, and to write a monumental study of India. The exchange shows a lively culture of disagreement in which observation was increasingly used to test inherited doctrine.

13. The Legacy

The Book of Optics was translated into Latin around 1200 as De aspectibus. It inspired the thirteenth-century “perspectivists” Roger Bacon, John Pecham and Witelo, whose textbooks spread Ibn al-Haytham’s ideas across European universities. [7, 1] In 1604 Johannes Kepler, building on this tradition, finally solved the problem Ibn al-Haytham had wrestled with: the eye’s lens focuses the cone of rays from each point of an object onto a point of the retina, forming an inverted image, just like a camera obscura with a lens. [14] Ibn Sina’s Canon shaped medical teaching for six centuries, and his philosophy influenced thinkers from Thomas Aquinas to Descartes.

Calling Ibn al-Haytham “the first scientist”, as is sometimes done, overstates the case: experiment has many ancestors, from Archimedes (Part 2) to Ptolemy. But his insistence that even the greatest authority must be tested, and his use of purpose-built experiments to decide between theories, mark a turning point on the road to Galileo (Part 5).

14. Try It Yourself: Light and Evidence

Try it yourself: test like Ibn al-Haytham, judge like Ibn Sina
  1. Camera obscura: darken a room on a sunny day, cover the window except for a hole a few millimetres wide, and hold a white card a metre or more away. You will see an inverted image of the scene outside. Change the hole size and watch the brightness and sharpness change.
  2. Lamps through a hole: place two candles or torches in front of a card with a hole and see two separate spots on the wall behind. Cover one candle: which spot disappears, and why does that show light travels in straight lines?
  3. Refraction: put a coin in an empty mug and move back until it just disappears behind the rim. Have someone pour in water without moving the coin. Why does it reappear?
  4. Evidence: think of a remedy for colds that people swear by. How would you design a fair test, following Ibn Sina’s rules and adding a comparison group?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: the scientist who feigned madness

The legend: Ibn al-Haytham promised the caliph al-Ḥākim that he could control the Nile’s floods, failed, and pretended to be mad for ten years to save his life, writing the Book of Optics while confined to his house.

The record: the story is told by biographers of the thirteenth century, such as Ibn al-Qifṭī, writing some two hundred years after the events; Ibn al-Haytham’s own surviving writings do not mention it, and some historians have even wondered whether the stories conflate two different men named Ibn al-Haytham. [6] Al-Ḥākim was indeed notoriously unpredictable, and Ibn al-Haytham did work in Cairo during his reign.

The grain of truth: the Book of Optics was written in Cairo during roughly the period of the story, and its scale suggests years of concentrated work. Whatever the circumstances, a long, quiet period of thinking and experimenting produced one of the most important books in the history of science.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: can we trust our senses?

Ibn al-Haytham trusted experience, but not naively: his catalogue of illusions shows how the mind can misjudge what the eye receives. His answer was to trust the senses under controlled conditions, with instruments and repeated tests that expose error. Ibn Sina’s floating man points the other way: there may be knowledge, of our own existence for example, that needs no senses at all. Their contrast replays the debate between Aristotle and Plato (Part 1) in a new key.

Modern science sides largely with Ibn al-Haytham, but philosophers have shown that observation is never entirely innocent: what we notice, and how we interpret it, depends on theories we already hold. A Ptolemaic and a Copernican astronomer watching the same sunrise might describe different events (Parts 5 and 11).

Questions to think about: If the senses can be fooled, how can experiments built on them give reliable knowledge? Does the floating man prove anything about the world, or only about how we think?
