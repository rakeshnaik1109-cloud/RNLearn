Making Chips: Lithography, Moore’s Law and the End of Dennard Scaling

A chip is printed with light, layer by layer, in features smaller than the wavelength used to draw them. For fifty years each new generation was cheaper, faster and cooler, until the physics of voltage stopped cooperating.

At a glance

In this part: how chips are made, and why they kept getting better. Ultra-pure silicon crystals; the integrated circuit and the planar process; photolithography and its resolution limit, from mercury lamps to extreme ultraviolet; doping, deposition, etching and copper wiring; defects and yield; Moore’s law; and Dennard scaling, the recipe that made transistors faster and cooler for thirty years, and why it ended.

You need: Parts 8 and 9 (MOSFETs and CMOS) and some basic optics (diffraction) is helpful.

1. From Sand to Chip

A modern processor contains tens of billions of transistors, each with features a few tens of atoms across, all of which must work. Making it takes about three months, over a thousand process steps, machines costing hundreds of millions of dollars, and factories cleaner than operating theatres. Yet the price per transistor has fallen by a factor of roughly a billion since the 1960s. This part follows a chip from raw material to finished die and explains the scaling rules that drove that astonishing improvement. [1]

3. Purifying Silicon and Growing Crystals

Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth’s crust, mostly as silica (SiO₂) in sand and quartz. Reduced with carbon in an arc furnace, it becomes metallurgical-grade silicon, about 98–99% pure. For electronics it is converted into a volatile chlorosilane gas, distilled, and deposited back as polycrystalline silicon of extraordinary purity: impurities at the level of parts per billion or below.

Devices need a single crystal, with every atom in its place. In 1916 Jan Czochralski discovered that a crystal can be pulled slowly from a melt, and in 1950 Gordon Teal and John Little at Bell Labs applied his method to grow large single crystals of germanium, greatly improving transistors. [2, 3] Today’s silicon ingots are 300 mm in diameter and up to two metres long; they are sliced into wafers less than a millimetre thick and polished flat to within nanometres.

4. The Integrated Circuit and the Planar Process

Early transistors were made one at a time and wired together by hand: the “tyranny of numbers” limited how complex electronics could become. In 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments built a circuit whose transistor, resistors and capacitor were all formed in one piece of germanium, connected by fine gold wires: the first integrated circuit. [4] At Fairchild Semiconductor in 1959, Jean Hoerni invented the planar process, which forms transistors by diffusing dopants through windows in a protective oxide, leaving a flat, sealed surface. [5] Robert Noyce realised that metal wiring could then be laid on top of the oxide to connect the devices, and patented the monolithic integrated circuit. [6] Kilby shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics; Noyce had died in 1990.

The planar process is the template for everything since: build devices in the silicon surface, then stack layers of insulators and metal wiring above them. A modern chip has more than a dozen wiring layers.

5. Photolithography and the Resolution Limit

Each layer of a chip is patterned by photolithography. The wafer is coated with a light-sensitive polymer (photoresist); light shines through a mask carrying the layer’s pattern, and a projection lens images it, typically reduced four times, onto the resist. Developing the resist leaves a stencil through which the wafer is etched or doped. A chip needs dozens of such masks, each aligned to the previous layers within a few nanometres.

The smallest pattern a lens can print is set by diffraction. A grating of lines diffracts light into orders at angles sin θ = mλ/p; to form any image, the lens must capture at least two orders, and the result is the resolution formula

half-pitch = k₁ λ / NA

where NA = n sin θmax is the lens’s numerical aperture and k₁ is a process factor, which cannot go below 0.25 for a single exposure. [7] To keep shrinking, the industry moved from mercury-lamp light at 436 and 365 nm to excimer lasers at 248 and 193 nm. In the mid-2000s it added a film of water between lens and wafer, raising NA to 1.35 (immersion lithography). [8] Then it split dense patterns across several exposures, and finally, after decades of development, adopted extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light at 13.5 nm, produced by hitting droplets of molten tin with a powerful laser and focused with mirrors in vacuum, because no lens is transparent at that wavelength. [9]

6. Doping, Deposition, Etching, Wiring and Yield

Between lithography steps, a chip passes through many other processes. Ion implantation fires dopant ions into the silicon at a controlled dose and depth, followed by rapid annealing to repair the damage. Thin films are grown or deposited: thermal oxide, layers from chemical vapour deposition, and films built up one atomic layer at a time (atomic layer deposition). Plasma etching removes material anisotropically, straight down, through the resist stencil. Chemical–mechanical polishing planarises each layer. Wiring has been made of copper since the late 1990s, laid into trenches etched in insulators (the damascene process), because copper conducts better than aluminium. [1]

Every step adds a risk: a dust particle or a flaw in the wrong place kills a chip. The fraction of working chips, the yield, falls roughly as e−AD with chip area A and defect density D, a relation used since the early days of integrated circuits. [10] Yield sets cost, so fabs obsess over contamination, and designers add redundant memory rows and spare cores.

7. Moore’s Law

In 1965, when the most complex chips held about 64 components, Gordon Moore of Fairchild plotted the number of components per chip that gave the lowest cost per component and saw it doubling every year. He predicted the trend would continue for at least ten years, to 65,000 components by 1975. [11] In 1975 he revised the rate to a doubling about every two years. [12] “Moore’s law” is not a law of nature but an economic observation that became a self-fulfilling plan: the industry organised its research and investment around keeping it true.

8. Dennard Scaling and Its End

More transistors would be useless if each one were slower or hotter. In 1974 Robert Dennard and his IBM colleagues showed that it need not be so. Shrink every dimension of a MOSFET by a factor κ and reduce the voltage and doping in proportion, and the transistor becomes κ times faster, its capacitance κ times smaller and its power κ² times lower, exactly balancing the κ² more transistors per unit area. [13]

QuantityDennard scaling (factor κ)After about 2005 (voltage stuck)
Dimensions, oxide thickness1/κ1/κ
Supply voltage1/κ≈ 1
Capacitance per transistor1/κ1/κ
Gate delay1/κimproves slowly
Power per transistor (CV²f)1/κ²≈ 1/κ at the same frequency
Power densityconstantrises

For thirty years this recipe delivered free performance: each generation, clock speeds rose and power stayed manageable. It broke down in the mid-2000s. Scaling the voltage requires scaling the threshold voltage, but the 60 mV-per-decade subthreshold limit (Part 8) means every reduction in threshold multiplies leakage. Oxides only a few atoms thick began to leak by tunnelling. Supply voltages stalled near 1 V, clock speeds stopped rising at 3–5 GHz, and the industry turned to multiple cores. [14] With power now the limit, a growing fraction of each chip cannot be switched on at full speed at once, a problem dubbed dark silicon, which drives the move to specialised accelerators (Parts 13 and 14). [15]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): resolution of a lithography tool

Compare the smallest half-pitch printable in one exposure by a 193 nm immersion scanner (NA = 1.35, k₁ = 0.28) and an EUV scanner (λ = 13.5 nm, NA = 0.33, k₁ = 0.4).

Solution

Immersion: 0.28 × 193 / 1.35 ≈ 40 nm. EUV: 0.4 × 13.5 / 0.33 ≈ 16 nm. EUV prints in one exposure what immersion tools need two to four exposures to achieve, saving steps and alignment errors despite its cost.

Problem 2 (easy): chips per wafer

Estimate how many 100 mm² chips fit on a 300 mm wafer, using dies per wafer ≈ πr²/A − πd/√(2A), where the second term accounts for partial dies at the edge.

Solution

πr²/A = π × 150² / 100 ≈ 707. Edge loss: π × 300 / √200 ≈ 67. So about 640 chips per wafer.

Problem 3 (medium): big chips are expensive

With a killer-defect density of 0.1 per cm², compare the yield and number of good chips for 100 mm² chips and 600 mm² chips on a 300 mm wafer.

Solution

Small: Y = e−1 × 0.1 ≈ 90%, so about 640 × 0.90 ≈ 580 good chips. Large: dies ≈ 118 − 27 ≈ 91; Y = e−6 × 0.1 ≈ 55%, so about 50 good chips. The large chip has 6 times the area but costs about 11–12 times as much wafer per good chip. Splitting it into six small chiplets would recover most of that loss, at the cost of connecting them.

Problem 4 (medium): checking Moore’s law

Starting from the Intel 4004’s 2,300 transistors in 1971, how many transistors does a doubling every two years predict for 2021? Compare with real flagship chips of 2020–2022 (16–80 billion).

Solution

Fifty years is 25 doublings: 2,300 × 225 = 2,300 × 3.4 × 107 ≈ 7.7 × 1010, about 77 billion: remarkably close to the largest chips of that time. A one-year doubling would have predicted 2,300 × 250 ≈ 2.6 × 1018, wildly too many.

Problem 5 (harder): the end of Dennard scaling

Over four generations, each shrinking dimensions by κ = 1.4 (doubling density), compare the power density of a chip under ideal Dennard scaling with one in which the supply voltage stays fixed but frequency still rises by κ each generation.

Solution

Per transistor, P = CV²f. Dennard: C → C/κ, V → V/κ, f → κf, so P → P/κ²; density × κ², so power density is unchanged. Fixed voltage: P → (C/κ)V²(κf) = P, unchanged per transistor, while density doubles, so power density doubles each generation: 2⁴ = 16 times after four generations. A 100 W chip would become 1.6 kW in the same area. That is why clock frequencies stopped rising and why parts of chips must be left idle.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: how long can scaling continue?

Marketing names such as “3 nm” no longer correspond to any physical dimension (Part 11), and the cost per transistor, which used to fall steadily, has fallen much more slowly at the newest nodes. High-NA EUV scanners (NA = 0.55) promise finer patterns but are extremely expensive and print a smaller field. The industry’s roadmaps now rely on new transistor shapes, stacking transistors on top of each other, delivering power from the back of the wafer, and packaging many chiplets together. Whether these will keep the economic heart of Moore’s law, cheaper transistors every generation, beating is a debated question.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “Moore’s law says computers double in speed every 18 months”

The myth: Moore predicted that computer speed doubles every 18 months.

The reality: Moore wrote about the number of components per chip, doubling first every year and later every two years. The popular 18-month figure for performance came from others combining more transistors with faster clocks during the Dennard era. Since the mid-2000s, single-thread speed has grown far more slowly, while transistor counts have kept rising.

Why it matters: more transistors no longer automatically mean faster programs; how they are used (cores, caches, accelerators) now decides performance.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Chips start as ultra-pure single-crystal silicon wafers grown by the Czochralski method.
  • Kilby’s integrated circuit (1958), Hoerni’s planar process and Noyce’s monolithic IC (1959) made it possible to build whole circuits at once.
  • Photolithography’s resolution is half-pitch = k₁λ/NA; the industry moved to 193 nm immersion, multiple patterning and 13.5 nm EUV.
  • Yield falls roughly as e−AD, making large chips expensive.
  • Moore’s law: transistor counts doubled about every two years for five decades.
  • Dennard scaling kept power density constant until voltages stalled around 2005; since then power limits performance.
