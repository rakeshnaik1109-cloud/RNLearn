Correlation and Regression: Lines, Curves and Causes

How do two quantities move together, and how can we predict one from the other? Here is correlation and regression, from Legendre’s comets to dose–response curves, with the checks that keep a fitted line honest.

1. Do Two Things Go Together?

Does a spring stretch in proportion to the load? Does reaction rate rise with temperature? Does a sensor’s signal follow the concentration? Do taller parents have taller children? Many scientific questions are about the relationship between two quantities rather than a difference between groups.

Two tools answer them. Correlation measures how strongly two variables move together. Regression goes further: it fits an equation that predicts one variable from another, with uncertainties on its parameters. This part covers both, from scatter plots to least squares, from checking a fit to fitting curves with χ², and from straight lines to the logistic curves used for yes-or-no outcomes and in machine learning. It ends where every discussion of correlation must: with causation. [1, 2]

3. Scatter Plots and Covariance

The first step is always a scatter plot: one variable on each axis, one point per observation. It shows at a glance whether there is a relationship, whether it is straight or curved, whether the scatter is even, and whether any points stand apart.

A number that captures how two variables move together is the covariance:

$$\operatorname{cov}(x,y) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}).$$

When points above the mean in x also tend to be above the mean in y, the products are mostly positive and the covariance is positive; when high x goes with low y, it is negative. But its size depends on the units: measure length in millimetres instead of metres and the covariance grows a thousandfold. Hence the need for a standardised measure.

4. The Correlation Coefficient

Dividing the covariance by both standard deviations gives Pearson’s correlation coefficient:

$$r = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(x,y)}{s_x s_y}.$$

It has no units and always lies between −1 and +1. The idea of “co-relation” came from Francis Galton in 1888, while studying the link between forearm length and height; Karl Pearson gave the formula used today in 1896. [3, 4] Its square, r², is the share of the variation in y that a straight line in x accounts for.

Like any statistic, r is uncertain. Its sampling distribution is skewed near ±1, so confidence intervals use Fisher’s z transformation, z = ½ ln((1 + r)/(1 − r)), which is approximately normal with standard error 1/√(n − 3). [5] With 20 points and r = 0.6, the 95% interval is 0.21 to 0.82 (p = 0.005); with r = 0.3, it is −0.16 to 0.66 (p = 0.20). Small samples pin down correlations very loosely.

5. When Correlation Misleads

The correlation coefficient measures only linear association, and only if no single point dominates. In 1973 Francis Anscombe made the point unforgettably with four small data sets that have the same means, variances, correlation (0.816) and regression line, but look nothing alike. [6]

Common traps:

  • Curved relationships. A perfect U-shape can have r = 0. Zero correlation does not mean no relationship.
  • Outliers and influential points. One extreme point can create a strong correlation from nothing or destroy a real one.
  • Restricted range. Sampling only a narrow range of x (only the best students, only room temperature) weakens the observed correlation.
  • Mixing groups. Combining groups with different means can create or reverse a correlation (Section 16).

When the relation is monotonic but not straight, or outliers are a worry, rank correlations help. Spearman’s ρ (1904) is Pearson’s r computed on the ranks; Kendall’s τ (1938) counts how many pairs of points are in the same order on both variables. [7, 8] For Anscombe’s data set C, the outlier pulls Pearson’s r down to 0.82, while Spearman’s ρ is 0.99.

6. Fitting a Line: Least Squares

Regression fits a line y = a + bx to predict y from x. Which line is best? The standard answer is the one that minimises the sum of the squared residuals, the vertical distances between the points and the line. This is the method of least squares, published by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1805 for calculating comet orbits. Carl Friedrich Gauss published it in 1809, connected it to the normal distribution of errors, and claimed to have used it since 1795, which started one of the famous priority disputes in science. [9, 10, 11] The solution is

$$b = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}.$$

Least squares treats x as known exactly and puts all the error in y. Regress x on y instead and you get a different line. When both variables have comparable errors, methods such as Deming regression or weighted orthogonal fitting are more appropriate; when x is set by the experimenter (loads, concentrations, times), ordinary least squares is the right choice.

7. How Good Is the Line?

A fitted line comes with uncertainties. The residual standard deviation s = √(Σ residuals² / (n − 2)) measures the scatter around the line (n − 2, because two parameters were estimated). From it:

  • The standard error of the slope is s/√Σ(x − x̄)². It shrinks with more points and with a wider spread of x. Spreading the x values widely is the cheapest way to pin down a slope.
  • A confidence interval for the slope is b ± t* × SE with n − 2 degrees of freedom. If it excludes zero, the relationship is statistically significant.
  • The confidence band shows where the true line probably lies; the prediction band shows where a new single measurement would probably fall. Both are narrowest at the mean of x and widen towards the ends, a warning against extrapolation.
  • R² is the share of the variation in y explained by the line. A high R² does not prove the model is right (Anscombe’s data set B has R² = 0.67 for a curve), and a low R² does not make a significant slope unimportant.

A subtle consequence of imperfect correlation is regression to the mean. Galton noticed in 1886 that children of very tall parents were, on average, tall but less extreme than their parents. [12] Whenever two measurements are imperfectly correlated, extreme values on one tend to be less extreme on the other. Samples selected for a high first reading will, on average, read lower next time even if nothing has changed, a trap for anyone who tests a treatment only on the worst cases.

8. Checking the Fit

The assumptions of ordinary least squares are like those of ANOVA: the relationship is linear, the errors are independent, have constant variance, and are roughly normal. The best check is a residual plot: residuals against fitted values (or against x). A good fit leaves a shapeless band around zero. Patterns reveal problems:

  • A curve means the model is missing a term: the relation is not straight.
  • A funnel means the scatter grows with the response (heteroscedasticity): use weights, transform the data (often a logarithm), or use robust standard errors.
  • A trend over time in residuals plotted in measurement order means the errors are not independent, perhaps because of drift.

Some points matter more than others. A point far from the others in x has high leverage: it can pull the line towards itself. If its y value is also unusual, it is influential. Cook’s distance (1977) measures how much the whole fit would change if a point were removed; values above about 1 deserve a close look. [13] Never delete a point just because it is influential; find out why.

9. Calibration and Inverse Prediction

Part 3 introduced calibration curves: fit the instrument’s signal against known standards, then read the line backwards to find the value of an unknown. This inverse prediction inherits the uncertainty of the fitted line. For an unknown with mean signal ȳ0 from m replicate readings, the standard uncertainty of the estimated value x̂0 = (ȳ0 − a)/b is approximately [1]

$$u(\hat{x}_0) \approx \frac{s}{|b|}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(\bar{y}_0-\bar{y})^2}{b^2\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2}}.$$

The three terms show how to make calibration more precise: more replicate readings of the unknown, more calibration standards, and an unknown near the middle of the calibration range.

10. Multiple Regression and Confounding

Most responses depend on more than one thing. Multiple regression fits y = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + …, still by least squares. Each coefficient bj is the expected change in y for a unit change in xj with the other variables held fixed. This is how regression adjusts for confounding: a third variable related to both x and y that can create, hide or reverse an apparent relationship. ANOVA and ANCOVA (Parts 5 and 6) are special cases of the same model.

Two cautions. When predictors are strongly correlated with each other (collinearity), their separate effects cannot be told apart and their coefficients become very uncertain. And adjusting only works for confounders you have measured; only randomisation (Part 9) protects against the ones you have not.

11. Fitting Curves: Weighted Least Squares and χ²

Many physical laws are not straight lines: exponential decay, Gaussian peaks, power laws, Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Nonlinear least squares fits such models directly by adjusting their parameters to minimise the squared residuals, usually with an iterative algorithm. It is better to fit the curve directly than to linearise it (for example by taking logarithms or reciprocals), because transformations distort the errors: a double-reciprocal plot gives enormous weight to the least precise points at low concentration.

When measurements have different uncertainties σi, each residual should be weighted by 1/σi², as in the weighted mean of Part 3. The quantity minimised is then [2]

$$\chi^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{y_i - f(x_i)}{\sigma_i}\right)^2.$$

If the model is right and the uncertainties are correct, χ² should be about equal to the number of degrees of freedom ν = n − (number of parameters), so the reduced chi-square χ²/ν should be close to 1. Much larger means the model does not fit or the uncertainties are underestimated; much smaller means the uncertainties are overestimated or the model is overfitting, chasing the noise.

For these data, a straight line gives χ²/ν = 1.58 (p = 0.10), a quadratic 1.13 and a cubic 0.86. More parameters always reduce χ², so adding terms must be justified by the physics or by a fair comparison such as an information criterion or cross-validation, not by χ² alone.

12. Yes or No: Logistic Regression

Many outcomes are binary: a seed germinates or not, a cell responds or not, a component fails or survives. The quantity to model is the probability p of the outcome, which must stay between 0 and 1. A straight line cannot do that. Logistic regression models the log-odds as a straight line:

$$\ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x, \qquad p = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1 x)}}.$$

The S-shaped logistic curve dates back to Pierre-François Verhulst’s 1838 model of population growth; Joseph Berkson championed its use for dose–response experiments in 1944 and coined the word “logit”, and David Cox developed the regression framework in 1958. [14, 15, 16] The coefficient β1 has a neat interpretation: eβ1 is the odds ratio, the factor by which the odds of the outcome are multiplied for each unit increase in x. Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood rather than least squares.

For these data the odds ratio is 3.0 per unit of dose (95% CI 2.1 to 4.3), and the dose giving a 50% response is 3.6. Logistic regression is also one of the most widely used classifiers in machine learning: it predicts a probability, and a threshold turns that into a yes or no. Our article on classification and regression metrics explains how to judge such predictions.

13. Worked Example: Hooke’s Law

A spring is loaded with masses from 0 to 300 g in steps of 50 g, and its extension is measured with a ruler: 2.0, 4.2, 5.7, 7.2, 9.5, 11.0 and 13.8 mm.

  1. Plot. The points lie close to a straight line; the residual plot shows no pattern.
  2. Fit. Slope b = 0.03771 mm/g with standard error 0.00144; intercept 1.97 ± 0.26 mm (the pointer did not start at zero, a systematic offset that the intercept absorbs). Residual SD 0.38 mm, R² = 0.993.
  3. Slope interval. With 5 degrees of freedom, t* = 2.571, so the 95% CI for the slope is 0.0340 to 0.0414 mm/g.
  4. Physics. Hooke’s law, F = kx, with F = mg: k = g/b = 9.81 N/kg ÷ 0.03771 mm/g = 260 ± 10 N/m (the relative uncertainty of k equals that of the slope, 3.8%).
  5. Prediction. At 175 g, the predicted extension is 8.57 mm. The 95% confidence interval for the mean extension is 8.19 to 8.95 mm; the 95% prediction interval for a single new measurement is 7.52 to 9.62 mm.
Try it in Python: correlation, linear and logistic regression 
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
from scipy import stats

m = np.array([0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300])
x = np.array([2.0, 4.2, 5.7, 7.2, 9.5, 11.0, 13.8])
print(stats.pearsonr(m, x))                  # r = 0.996

fit = sm.OLS(x, sm.add_constant(m)).fit()
print(fit.params, fit.bse, fit.conf_int())   # slope 0.03771 +/- 0.00144
print(fit.get_prediction([[1, 175]]).summary_frame())   # bands at 175 g
print("k =", 9.81 / fit.params[1], "N/m")    # about 260 N/m

dose = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]); resp = np.array([1, 3, 7, 12, 16, 19])
glm = sm.GLM(np.c_[resp, 20 - resp], sm.add_constant(dose),
             family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit()
print(np.exp(glm.params[1]))                 # odds ratio about 3.0

14. Try It Yourself: Measure g from a Slope

Try it yourself: a pendulum and a straight line
  1. Make a pendulum from a string and a small heavy weight. Measure its period T (time 20 swings and divide) for five or six lengths L between about 0.2 and 1.2 m.
  2. The period of a simple pendulum is T = 2π√(L/g), so T² = (4π²/g) L: a straight line through the origin with slope 4π²/g.
  3. Plot T² against L and fit a straight line. Is the intercept consistent with zero? (If not, perhaps you measured L to the wrong point on the bob.)
  4. Calculate g = 4π²/slope and its uncertainty from the standard error of the slope. Compare with the single-length measurement of Part 3: which method gives the smaller uncertainty, and why does the intercept help protect against a systematic error in L?

Plotting data so that the expected law becomes a straight line is a classic laboratory technique. It lets you check the form of the law by eye, from the residuals, as well as estimate its constant.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: why is it called “regression”?

The legend: “regression” is just an old word for fitting a line to data.

The record: the word described a biological finding before it described a method. In 1886 Francis Galton compared the heights of 928 adult children with those of their parents and found that the children of unusually tall or short parents were, on average, closer to the population mean. He called this “regression towards mediocrity”. [12] The line he drew through the data to describe it became the “regression line”, and the name stuck to the whole method, even for relationships that have nothing to do with heredity or a return to the mean.

The grain of truth: Galton’s “regression” was not a biological force pulling families towards the average. It is a statistical consequence of imperfect correlation, and it appears in any repeated measurement with random error, which is why regression to the mean is still a frequent source of mistaken conclusions.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: when does correlation mean causation?

Across countries, chocolate consumption per person is strongly correlated with the number of Nobel laureates per head of population (r = 0.79), as a tongue-in-cheek paper pointed out in 2012. [17] Few would conclude that chocolate makes Nobel laureates. Wealth, education and research spending are obvious common causes. Correlation can arise from causation in either direction, from a common cause, from selection, or from chance.

Grouping can even reverse an association. In 1973 the University of California, Berkeley, admitted about 44% of male applicants to graduate school but only 35% of female applicants. Department by department, however, women were admitted at similar or higher rates; women had applied more often to departments that admitted few applicants of either sex. [18] This reversal is known as Simpson’s paradox. [19]

Experiments, by randomising the cause, break these alternative explanations. When experiments are impossible, as for smoking and lung cancer, scientists weigh the evidence. In 1965 Austin Bradford Hill listed considerations including the strength and consistency of the association, the correct order in time, a dose–response gradient, and plausibility. [20] They are not a checklist, but they are a guide to reasoning carefully from correlation towards cause.

Questions to think about: Can a correlation ever prove causation on its own? If not, how did science establish that smoking causes lung cancer without randomised experiments on people?
