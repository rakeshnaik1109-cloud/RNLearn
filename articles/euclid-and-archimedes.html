Euclid and Archimedes: Can Reasoning from Axioms Describe Nature?

One built all of geometry from five assumptions; the other used the same method to discover why levers balance and ships float. Together they showed how far pure reasoning can reach into the physical world.

1. Can Reasoning Describe Nature?

Plato believed that true knowledge comes from reason, and that mathematics is its purest form (Part 1). In the century after his death, two Greek mathematicians put that belief to its most impressive test. Euclid, working in Alexandria, showed how an entire science could be built by pure deduction from a handful of starting assumptions. Archimedes, in Syracuse, used the same method to discover laws of the physical world: why levers balance, why ships float, how large a circle is.

Their work raises a question that runs through this series. If a few self-evident axioms and careful logic can tell us how levers and water behave, is the world fundamentally mathematical? Or do the axioms themselves come from experience, and could they be wrong? It would take two thousand years, and the discovery of geometries in which Euclid’s axioms fail, to see how deep the question goes. [1, 2]

3. Alexandria and the Museum

After Alexander the Great died in 323 BCE, his general Ptolemy took Egypt and made Alexandria, the city Alexander had founded on the coast, his capital. The Ptolemies built the Museum (a “shrine of the Muses”, more like a research institute than a museum) and its famous Library, and paid scholars to live and work there. For about two centuries Alexandria was the centre of Greek science: Euclid wrote there, Eratosthenes measured the Earth there, and Herophilus dissected human bodies there. [3, 2] The Library’s eventual decline was gradual rather than a single dramatic fire, but the losses were enormous; much of what we know of ancient mathematics survives only in later copies and commentaries.

4. Euclid’s Elements: Definitions, Postulates and Proof

Almost nothing is known about Euclid’s life. He probably worked in Alexandria around 300 BCE. A story told by Proclus seven centuries later has King Ptolemy asking for a shorter way to learn geometry, and Euclid replying that “there is no royal road to geometry”; the same anecdote is told of other mathematicians, so it may not be true. [4]

His Elements, in thirteen books, collected and organised the mathematics of his predecessors, including the work of Plato’s circle, into a single deductive system. It begins with:

  • Definitions, such as “a point is that which has no part” and “a line is breadthless length”.
  • Five postulates, assumptions about what can be done: to draw a straight line between any two points; to extend a line; to draw a circle with any centre and radius; that all right angles are equal; and the famous fifth, the parallel postulate.
  • Common notions (axioms) true of all quantities, such as “things equal to the same thing are equal to each other” and “the whole is greater than the part”.

From these, Euclid proved 465 propositions, each step justified by what came before: from constructing an equilateral triangle (Book I, Proposition 1) and the theorem of Pythagoras (I.47) to the construction of the five Platonic solids in Book XIII. [5] The Elements became the most widely studied mathematics book in history and, for two millennia, the model of what certain knowledge looks like. Its method, start from clear assumptions and deduce everything else, shaped not only mathematics but physics (Newton’s Principia is laid out like the Elements), philosophy (Spinoza wrote his Ethics “in geometrical order”) and even politics.

Euclid was not flawless. His very first proof assumes that two circles drawn in a certain way must meet, but none of his postulates guarantees it. Such gaps were only fully repaired in 1899, when David Hilbert gave geometry a complete set of axioms. [6]

5. The Parallel Postulate

The fifth postulate says, in Euclid’s wording, that if a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on one side less than two right angles, the two lines, if extended, meet on that side. It is equivalent to the more familiar statement that through a point not on a line there is exactly one parallel, and to the statement that the angles of every triangle add up to two right angles (180°).

It is longer and less obvious than the others, and Euclid himself avoided using it for his first 28 propositions. For two thousand years mathematicians, from Ptolemy to the Persian poet-mathematician Omar Khayyam and the Jesuit Giovanni Saccheri, tried to prove it from the other four. All failed. In the 1820s and 1830s Nikolai Lobachevsky and János Bolyai, and privately Carl Friedrich Gauss, realised why: one can replace the fifth postulate with its opposite and obtain a perfectly consistent geometry, in which triangles have angle sums of less than 180°. On a sphere, the angles add to more. [7] The question “which geometry is true?” turned out to be a question about the physical world, not about logic, and Einstein would answer it in an unexpected way (Part 10).

6. Primes and the Oldest Algorithm

The Elements is not only about shapes. Books VII to IX treat whole numbers, using lengths to represent them. Two results show the power of pure reasoning:

  • There are infinitely many primes (IX.20). Given any list of primes, multiply them together and add 1. The result is not divisible by any prime on the list, so it is either a new prime or divisible by a prime not on the list. No list can be complete.
  • The Euclidean algorithm (VII.1–2) finds the greatest common measure of two numbers by repeatedly subtracting the smaller from the larger. More than two thousand years later Donald Knuth called it the “granddaddy” of algorithms, the oldest nontrivial one still in everyday use; it runs inside the encryption that protects online banking. [8]

7. Archimedes of Syracuse

Archimedes was born around 287 BCE in Syracuse, a Greek city in Sicily; in one of his works he names his father, Phidias, as an astronomer. He probably spent time in Alexandria and corresponded with mathematicians there throughout his life, sending them his discoveries, sometimes with deliberately false claims mixed in to catch those who pretended to have proved them first. [9, 10]

He is often called the greatest mathematician of antiquity. He found the area under a parabola, the surface area and volume of a sphere (he was proudest of showing that a sphere has two-thirds the volume of the cylinder that encloses it), and methods of summing infinitely many ever-smaller pieces that anticipate integral calculus. But unlike most Greek mathematicians, he also applied geometry to physical problems, founding the mathematical sciences of statics and hydrostatics. [11]

8. The Law of the Lever

In On the Equilibrium of Planes, Archimedes treats the balance like a geometry problem. He begins with postulates such as “equal weights at equal distances balance” and “if unequal weights are at equal distances, the beam tilts towards the greater”, and from them proves that two weights balance when their distances from the fulcrum are inversely proportional to the weights: a weight of 3 at distance 2 balances a weight of 1 at distance 6. [11] He then uses the result to find the centres of gravity of triangles, parallelograms and parabolic segments.

This is the first known physical law stated and proved mathematically. It is also the source of his famous boast, reported by Pappus of Alexandria some five centuries later: “Give me a place to stand, and I will move the Earth.” [1]

9. Buoyancy and the Golden Crown

In On Floating Bodies, Archimedes started from a postulate about fluids (that a fluid pushes on each part with the pressure of the fluid above it) and proved what is now called Archimedes’ principle: a body immersed in a fluid is pushed upwards by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. A body denser than the fluid sinks but weighs less; a body less dense floats, sinking until it displaces its own weight. He went on to analyse the stability of floating shapes, results still relevant to the design of ships. [11]

The best-known story about him comes from the Roman architect Vitruvius, writing about two centuries after Archimedes’ death. King Hiero had given a goldsmith gold to make a votive crown and suspected that some had been replaced with silver. Archimedes, stepping into a full bath, noticed the water overflowing and realised he could compare the crown’s volume with that of equal weights of gold and silver. [12] Whether or not it happened that way, the physics is sound, as the figure shows. In 1586 a young Galileo argued that overflowing water would be far too crude a measurement and that Archimedes would surely have used a balance under water, applying his own principle. [10]

10. Measuring the Circle

The ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter, which we call π, cannot be found exactly by Euclid’s constructions. Archimedes, in Measurement of a Circle, trapped it instead. A polygon drawn inside a circle has a shorter perimeter than the circle; one drawn outside has a longer one. Starting from hexagons and doubling the number of sides four times to 96, he calculated both perimeters, using approximations to square roots that always erred on the safe side. His result: 3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7, that is, between about 3.1408 and 3.1429. [11] The method, squeezing an unknown between two bounds that close in on it (the Greek method of exhaustion), is the ancestor of the limit, the central idea of calculus.

11. The Method and the Palimpsest

Greek proofs give no hint of how their results were discovered. Archimedes is the exception. In a letter to Eratosthenes known as The Method, he explained that he first found results by imagining figures sliced into infinitely many thin strips and balancing them, strip by strip, on a lever; only afterwards did he prove them rigorously. Mechanical intuition led; geometrical proof confirmed. [13]

The Method was lost for centuries. In 1229, a scribe in the Byzantine world scraped the ink off a tenth-century copy of Archimedes’ works and wrote a prayer book over it, a palimpsest. In 1906 the Danish scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg recognised Archimedes beneath the prayers in a library in Constantinople and read what he could with a magnifying glass. The book then disappeared, reappearing at an auction in 1998, mouldy and damaged by forged illustrations painted over it in the twentieth century. Over the following decade, a team at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore imaged it with ultraviolet light, multispectral imaging and X-ray fluorescence, recovering text that Heiberg could not read, including more of The Method and a work on a combinatorial puzzle called the Stomachion. [13]

12. Mathematics Meets Nature

Euclid and Archimedes both applied geometry beyond pure mathematics. Euclid’s Optics treats vision as straight rays from the eye and derives, as theorems, how the apparent size of objects depends on distance and angle: the beginning of geometrical optics and of perspective (Part 4 returns to the question of whether rays leave or enter the eye). Archimedes’ Sand-Reckoner invented a notation for enormous numbers to show that even the grains of sand needed to fill the universe, about 1063 by his estimate, could be counted; along the way it records Aristarchus of Samos’ proposal that the Earth moves around the Sun, our main evidence for that daring idea. [11, 1]

The pattern Archimedes established, idealise the physical situation, state a few postulates, and deduce consequences mathematically, became the model for mathematical physics. When Galileo and Newton revived it in the seventeenth century (Parts 5 and 6), they consciously saw themselves as following Archimedes, whom Galileo praised again and again as his model.

13. The Death of Archimedes and the Legacy

During the Second Punic War, Syracuse sided with Carthage, and a Roman army under Marcellus besieged it from 214 BCE. According to Polybius, Livy and Plutarch, Archimedes’ machines, catapults and cranes that lifted ships out of the water, held the Romans off for months. When the city finally fell in 212 BCE, Archimedes was killed by a Roman soldier, against Marcellus’ orders. Plutarch gives several versions of the scene; in the best known, Archimedes was absorbed in a diagram and told the soldier not to disturb his circles. [14] The tales of burning the Roman ships with mirrors appear only in writers many centuries later and are probably legend.

About 137 years later, Cicero, then a Roman official in Sicily, searched for Archimedes’ tomb and found it among overgrown thorns, recognised by its carving of a sphere and cylinder, the result Archimedes had wished to be remembered for. [15] His works were translated into Arabic in the ninth century and into Latin in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, and inspired the mathematicians who created the calculus.

14. Try It Yourself: Proofs and Experiments

Try it yourself: reason like Euclid, test like Archimedes
  1. Euclid: with only a compass and a straight edge, construct an equilateral triangle, then bisect one of its angles. Write down which postulate justifies each step.
  2. Primes: multiply 2 × 3 × 5 × 7 × 11 × 13 and add 1. Is the result prime? (It is 30,031 = 59 × 509.) Why does this not contradict Euclid’s proof?
  3. Lever: balance a ruler on a pencil. Place two coins at 3 cm on one side; where must one coin go on the other side?
  4. Buoyancy: hang a stone from a kitchen scale or spring, then lower it into water. The loss in weight, in grams, is its volume in cubic centimetres; divide its weight in air by this to find its density.

Notice the difference: the first two need no equipment at all, and their conclusions are certain; the last two depend on measurement, and their results are only as good as your instruments.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: “Eureka!”

The legend: Archimedes, stepping into a bath, saw the water overflow, realised how to test King Hiero’s crown, and ran naked through the streets of Syracuse shouting “Eureka!” (“I have found it!”).

The record: the story is told by Vitruvius in the preface to Book IX of On Architecture, written around 25 BCE, nearly two centuries after Archimedes’ death; no earlier source mentions it, and Archimedes’ own surviving works do not. [12] The method Vitruvius describes, measuring overflowing water, would barely detect the difference for a crown of realistic size, as Galileo pointed out.

The grain of truth: Archimedes really did discover the principle of buoyancy, and proved it in On Floating Bodies; with a balance under water, his principle easily exposes added silver. The story captures something real about discovery: sudden insight often comes when the mind is relaxed, after long effort.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: are axioms true?

For two thousand years, Euclid’s geometry was the standard example of knowledge that is both certain and about the real world. Kant even argued that its truths are built into the way our minds must experience space (Part 7). The discovery of non-Euclidean geometries shattered this. Logic alone cannot tell us which geometry describes physical space; that is a question for measurement. And in 1915 Einstein’s general relativity showed that space near massive bodies is not Euclidean at all.

Today mathematicians treat axioms as starting assumptions to be explored, not as self-evident truths, while physicists ask which mathematical structures fit nature. Archimedes’ work shows both sides: his lever postulates were chosen because they matched experience, but once chosen, the conclusions followed with certainty. As Einstein put it in 1921, “as far as the laws of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and as far as they are certain, they do not refer to reality.” [16]

Questions to think about: Why should mathematics invented by pure thought turn out to describe the physical world so well? Are Archimedes’ lever postulates truths of reason or summaries of experience?
