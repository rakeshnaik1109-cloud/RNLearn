How Fast Is Light? Jupiter’s Moons, Spinning Wheels and a Defined Constant

Light seems to arrive instantly. Showing that it does not, and then measuring how fast it goes, took the moons of Jupiter, a spinning wheel, two mountains and a laser, and ended by redefining the metre.

1. Instant, or Just Very Fast?

Switch on a lamp and the room fills with light at once. Does light arrive instantly, or does it travel, just too fast for us to notice? For two thousand years this was a question for philosophers. The Greek thinker Empedocles believed that light must take time to travel; Aristotle disagreed and treated light as something that appears all at once. In the eleventh century Ibn al-Haytham, in his great Book of Optics, argued that light moves, very fast but not infinitely fast. As late as the 1630s, Kepler and Descartes both believed it was instantaneous. [2, 20]

In this part of the series we follow how people first showed that light takes time, then measured how much: with the moons of Jupiter, with a telescope that had to be tilted like an umbrella in the rain, with spinning wheels and mirrors, with mountains, microwaves and lasers. The story connects to every other part of the series. To turn the first observations into a speed, astronomers needed the Sun's distance from Part 4; and today the speed of light defines the metre itself.

3. Galileo's Lanterns

The first proposal for an experiment came from Galileo. In his Two New Sciences (1638) he described two people standing some distance apart, each with a covered lantern. The first uncovers his lantern; the second, as soon as he sees the light, uncovers his; the first measures how long it takes for the reply to appear. After practising at close range, they repeat it at a greater distance and see whether the delay grows. Galileo reported trying it at less than a mile, without being able to tell whether light was instantaneous, and concluded that if it was not, it was "extraordinarily rapid". [1]

As Figure 2 shows, the experiment was hopeless: light crosses a few kilometres in a few millionths of a second, while a human reaction takes about a quarter of a second, and varies by more than that from one try to the next. Galileo's real contribution was the idea that the question could be settled by experiment at all. To measure the speed of light, people needed either far greater distances or far finer timing. The first answer came from the largest distances available: those of the solar system.

4. Rømer and the Moons of Jupiter

In 1610 Galileo had discovered four moons circling Jupiter. The innermost, Io, goes round every 42½ hours and disappears into Jupiter's shadow on every orbit. These eclipses happen like clockwork, and in the 1660s and 1670s astronomers, especially Giovanni Domenico Cassini at the Paris Observatory, timed them carefully, hoping to use them as a clock in the sky for finding longitude.

But the clock misbehaved. A young Danish astronomer working at Paris, Ole Rømer, noticed that the eclipses came progressively later than predicted as the Earth moved away from Jupiter, and earlier as it approached. In 1676 he announced that an eclipse of Io due in November would appear about ten minutes later than expected, and it did. His explanation was bold: when the Earth is on the far side of its orbit from Jupiter, the light from Io has further to travel, and it takes time to do so. He estimated that light needs about 22 minutes to cross the diameter of the Earth's orbit. [3, 4]

Rømer did not turn his delay into a speed. That step was taken by the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens, who combined Rømer's 22 minutes with the size of the Earth's orbit (as measured by Cassini and Richer, Part 4) and in his Treatise on Light (1690) gave a value equivalent to about 212,000 km/s. [5, 6] The true crossing time is about 16.6 minutes, so the value was about 30 per cent too low, but it was the right order of magnitude, and it established that light, though fantastically fast, is not instantaneous. Not everyone was convinced: Cassini himself was doubtful, and the idea was not universally accepted until a second, completely different effect confirmed it.

5. Bradley and the Tilted Telescope

In the 1720s the English astronomer James Bradley was trying to detect the parallax of a star: the tiny yearly shift in its position caused by the Earth's motion around the Sun. He chose the star Gamma Draconis, which passes almost directly overhead in London, and watched it with a long telescope fixed to a chimney. The star did shift, by about 40 seconds of arc over the year, but at the wrong times: its movement was a quarter of a year out of step with what parallax would produce. [7]

The explanation, the story goes, came to him while sailing on the River Thames, when he noticed that a weather vane on the boat changed direction each time the boat turned, although the wind had not changed. The direction of the wind, as felt on the moving boat, depends on the boat's own motion. In the same way, starlight arrives at the moving Earth from a slightly shifted direction, just as you must tilt an umbrella forward when you walk through vertical rain. This effect is called the aberration of starlight.

The size of the tilt gives the ratio of the Earth's speed to the speed of light: about one to ten thousand. Bradley announced his discovery in 1729 and calculated that light takes 8 minutes 12 seconds to travel from the Sun to the Earth, only about 1 per cent off the modern value of 8 minutes 19 seconds. [7] Aberration was independent proof that light travels at a finite speed, and also the first direct evidence that the Earth really does move around the Sun. (Bradley never found the parallax he was looking for: it is hundreds of times smaller, and was not measured until 1838, as we will see in Part 8.)

6. Fizeau's Toothed Wheel

Both Rømer and Bradley needed astronomy. Could light be timed on Earth? In 1849 the French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau found a way. He sent a beam of light through a gap between the teeth of a spinning wheel with 720 teeth, to a mirror on a hill at Montmartre, 8,633 metres away, and back through the wheel to his eye at Suresnes. [8]

When the wheel turned slowly, the light came back through the same gap and Fizeau saw it. But as the wheel sped up, it turned a little during the light's round trip, which took about 58 millionths of a second. At a certain speed, light that left through a gap returned to find a tooth in the way, and the image disappeared. Speed the wheel up further, and the light came back through the next gap and reappeared. Knowing the distance, the number of teeth and the speed of the wheel, Fizeau could calculate the speed of light.

Fizeau saw the first darkness at about 12.6 turns per second and obtained about 315,000 km/s, about 5 per cent too high. It was a remarkable achievement: the first measurement of the speed of light that did not depend on astronomy or on the size of the Earth's orbit, using a light beam that travelled just 17 km.

7. Foucault's Spinning Mirror

Fizeau's friend and rival Léon Foucault, famous for his pendulum, used a spinning mirror instead of a wheel. Light reflected from the rapidly rotating mirror travels to a distant fixed mirror and back; by the time it returns, the spinning mirror has turned slightly, so the returning beam is deflected by a small angle. Measure the angle and the mirror's speed, and you have the speed of light. The great advantage was that the light path could be much shorter, small enough to fit in a laboratory.

In 1850 Foucault (and, independently, Fizeau with Louis Breguet) used the method to settle a long-standing argument. Newton's theory that light consists of particles predicted that light should travel faster in water than in air; the wave theory predicted slower. With a tube of water in part of the light path, Foucault showed that light is slower in water: a powerful argument for waves. [21] In 1862, with a path of about 20 metres, he measured 298,000 km/s, within 0.6 per cent of the modern value, and used the result to argue that the accepted distance of the Sun was too large. [9]

8. Maxwell: Light Is an Electromagnetic Wave

Meanwhile the speed of light appeared in a completely unexpected place. In 1856 the German physicists Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch measured a constant that relates electric charge measured by its electric effects to charge measured by its magnetic effects. The constant had the units of a speed, and expressed in the units Maxwell later used, it came out at about 310,000 km/s: suspiciously close to the speed of light.

In 1865 the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell completed his theory of electricity and magnetism. His equations predicted that electric and magnetic fields could travel through space as waves, and that their speed should be set by exactly this constant. Maxwell drew the conclusion: "light is an electromagnetic disturbance propagated through the field according to electromagnetic laws". [10] The speed of light was no longer just a property of light; it was a basic constant linking electricity and magnetism. Twenty years later Heinrich Hertz produced and detected electromagnetic waves in the laboratory, the radio waves on which much of modern life depends.

9. Michelson: A Lifetime of Measuring Light

No one devoted more of their life to the speed of light than the American physicist Albert Michelson. As a young instructor at the US Naval Academy in 1879, he improved Foucault's rotating-mirror method, using a much longer path and a deflection large enough to measure accurately, and obtained 299,910 ± 50 km/s. [11] In 1887, with Edward Morley, he built the famous interferometer that searched for the Earth's motion through the "luminiferous ether" that was supposed to carry light waves, and found none. [14] In 1905 Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity explained why: the speed of light in empty space is the same for every observer, however they are moving, and there is no ether to detect. [15]

Michelson kept improving his measurements. In 1926 he sent light from a spinning eight-sided mirror on Mount Wilson in California to a mirror on Mount San Antonio, about 35 km away, after the distance between the two mountains had been surveyed to a few millimetres. The result was 299,796 ± 4 km/s. [12] His last experiment used a mile-long evacuated pipe, so that the light travelled through near-vacuum, with mirrors bouncing it back and forth several times. Michelson died in 1931, before it was finished; his colleagues Francis Pease and Fred Pearson published the result, 299,774 ± 11 km/s, in 1935. [13] It was in fact slightly further from the true value than the 1926 measurement, a reminder that small, unrecognised errors can affect even the most careful work.

10. Microwaves, Lasers and the Last Measurement

After the Second World War, radar technology brought new methods. In 1950 the British physicist Louis Essen measured the frequencies at which microwaves resonate inside a precisely made metal cavity. Since the size of the cavity fixes the wavelength, frequency times wavelength gives the speed: 299,792.5 km/s, so much higher than the accepted value of the time that many physicists at first doubted it. It turned out to be right to within a couple of metres per second. [16]

The final step came with the laser. In 1972 a team led by Kenneth Evenson at the US National Bureau of Standards in Boulder measured both the frequency and the wavelength of a highly stable infrared laser. The product gave a speed of light of 299,792,456.2 ± 1.1 metres per second, about a hundred times more precise than any earlier value. [17] By then the main uncertainty was no longer in the measurement of light at all, but in the definition of the metre, which was based on the wavelength of light from krypton atoms and could not be realised more precisely.

So in 1983 the General Conference on Weights and Measures turned the problem around. The speed of light was fixed at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, and the metre was redefined as the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second. [18] Since then, the speed of light can no longer be "measured". An experiment that times light over a distance is now, strictly, a measurement of the distance.

12. Try It Yourself: The Speed of Light in a Microwave Oven

Try it yourself: measure c with chocolate

A microwave oven fills its box with microwaves that bounce off the metal walls and form a standing wave: a fixed pattern of hot and cold spots. Hot spots are half a wavelength apart.

  1. Take the turntable out (or put a plate upside down over its spindle) so that the food does not rotate.
  2. Put a flat bar of chocolate, or a plate covered evenly with grated cheese or marshmallows, inside.
  3. Heat on full power for about 15–20 seconds, just until a few spots start to melt. Do not run the oven empty.
  4. Measure the distance between the centres of neighbouring melted spots. It is typically about 6 cm.
  5. Find the oven's frequency on the label on the back or inside the door, usually 2,450 MHz (2.45 GHz).

The wavelength is twice the spacing of the spots, and speed = frequency × wavelength. With spots 6.1 cm apart: c = 2.45 × 109 per second × 0.122 m ≈ 300,000 km/s. Ovens have a complicated pattern of waves, so your result may be off by 10–20 per cent, but you will have measured the speed of light with a kitchen appliance. Let the oven and food cool before handling, and ask an adult for help if needed.

13. How Did They Know? Turning Time into Something You Can See

How did they know? Measuring millionths of a second without electronics

Fizeau and Foucault had no electronic timers. Their trick was to turn an impossibly short time into something they could see or measure at leisure: a dark or bright image, or a small shift in position. [20]

In Fizeau's experiment, the short time is converted into the rotation of a wheel. The wheel's speed was found by counting turns with clockwork gears over many seconds, an easy measurement; at that speed, the wheel turns by half a tooth spacing (about 1/1,440 of a turn) during the light's round trip. In Foucault's method, the time becomes an angle: while the light travels to the distant mirror and back, the spinning mirror turns by a tiny angle, and the returning beam lands slightly to one side of where it started. That displacement, a fraction of a millimetre, could be measured with a microscope.

Later methods used the same principle with different tools. Essen turned the problem into a measurement of frequency, which could be counted electronically with great precision, and Evenson's team measured the frequency of a laser by comparing it, step by step, with the cesium atomic clocks that define the second.

14. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did Rømer measure the speed of light?

The legend: in 1676 Ole Rømer measured the speed of light and found it to be about 220,000 km/s.

The record: Rømer showed that light takes time to travel, and estimated how long it takes to cross the Earth's orbit, about 22 minutes. He did not publish a value for the speed itself. The first number came from Huygens, who combined Rømer's delay with Cassini's estimate of the size of the Earth's orbit. [6, 4]

The grain of truth: Rømer's insight was the crucial one: that the timing of Io's eclipses depends on distance. Turning it into a speed was arithmetic, once the size of the Earth's orbit was known.

A second legend: "Rømer's result was immediately accepted." In fact, many astronomers, including Cassini, remained unconvinced for decades. It was Bradley's discovery of aberration in 1729 that finally settled the question.

15. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: can we measure the one-way speed of light?

Look again at the experiments in this part. Fizeau, Foucault and Michelson all sent light to a distant mirror and timed its return. Essen and Evenson measured waves bouncing back and forth. Every one of them measured the round-trip speed of light. Could we instead time light going in one direction only, from A to B?

To do that, we need a clock at A and a clock at B that show the same time. But how do we synchronise two distant clocks? We could send a light signal from one to the other, but then we must already know how long the signal takes to travel, which is exactly what we are trying to measure. We could carry a clock from A to B, but according to relativity, moving clocks run slow, and the amount depends on the speed... measured in a way that again assumes the one-way speed of light.

Einstein, in his 1905 paper, simply defined distant clocks to be synchronised when light takes equal times in both directions. [15] Philosophers such as Hans Reichenbach argued that this is a convention, not a fact we have discovered: we could, in principle, choose a different convention in which light travels faster one way than the other, and all our predictions would come out the same. [19] Physicists still debate how deep this point goes, but every experiment so far is consistent with the simple choice.

Questions to think about: If no experiment could ever tell the difference, does it make sense to ask whether light "really" travels at the same speed in both directions? How much of what we call a measurement depends on definitions we have chosen?
