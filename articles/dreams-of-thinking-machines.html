Dreams of Thinking Machines: From Bronze Giants to Babbage’s Engines

Long before computers, people dreamed of machines that could act, calculate and even think. This is the story of how myths, clockwork toys, weaving looms and the algebra of logic quietly laid the foundations of artificial intelligence.

1. What Is a Thinking Machine?

Today you can type a question into a phone and get a fluent answer back in seconds. It feels brand new, but the wish behind it is very old. For nearly three thousand years people have imagined, and sometimes tried to build, machines that could act, calculate or even think like us. [1]

This series tells that story in eight parts, from the first myths to the AI of today and the arguments about where it is heading. This first part covers the long period before electronic computers. Nobody in it built a machine that could think. But between them, they came up with three ideas that modern AI still rests on:

  1. A machine can follow a stored set of instructions, a program, and changing the instructions changes what it does.
  2. Reasoning might be a kind of calculation, a matter of combining symbols by fixed rules.
  3. Logic can be written as arithmetic with just two values, true and false, or 1 and 0.

When these three ideas finally met electricity in the 1930s, the computer age began. That is where Part 2 picks up.

2. The Story at a Glance

Tap any point on the timeline to read about it. The points are in order but not to scale: the gaps between them shrink from centuries to decades as the story speeds up.

3. Machines in Myth

The oldest thinking machines are found in stories. In Homer's Iliad, composed around 700 BC, the lame smith-god Hephaestus is helped around his workshop by maidens made of gold. Homer says they have "understanding in their hearts", along with speech and strength. Hephaestus also builds three-legged stands on golden wheels that roll by themselves to the gods' meetings and back. [2]

An even more famous creation is Talos, a giant of bronze who guards the island of Crete. In the version told by Apollonius of Rhodes in the Argonautica (3rd century BC), Talos strides round the island three times a day, hurling boulders at approaching ships. He has a single vein running from his neck to his ankle, sealed with a bronze nail. The sorceress Medea defeats him by attacking that one weak spot. (In Apollonius's telling, she bewitches him so that he grazes his ankle on a rock, and his life pours out like molten lead.)

The historian Adrienne Mayor points out that these stories are not about magic in the usual sense. Talos is made, by a craftsman, out of materials, and he works by a kind of internal mechanism. He even has a design flaw. The Greeks were already imagining what we would call robots, and already imagining how they might go wrong. [2]

Other cultures had their own versions. In Jewish folklore a golem is a figure of clay brought to life by sacred words. The best-known tale, of a golem made by Rabbi Judah Loew to protect the Jews of 16th-century Prague, was only attached to him much later, in the 18th and 19th centuries. In many versions the golem grows dangerous and has to be switched off by removing the word that animates it. The fear that a servant made by humans might slip out of control is one of the oldest themes in this whole story.

4. Real Machines That Moved by Themselves

Myths were not the only thing. People also built automata (singular: automaton), machines that move by themselves once set going. [1]

In the 1st century AD, Hero of Alexandria wrote books describing temple doors that opened when a fire was lit on an altar, birds that sang using water and air pressure, and a small theatre that performed a whole play by itself. The theatre was driven by a slowly falling weight. Its cord was wound round drums in a particular arrangement, and pegs on the drums made the cord pull different parts at different moments. The order of the scenes was built into how the cord was wound: a simple stored sequence of actions. [3, 4]

Around 850, three brothers in Baghdad known as the Banu Musa ("sons of Musa") described an automatic flute player. Its tune was set by pins sticking out of a turning cylinder, rather like a music box. Each pin opened a hole in the flute as it came round. Swap in a cylinder with the pins in different places and the same machine plays a different tune. Historians of technology regard it as one of the earliest known programmable machines: its behaviour is set by a pattern that can be changed without rebuilding the machine. [4]

In 1206 the engineer Ismail al-Jazari, working for a ruler in what is now southeastern Turkey, finished his Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices. It describes water clocks, pumps and automata, with careful drawings so that others could build them. One design is a boat carrying four mechanical musicians to entertain guests at a party. The drummer is worked by pegs on a turning shaft that lift and drop his arms. A modern reconstruction by the roboticist Noel Sharkey showed that moving the pegs changes the rhythm, which is why the boat is sometimes called an early programmable robot. Whether al-Jazari himself thought of it that way, we can't know. [5]

None of these machines could think, or even choose. They repeated the same performance every time. But they introduced an idea that is easy to overlook: you can separate what a machine is from what it does, by storing the "what it does" as a pattern of pins, pegs or cords.

5. Clockwork Life

In 17th- and 18th-century Europe, clockmaking became extraordinarily precise, and automata became a craze. They were not just toys. They raised a serious question: if a machine can imitate a living thing this well, perhaps living things are machines too. [6]

The French inventor Jacques de Vaucanson exhibited a life-sized flute player in 1738 that really blew into its flute, moving its lips and fingers to play twelve tunes. In 1739 he showed his most famous creation: a gilded copper duck that flapped its wings, quacked, ate grain and then appeared to digest it and pass droppings. Audiences were amazed. Over a century later, the magician Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, who repaired the duck, revealed the trick: the grain went into one compartment, and the "droppings" were stored in another, ready-made. [6]

An even more remarkable machine still works today. Between 1768 and 1774, the Swiss watchmaker Pierre Jaquet-Droz, his son Henri-Louis and their colleague Jean-Frédéric Leschot built The Writer: a doll of a boy at a desk, made of about 6,000 parts, who dips a quill in ink and writes. Inside is a wheel on which the letters of a message can be set, up to 40 characters long. Change the settings and the boy writes something different. It is one of the clearest early examples of a machine whose output is set by a changeable program. It can still be seen working in the museum of art and history in Neuchâtel, Switzerland.

These machines fed a heated debate. Some thinkers, such as the French doctor Julien Offray de La Mettrie in his book Man a Machine (1747), argued that humans are simply very complicated machines. Others, following René Descartes, insisted that bodies might be mechanical, but minds could not be. We return to that argument in section 15.

6. Could Reasoning Be Calculation?

Automata imitated movement. A different line of thinkers asked whether thought itself could be mechanised.

The first serious attempt came from Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1316), a writer and missionary from the island of Majorca. Llull wanted to convince Muslims and Jews of the truth of Christianity, not by force but by an argument so systematic that any reasonable person would have to accept it. His method, which he called his Art, began with a short list of basic ideas, each given a letter: B for Goodness, C for Greatness, D for Eternity, and so on up to K. [7]

He then drew these letters around circles, and some versions of the Art had circles of paper that could be turned against each other. As you turned an inner wheel, each letter lined up with a different partner, so every possible pairing (Goodness with Truth, Power with Wisdom, and so on) came up in turn, without anything being missed.

Llull's wheels produced no new knowledge by themselves: someone still had to think about what each pairing meant. But he had hit on something new. He used a mechanical procedure to make sure every combination was considered, and he treated ideas as symbols that could be combined by rules. Four centuries later, his work inspired Leibniz. [7, 9]

In 1651 the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes put the idea bluntly in his book Leviathan. Reason, he wrote, "is nothing but reckoning (that is, adding and subtracting)". In Hobbes's view, to think is to calculate with words, adding ideas together and taking them away. If he was right, a calculating machine might in principle be able to reason. [8]

7. Leibniz: "Let Us Calculate"

Calculation itself was being mechanised at the same time. In 1642 the French teenager Blaise Pascal built a box of geared wheels that could add and subtract, to help his father, a tax official, with his endless sums. About fifty of these "Pascalines" were made. [9]

The German thinker Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), who also invented calculus (see Part 11 of How We Came to Know), went further. In 1673 he showed the Royal Society in London a design for a machine that could multiply and divide as well. Its key part, a drum with steps of different lengths, was still used in mechanical calculators in the 20th century. Leibniz wrote that it was "unworthy of excellent men to lose hours like slaves in the labour of calculation", which could safely be left to a machine.

But Leibniz's real dream was much bigger. He imagined two things:

  • A universal language of thought, in which every basic idea would have its own symbol, and complex ideas would be built from these like numbers built from digits.
  • A calculus of reasoning: rules for combining those symbols so exactly that working out whether an argument is correct would be like checking a sum.

If this could be done, Leibniz wrote, then when two people disagreed, there would be no need for long quarrels. They could simply sit down and say to each other: "Calculemus!", "Let us calculate!" [9]

Leibniz never built his language of thought, and it later turned out (Part 11 of How We Came to Know) that some questions can't be settled by any calculation at all. But he also left a quieter gift. He studied binary numbers, which use only the digits 0 and 1, and published an account of them in 1703. Today every digital computer stores everything (numbers, words, pictures and AI models) as long strings of 0s and 1s. [9]

8. Weaving with Holes: Jacquard's Loom

The next step came from an unexpected place: silk weaving in Lyon. Weaving a complicated pattern, such as flowers in silk, meant lifting different threads at every pass of the shuttle. It needed an assistant (often a child) perched on the loom to pull the right threads in the right order, and mistakes were easy. [10]

Building on earlier inventions, Joseph-Marie Jacquard perfected a loom around 1804 that did the job automatically. The pattern was stored on a long chain of stiff cards with holes punched in them. For each pass, one card was pressed against a set of rods: where there was a hole, a rod passed through and its thread was lifted; where there was no hole, it stayed down. Change the cards and the same loom weaves a completely different design.

The Jacquard loom was a huge commercial success, and it showed something important: a long and complicated set of instructions could be stored as holes in cards. One famous demonstration was a portrait of Jacquard himself, woven in silk using about 24,000 cards. It was so fine that people mistook it for an engraving. The mathematician Charles Babbage owned a copy and liked to show it to visitors. It gave him an idea. [10, 11]

9. Babbage's Engines

In the early 1800s, navigators, engineers, bankers and astronomers all relied on printed tables of numbers: logarithms, tides, star positions. The tables were worked out by people called "computers" (the word first meant a person who computes), then copied by hand and typeset. Every stage introduced errors, and errors in a navigation table could wreck ships. [11]

The English mathematician Charles Babbage (1791–1871) is said to have exclaimed while checking a set of tables with his friend John Herschel: "I wish to God these calculations had been executed by steam!" In 1822 he proposed a machine to do it: the Difference Engine, which would calculate tables automatically and print the results, removing human error from every step.

The clever part was a technique called the method of differences. It lets you calculate a whole table of values for many formulas using nothing but addition, which is much easier to build into gears than multiplication. Take the square numbers 0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. The differences between them are 1, 3, 5, 7, 9; and the differences between those are always 2. So to get the next square, you just add 2 to the last difference, then add that difference to the last square. Figure 4 lets you turn the handle.

The British government funded the project, eventually spending about £17,000, a huge sum then. But the engine needed thousands of precisely made parts, and after a dispute with his chief engineer, Joseph Clement, work stopped in 1833. Only a small demonstration section was ever finished. It worked beautifully. [11]

By then Babbage had lost interest in it anyway, because he had thought of something far more powerful. From 1834 he designed the Analytical Engine, a machine that could carry out any sequence of arithmetic operations, not just one kind of table. Its design had parts we would recognise in a modern computer:

  • a store holding up to 1,000 numbers of 40 digits each (what we call memory);
  • a mill that did the arithmetic (a processor);
  • instructions and data fed in on punched cards, borrowed from Jacquard;
  • the ability to choose what to do next depending on a result, for example repeating a set of steps until a number reached zero.

That last feature is crucial. A machine that can make decisions based on its own results, and loop back to repeat steps, can in principle carry out any calculation that can be described step by step. A century later, Alan Turing would give a precise meaning to that idea (Part 2). Babbage spent the rest of his life refining the design, but the Analytical Engine was never built.

10. Ada Lovelace: Beyond Numbers

Ada Lovelace (1815–1852) was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron, though she never knew him. Her mother, determined that Ada should not inherit her father's "poetic" moods, had her educated in mathematics and science, unusual for a girl at the time. At seventeen she met Babbage, saw his demonstration engine and was fascinated. [11, 12]

In 1842 an Italian engineer, Luigi Menabrea, published a description of the Analytical Engine in French. Lovelace translated it into English and, encouraged by Babbage, added her own notes, labelled A to G. Published in 1843, her notes were about three times longer than the article itself.

The last note, Note G, set out in full detail how the engine could calculate a series of numbers known as the Bernoulli numbers: every operation, every number moved in and out of the store, in order. It is often called the first computer program. Historians point out that Babbage had sketched shorter programs of his own in earlier years, so "the first published program of real complexity" is fairer. Either way, it shows a deep understanding of how the machine would actually work. [12]

But Lovelace's most original contribution was an idea, not a program. She saw that the engine was not really a number machine at all. It manipulated symbols according to rules, and the symbols could stand for anything:

Supposing, for instance, that the fundamental relations of pitched sounds in the science of harmony and of musical composition were susceptible of such expression and adaptations, the engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent.

She also put it more poetically: "The Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves." This leap, from a calculator to a general symbol-handling machine, is exactly what makes today's computers, and today's AI, possible.

Yet Lovelace also set a limit, one that is still argued over. The engine, she wrote, "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." In 1950 Alan Turing called this "Lady Lovelace's Objection" and tried to answer it (section 15 and Part 2). [12, 21]

11. The Algebra of Thought: Boole

The final ingredient came from a self-taught mathematician, the son of a shoemaker from Lincoln in England. George Boole (1815–1864) left school early, taught himself languages and mathematics, and became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College, Cork, in Ireland. [13]

In 1854 he published An Investigation of the Laws of Thought. Its aim was to show that logic, the rules of correct reasoning that had come down from Aristotle, could be written as a kind of algebra. In Boole's system a statement is either true or false, written 1 or 0, and statements are combined with a few simple operations:

  • AND: "it is sunny AND I am free" is true only if both parts are true. Boole wrote this as multiplication, since 1 × 1 = 1 but anything times 0 is 0.
  • OR: "it is sunny OR I am free" is true if at least one part is true.
  • NOT: "it is NOT sunny" simply flips true and false. Boole wrote it as 1 − x.

Hobbes had said reasoning was adding and subtracting. Boole showed, in precise detail, what that could actually mean. His work was admired but seen as rather abstract for decades. Then in 1937 a 21-year-old student at MIT, Claude Shannon, noticed in his master's thesis that Boole's algebra describes electrical switches perfectly. Two switches in a row act as AND: the current only flows if both are closed. Two switches side by side act as OR. [15]

Before Shannon, there had already been machines that did logic. In 1869 the English economist William Stanley Jevons built a "logic piano", with keys for words like "and" and "or" and for the terms of an argument. Press the keys to enter the premises, and the machine displayed which conclusions were still possible. Jevons described it to the Royal Society in 1870. It was slow and limited, but it was the first machine to solve logic problems faster than a person could by hand. [14]

Shannon's thesis showed that any logical rule Boole could write down could be built from switches. Put that together with Babbage's stored program and Leibniz's binary numbers, and you have the blueprint for a digital computer.

12. Stories of Artificial Minds

As machines grew more powerful in the 19th century, writers began to ask what might happen if they kept improving. [1]

In 1863, just four years after Darwin's On the Origin of Species, the English writer Samuel Butler, then sheep-farming in New Zealand, published a letter in a Christchurch newspaper called "Darwin among the Machines". If living things evolve, he asked, why not machines? They were improving far faster than animals. One day, he suggested, they might become our masters, with humans reduced to tending them. Half joking and half serious, he called for war against machines before it was too late. He developed the idea in his novel Erewhon (1872), set in a country that has banned machines for exactly this reason. [16]

The word we still use came from a play. Karel Čapek's R.U.R. (Rossum's Universal Robots) was published in 1920 and first performed in Prague in January 1921. Its robots are artificial people made in a factory to do humanity's work. In the end they rebel and wipe out their makers. Čapek's brother Josef suggested the name, from the Czech word robota, meaning forced labour. The play was a worldwide hit, and "robot" entered almost every language. [17]

Two worries that dominate today's debates about AI were therefore already clear before a single electronic computer existed: that thinking machines might take our work, and that they might one day turn against us. We come back to both in Part 8.

13. How Did They Know? Would Babbage's Engine Have Worked?

How did they know? Building Babbage's design 150 years late

Babbage never finished a full engine, so for a long time people argued about whether his designs could ever have worked. Perhaps Victorian engineering simply wasn't precise enough, or perhaps the designs had hidden mistakes. How could anyone find out? [11]

In the 1980s, Doron Swade, curator of computing at the Science Museum in London, decided on the most direct test possible: build one. The team worked from Babbage's detailed drawings for his later, improved design, Difference Engine No. 2 (drawn in 1847–1849). They followed three rules:

  1. Stick to the drawings: change nothing except to fix clear slips in the plans, and record every change.
  2. Use only what Babbage could have had: parts were made no more precisely than 19th-century workshops could manage, so a success would show the design worked with the technology of its time.
  3. Test it properly: the finished machine would be judged on whether it produced correct tables, not just whether it moved.

The calculating section was finished in 1991, for the 200th anniversary of Babbage's birth. It has about 4,000 parts and weighs around 2.6 tonnes. After some early jams were cured, it worked. It calculates to 31 digits, and a printer built later from Babbage's plans prints the results. The verdict was clear: Babbage's failure was one of money, management and politics, not of ideas. Had he been able to finish it, the machine would have worked.

14. Legend and Record: The Chess-Playing Turk

Legend and Record: a thinking machine that wasn't

In 1770 the Hungarian inventor Wolfgang von Kempelen unveiled a machine at the court of Empress Maria Theresa in Vienna: a life-sized figure dressed in Turkish robes, seated at a cabinet with a chessboard on top. It played chess against human challengers, and usually won. [18]

The legend: a machine that could think. Before each show, Kempelen opened the cabinet's doors one at a time to reveal a mass of gears, and audiences went away convinced they had seen a mechanical mind. The Turk toured Europe and America for more than eighty years. Accounts say it played Benjamin Franklin in Paris and Napoleon in 1809, though some of the details of these games come from later retellings.

The record: a strong human chess player was hidden inside. The doors were opened in an order that let the operator slide out of view, and he followed the game from below through magnets under the board. Many people suspected a trick. In 1836 Edgar Allan Poe published an essay, "Maelzel's Chess-Player", arguing that a person must be inside. The secret was finally published in 1857, after the Turk was destroyed in a fire in Philadelphia in 1854. [18, 19]

Why it matters: the Turk shows how easily people believe a machine is thinking when it behaves cleverly, and how important it is to look inside. That lesson matters even more today. Amazon even named its online service for small human tasks, which quietly supports many "automatic" systems, "Mechanical Turk".

15. Philosophy Corner: Could a Machine Ever Really Think?

Philosophy Corner: Descartes's two tests

In 1637, in his Discourse on the Method, the French philosopher René Descartes asked how we could tell a very clever machine from a real human being. He gave two tests, which he was sure no machine could ever pass: [20]

  • Language: a machine might be built to say particular words when touched in a particular place. But it could never arrange words in different ways to reply sensibly to whatever is said to it, "as even the dullest of men can do".
  • Flexibility: a machine might do some things as well as we do, or better. But it would fail at others, because it acts only according to how its parts are arranged, not from understanding. Reason, by contrast, is a "universal instrument" that can deal with any situation.

Descartes concluded that the mind must be something other than a machine. Ada Lovelace, two centuries later, drew a similar line: a machine only does what it is told (section 10).

Here is the twist. Today's AI chatbots can hold a fluent conversation on almost any topic, which looks very like Descartes's first test being passed. So did Descartes get it wrong? Or does producing the right words not prove there is understanding behind them, just as the Turk's moves didn't prove the cabinet could think? Philosophers still disagree. In 1950 Alan Turing proposed a test of his own, built on exactly this question, and that is where Part 2 begins. [21]
