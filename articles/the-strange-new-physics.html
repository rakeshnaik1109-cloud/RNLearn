1. Two Small Clouds: Physics in 1900

By 1900, physics looked almost finished. Newton's laws explained the motion of planets and comets; Maxwell's equations united electricity, magnetism and light (Part 9); and the laws of thermodynamics governed heat and energy. Many physicists felt the main work was done, and that future generations would only be measuring things more precisely. [3]

On 27 April 1900, the 75-year-old Lord Kelvin, one of the most respected scientists of the age, gave a lecture at the Royal Institution in London. He praised the clarity of physics, but warned that two "clouds" still darkened the sky:

  1. The missing ether wind: the Earth moves at about 30 kilometres per second through the "ether", the invisible substance light was thought to travel through. So why could nobody detect any effect of this motion on light?
  2. The heat puzzle: why did the best theory of heat give the wrong answers for how gases store heat, and for the colours of light given off by hot objects?

Kelvin hoped these would be cleared up with small adjustments. Instead, within thirty years they produced two revolutions. The first cloud led to Einstein's relativity, which overturned Newton's fixed space and time. The second led to quantum physics, which replaced definite paths with probabilities.

2. The Ether Wind That Wasn't There: Michelson and Morley

In the 1800s everyone assumed that a wave needs something to travel through. Sea waves move through water; sound moves through air. When Maxwell showed that light is a wave (Part 9), physicists concluded that it must travel through something too: an invisible substance filling all of space, called the luminiferous ether ("light-carrying ether"). [5]

The ether had to be very strange: stiffer than steel to carry such fast waves, yet so thin that planets could move through it for billions of years without slowing down. And it gave a fixed background, a universal "at rest", against which all motion could be measured.

The Earth goes round the Sun at about 30 km per second, so it should be rushing through the ether. On Earth we should feel an "ether wind", just as you feel a breeze on your face when cycling on a still day. And light should travel at slightly different speeds depending on whether it goes with this wind, against it or across it.

In 1887, in Cleveland, Ohio, the physicist Albert Michelson (1852–1931) and the chemist Edward Morley (1838–1923) built an instrument sensitive enough to detect this: an interferometer.

It split a beam of light into two beams at right angles, sent each along an arm about 11 metres long (folded back and forth between mirrors), and brought them back together. When two light beams recombine, their waves add up in some places and cancel in others, making a pattern of light and dark stripes called fringes. If one beam took even slightly longer than the other, the stripes would shift.

If the ether wind existed, the beam travelling along the wind and back should take a tiny bit longer than the beam going across it, like a swimmer who finds going upstream and back slower than going across a river and back. Turning the whole instrument round by 90 degrees would swap the roles of the two arms and shift the stripes by about four-tenths of a stripe. To turn it smoothly without shaking the mirrors, they mounted everything on a heavy stone slab floating on a pool of mercury.

In July 1887 they made their measurements, and the stripes didn't move. Any shift was less than about a twentieth of what was expected. As far as they could tell, there was no ether wind at all. Later experimenters repeated the test, at other times of year and with better equipment, with the same result.

For eighteen years theorists tried to save the ether. George FitzGerald and Hendrik Lorentz suggested that anything moving through the ether is squashed very slightly in its direction of motion, by exactly the right amount to hide the effect. It worked, but it looked like a convenient excuse. Nature simply refused to show any universal "at rest".

3. The Glow of Hot Objects: Planck's Quantum

While Michelson's mirrors were defeating the ether, physicists in Berlin were studying Kelvin's second cloud: the light given off by hot objects. German industry wanted better electric lighting, and the national physics laboratory set out to measure exactly how much light of each colour a hot object gives off. The ideal case is a perfect absorber called a black body, whose glow depends only on its temperature. [6, 4]

When physicists applied the established laws of heat and light to this problem, they got nonsense. Lord Rayleigh and James Jeans worked out the prediction of classical physics, and it said that the shorter the wavelength, the more energy a hot object should give off, without limit. Every hot oven should blast out unlimited amounts of ultraviolet light and X-rays. The physicist Paul Ehrenfest later called this the ultraviolet catastrophe. (Figure 2 shows the classical prediction shooting off the top of the chart while the real curve stays finite.)

In Berlin, the theoretical physicist Max Planck (1858–1947), a careful, conservative man who had spent decades on the science of heat, took up the problem. On 7 October 1900 his colleague Heinrich Rubens visited him with the latest measurements. That same evening Planck found a formula that matched the measurements extremely well at all wavelengths.

But a formula that merely fits isn't an explanation. Planck spent the next weeks trying to derive it from basic principles, and later said he did it "as an act of desperation". He had to use Ludwig Boltzmann's counting approach to entropy (Part 9), which he had previously rejected.

On 14 December 1900 he presented his derivation. To make it work, he had made a strange assumption: the tiny vibrating parts of the hot material can't give out or take in energy smoothly, in any amount. They can only do it in fixed-size packets, which he called quanta. The size of a packet depends on the frequency of the light:

$$E = hf$$

Here $f$ is the frequency (how many times the wave vibrates per second) and $h$ is a new constant of nature, now called Planck's constant, an incredibly small number (about 6.6 × 10−34 joule-seconds).

This solved the catastrophe. High-frequency light (ultraviolet and beyond) comes in big, "expensive" packets. A hot object simply doesn't have enough heat energy to spend on many of them, so very little ultraviolet is given off. At low frequencies, where the packets are small, Planck's formula agrees with the old theory.

Planck himself was a reluctant revolutionary. He saw the quantum as a mathematical trick about how hot material gives off light, not as a fact about light itself. It took a younger, bolder thinker to take the idea seriously.

4. 1905: Einstein's Miracle Year and Particles of Light

In 1905 a 26-year-old clerk at the Swiss patent office in Bern was working six days a week examining patent applications for electrical devices. He hadn't managed to get a university job. In his spare time he discussed physics and philosophy with his wife Mileva Marić and with friends in an informal group they jokingly called the "Olympia Academy". His name was Albert Einstein (1879–1955). [2, 3]

Between March and September 1905 he sent four extraordinary papers to the journal Annalen der Physik. Historians call 1905 his miracle year:

  • March: light comes in packets (the photoelectric effect).
  • May: the jiggling of tiny particles in water (Brownian motion), which gave convincing evidence that atoms are real.
  • June: special relativity, overturning fixed space and time.
  • September: mass and energy are equivalent: $E = mc^2$.

The only one Einstein himself called "revolutionary" was the paper on light. For a century, experiments had shown that light is a wave. Yet when ultraviolet light shines on a metal plate, it knocks electrons out of it. This photoelectric effect had been discovered by Heinrich Hertz in 1887 and studied carefully by Philipp Lenard in 1902.

Lenard found two things that the wave picture couldn't explain:

  1. Brighter light doesn't make faster electrons. A brighter wave carries more energy, so it should knock electrons out harder. But brighter light only knocked out more electrons; the fastest ones were no faster.
  2. The colour matters, not the brightness. Even dim ultraviolet light knocked electrons out instantly. But red light, however bright and however long it shone, knocked out none at all. With waves, even faint red light should eventually build up enough energy.

Einstein took Planck's quantum literally. Light isn't just given off in packets; light itself is made of packets, later named photons. Each photon carries an amount of energy that depends only on its frequency ($E = hf$). Ultraviolet photons carry more energy than red ones.

When a photon hits an electron in the metal, it gives up all its energy at once. Part of that energy is needed just to pull the electron free of the metal; the rest becomes the electron's energy of motion. So a red photon, which carries too little energy, can never free an electron, however many of them arrive. A brighter light has more photons, so it frees more electrons, but each photon still has the same energy, so the electrons are no faster. It explained both puzzles at once.

Einstein's idea also made a precise prediction: plot the energy of the fastest electrons against the frequency of the light, and you should get a straight line whose slope is Planck's constant. The American experimenter Robert Millikan spent about ten years trying to prove Einstein wrong. In 1916 he had to admit that the prediction was right, and his measurements gave the best value of Planck's constant yet. Einstein received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1921, specifically for the law of the photoelectric effect.

5. Special Relativity: Time Can Stretch

In June 1905 Einstein took on Kelvin's first cloud in a paper called "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies". Lorentz and Henri Poincaré had been trying to patch up the ether. Einstein simply threw it away and built the special theory of relativity on two simple principles: [1, 3]

  1. No one is truly "at rest": the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving at a steady speed. There is no special, absolute state of rest.
  2. The speed of light is the same for everyone: light in empty space always travels at about 300,000 km per second, whoever measures it and however they are moving.

These two simple rules have startling consequences. In Newton's physics, time flows at the same rate everywhere, for everyone. Einstein showed that it doesn't. Two events that happen at the same moment for someone standing on a station platform do not happen at the same moment for someone on a passing train.

Imagine a "light clock": a flash of light bouncing up and down between two mirrors. Each bounce is one tick. For someone travelling with the clock, the light just goes straight up and down.

Now watch the clock fly past you. The mirrors move sideways while the light is travelling, so from your point of view the light has to follow a longer, slanted, zigzag path. But the light can't go faster to make up for it; its speed is fixed. So each tick takes longer. To you, the moving clock runs slow. This is called time dilation. Using Pythagoras' theorem on the slanted path gives the exact amount:

$$t_{\text{moving}} = \frac{t_{\text{rest}}}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$$

where $t_{\text{rest}}$ is the time between ticks for the clock at rest, $t_{\text{moving}}$ the time between ticks when it moves, $v$ is the clock's speed and $c$ the speed of light. At everyday speeds the effect is far too small to notice. At 87% of the speed of light, a moving clock runs at half speed. Moving objects are also shortened in their direction of motion (length contraction), and nothing with mass can be pushed up to the speed of light.

These effects are real and measured every day. Fast-moving particles from space, which should decay long before reaching the ground, survive the trip because their internal clocks run slow. GPS satellites must allow for relativity, or your phone's position would drift off by kilometres a day.

In 1908 the mathematician Hermann Minkowski showed that relativity is best understood by combining space and time into a single four-dimensional spacetime:

Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality.

In September 1905, in a three-page follow-up, Einstein showed that when an object gives off energy, its mass goes down. Mass is a very concentrated form of energy, and the exchange rate is the speed of light squared:

$$E = mc^2$$

Because the speed of light squared is such a huge number, a tiny amount of mass holds an enormous amount of energy. One gram of matter is equivalent to about 90 trillion joules, about as much as 20,000 tonnes of TNT. This is the energy source of the Sun and of nuclear power. Lavoisier's conservation of mass (Part 7) and the conservation of energy (Part 9) turned out to be two sides of one law.

6. Inside the Atom: Rutherford Finds the Nucleus

While Einstein was reshaping space and time, other physicists were looking inside the atom. Since Dalton (Part 7), atoms had been pictured as solid, unbreakable balls. In 1897, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, J. J. Thomson (1856–1940) found something smaller: the electron, a negatively charged particle nearly two thousand times lighter than the lightest atom, hydrogen. [7]

Atoms are electrically neutral overall, so if they contain negative electrons they must also contain positive charge. In 1904 Thomson suggested the plum pudding model: the atom is a ball of spread-out positive charge with tiny electrons dotted through it, like raisins in a pudding.

At Manchester, the New Zealand-born physicist Ernest Rutherford (1871–1937), with his assistant Hans Geiger and the student Ernest Marsden, put this model to the test. They used alpha particles, heavy, positively charged particles shot out at high speed by radioactive materials, and fired a narrow beam of them at a sheet of gold foil only a few thousand atoms thick. Behind the foil was a screen that gave a tiny flash of light whenever an alpha particle hit it. Geiger and Marsden spent hours in the dark counting the flashes through a microscope.

If Thomson was right, the positive charge in each atom was spread thinly through the whole atom, so its push on a fast, heavy alpha particle would be weak. The alpha particles should sail through with only tiny changes of direction, never more than a degree or two.

Most did go straight through. But Rutherford asked Marsden to look for particles bounced through large angles, and to everyone's amazement he found some: about 1 in 8,000 was turned through more than 90 degrees, and a few bounced almost straight back.

Rutherford later described his astonishment:

It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.

In 1911 Rutherford published the explanation: for an alpha particle to bounce back, it must have met an enormous push from a tiny, concentrated lump of positive charge. All the positive charge, and almost all the mass, of an atom is packed into a tiny central core: the nucleus.

The nucleus is about ten thousand times smaller across than the atom. If an atom were the size of a cathedral, the nucleus would be a fly in the middle, and the electrons would be specks of dust near the walls. Solid matter is almost entirely empty space.

7. Bohr's Quantum Atom

Rutherford's picture had a serious problem: according to Maxwell's laws, it couldn't last. An electron going round a nucleus is constantly changing direction, and a charge that changes direction gives off light, losing energy. The electron should spiral inwards and crash into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second. Every atom in the universe should collapse almost instantly. [8, 4]

There was a second puzzle. A spiralling electron would give off a smear of all colours. But glowing gases, such as hydrogen in a discharge tube, give off only a few sharp colours, like a barcode that is different for each element. In 1885 a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, had found a formula that fitted hydrogen's visible colours exactly, but nobody knew why it worked.

The answer came in 1913 from a 27-year-old Danish physicist who had worked with Rutherford: Niels Bohr (1885–1962). When a colleague showed him Balmer's formula, Bohr later said, "As soon as I saw Balmer's formula, the whole thing was immediately clear to me."

Bohr combined Rutherford's atom with Planck's quantum in three bold rules:

  1. Only certain orbits are allowed. The electron can circle the nucleus only in particular orbits, and while it is in one of them it doesn't give off any light, contrary to Maxwell's laws.
  2. Each allowed orbit has a fixed energy. These are the atom's energy levels, like the rungs of a ladder. For hydrogen, the energy of level $n$ is $$E_n = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$ (An eV, or electronvolt, is a tiny unit of energy used for atoms.)
  3. Light comes from jumps. An electron gives off light only when it jumps down from a higher rung to a lower one, and the light carries away exactly the energy difference. Since $E = hf$, each jump produces one particular colour.

Because only certain rungs exist, only certain jumps are possible, so only certain colours appear: exactly the barcode seen in hydrogen. From basic constants of nature Bohr calculated the number in Balmer's formula, and it matched the measured value closely. The sharp colours of glowing gases were the signatures of electrons jumping between the rungs of a quantum ladder.

8. Matter Behaves Like Waves: de Broglie

Bohr's model worked brilliantly, but it was an odd mixture of old-style orbits and new quantum rules. Why should only certain orbits be allowed? [4]

The answer came in 1923–1924 from a French aristocrat who had first studied history: Prince Louis de Broglie (1892–1987). His doctoral thesis was so daring that his examiners hesitated to pass it until Einstein said it was worth taking seriously.

Einstein had shown that light, a wave, also behaves like particles. De Broglie asked the reverse question: if waves can behave like particles, can particles such as electrons behave like waves?

He proposed that every moving object has a wave associated with it, with a wavelength that depends on its momentum (mass times speed):

$$\text{wavelength} = \frac{h}{\text{mass} \times \text{speed}}$$

For everyday objects, Planck's constant $h$ is so tiny that the wavelength is unimaginably small, so we never notice. But for an electron it is about the size of an atom.

This explained Bohr's allowed orbits. If an electron is a wave going round the nucleus, the wave must join up smoothly with itself after one lap: a whole number of wavelengths must fit around the orbit, like the vibrations of a guitar string, which must fit a whole number of half-waves between its ends. Only orbits where the wave fits are allowed.

In 1927, at Bell Telephone Laboratories in New York, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired electrons at a nickel crystal and found that they bounced off in a pattern of strong and weak directions, exactly as waves do. At the same time in Aberdeen, George Paget Thomson, son of J. J. Thomson, saw the same wave behaviour by firing electrons through thin foils. There is a neat twist: J. J. Thomson won the Nobel Prize in 1906 for showing the electron is a particle, and his son won it in 1937 for showing it behaves as a wave.

9. Waves of Probability: Schrödinger and Born

If particles are waves, what equation do the waves obey? Over the winter of 1925–1926, while on holiday in the Swiss mountains, the Austrian physicist Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961) found it: the Schrödinger equation, the basic equation of quantum mechanics. It describes how a quantity called the wavefunction (written ψ, "psi") changes over time. [4]

Schrödinger solved his equation for the hydrogen atom and got all of Bohr's energy levels, without needing Bohr's special rules. Instead of flat orbits, his solutions described three-dimensional shapes, called orbitals: some like spheres, some like dumbbells, some like four-leaf clovers. Chemists still use them to explain how atoms bond.

Meanwhile in Göttingen, the 23-year-old Werner Heisenberg, working with Max Born and Pascual Jordan, had built a completely different-looking quantum theory, called matrix mechanics, using tables of numbers instead of waves. In 1926 Schrödinger showed that the two theories were really the same theory written in different ways.

But what is the wavefunction? Schrödinger first hoped the electron was literally a spread-out wave, like a cloud of charge. That didn't work: waves spread out, but whenever an electron is detected, it shows up at one single spot.

In 1926 Max Born (1882–1970) gave the answer still used today. The wavefunction isn't a physical substance; it tells you probabilities. Where the wave is large (more exactly, where its square is large), you are likely to find the particle; where it is small, you are unlikely to. The Born rule says that the chance of finding the particle in a place is given by the square of the wavefunction there.

This was a huge change. Physics could no longer, even in principle, predict exactly where an electron would be found. It could only give the odds.

10. The Uncertainty Principle: Heisenberg

In February 1927, at Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, Heisenberg faced a basic question: if an electron doesn't follow a definite path, what can we actually know about it? [9, 4]

He imagined trying to find an electron's position very precisely with a microscope. To see something tiny clearly, you need light of very short wavelength. But short-wavelength light comes in high-energy photons, and when one bounces off the electron, it gives it a hard, unpredictable kick, changing its speed and direction.

Use gentle, long-wavelength light and you don't disturb the electron much, but you can't see clearly where it is. Use sharp, short-wavelength light to pin down its position and you knock it off course. You can't have both. Heisenberg turned this into the uncertainty principle:

$$(\text{spread in position}) \times (\text{spread in momentum}) \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$

Importantly, this isn't just a limit of our instruments. It comes from the wave nature of matter itself. A wave squeezed into a small region has to be made by combining many different wavelengths, and different wavelengths mean different momenta (de Broglie's rule). So the more tightly a particle's position is defined, the more spread out its momentum must be. Figure 7 lets you see this trade-off directly.

A similar trade-off links energy and time: something that lasts only a very short time doesn't have a precisely defined energy.

11. Gravity as Curved Spacetime: General Relativity

While others were building quantum theory, Einstein spent eight years in a mostly solitary effort to extend relativity to include gravity. The result, in 1915, was the general theory of relativity. [3, 10]

He started from what he called his "happiest thought": a person falling freely doesn't feel their own weight. In a lift falling freely, everything inside floats, as in a space station. Gravity seems to vanish. Einstein turned this into the equivalence principle: the effects of gravity and the effects of acceleration are the same.

From this he concluded that gravity isn't really a force pulling across empty space, as Newton thought. Instead, mass and energy curve spacetime, and objects simply follow the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime. Think of a heavy ball on a trampoline, making a dip that makes marbles roll towards it. As the physicist John Wheeler put it: "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."

Einstein summed this up in his field equations, which connect the curvature of spacetime to the matter and energy in it.

General relativity made testable predictions. It explained a small, long-standing puzzle in the orbit of Mercury (its slow turning was 43 seconds of arc per century more than Newton's laws allowed). It predicted that light climbing out of strong gravity loses energy and shifts towards red. And it predicted that light from distant stars passing close to the Sun should be bent by 1.75 seconds of arc, twice what a Newtonian calculation gave.

On 29 May 1919, during a total eclipse of the Sun, British expeditions photographed stars close to the darkened Sun: Arthur Eddington on the island of Príncipe off West Africa, and a second team at Sobral in Brazil. Comparing these with photos of the same stars taken at night months earlier, they found shifts of about 1.6 and 2.0 seconds of arc, close to Einstein's prediction. The measurements were difficult and their analysis was debated (Part 12 tells that story), but later eclipse and radio-telescope measurements have confirmed Einstein's value precisely.

The results were announced in London on 6 November 1919. The philosopher Alfred North Whitehead, who was there, recalled: "It was like a Greek drama: we were the chorus commenting on the decree of destiny as disclosed in the development of a supreme event." Almost overnight Einstein became world-famous.

12. Einstein and Bohr: Does God Play Dice?

In October 1927, twenty-nine of the world's leading physicists met in Brussels for the fifth Solvay Conference. Seventeen of them were or would become Nobel prize winners. There Einstein and Bohr began a famous debate about what quantum mechanics really means. [3, 4]

Bohr defended what became known as the Copenhagen interpretation: before it is measured, a particle doesn't have definite properties such as an exact position. It is in a superposition, a combination of possibilities, and only when it is measured does one possibility become real (the wavefunction "collapses"). We can't separate what we observe from how we observe it.

Einstein couldn't accept this. He believed in a real world that exists whether or not anyone is looking. "Do you really believe the Moon is not there when nobody is looking at it?" he once asked. In a famous letter to Max Born he wrote:

Quantum mechanics is very impressive. But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the real thing. The theory produces a good deal, but hardly brings us closer to the secret of the Old One. I am at all events convinced that He does not play dice.

At the conference, Einstein would come to breakfast with a clever thought experiment that seemed to beat the uncertainty principle. Bohr would worry over it all day and come back with an answer, sometimes using Einstein's own general relativity against him, as at the 1930 conference, when he showed that weighing a box of light would disturb a clock inside it in just the way the uncertainty principle requires.

In 1935, with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, Einstein raised his sharpest objection, now called the EPR paradox. Imagine two particles created together and then sent far apart, even light-years apart. Quantum mechanics says that measuring one instantly fixes what you will find when you measure the other. These particles are said to be entangled. Einstein later called this "spooky action at a distance". He concluded that quantum mechanics must be incomplete: the particles must carry hidden instructions, set when they were created, that decide the results in advance.

In 1964 the Northern Irish physicist John Bell showed that this could be tested. If hidden instructions of the kind Einstein wanted existed, the results of certain measurements would have to obey a mathematical limit; quantum mechanics predicts that limit is broken. Experiments from the 1970s (John Clauser) and 1980s (Alain Aspect) onwards found that it is broken, just as quantum mechanics says, ruling out Einstein's kind of hidden instructions. Clauser, Aspect and Anton Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize for this work. The quantum world turned out to be stranger than even Einstein would accept.

13. How Did They Know? The Michelson–Morley Interferometer

How did they know? Catching a tiny delay with light waves

How could Michelson and Morley detect a difference in travel time of about a ten-thousand-million-millionth of a second (10−16 s)? [5]

The trick was to turn a tiny delay into something visible: a shift in a pattern of light and dark stripes. Three ideas made it work:

  1. Longer light paths: in Michelson's first attempt, in 1881, the arms were only 1.2 metres long, and the expected effect was barely bigger than the errors. In 1887 they used mirrors to bounce each beam back and forth several times, making each path about 11 metres long and the expected effect about ten times bigger.
  2. Floating on mercury: the whole instrument sat on a heavy stone slab floating on a pool of mercury. That let them turn it smoothly in a full circle without bending the slab or jolting the mirrors.
  3. Comparing two beams: instead of measuring the speed of light along one path, they compared two paths at right angles using the same light. Anything that affected both beams equally, such as a change in room temperature, cancelled out.

Ruling out any shift bigger than about two-hundredths of a stripe, when four-tenths was expected, Michelson and Morley produced one of the most famous "null results" in science: an experiment that found nothing, and in doing so overturned a century of belief in the ether.

14. Legend and Record: Did Michelson–Morley Inspire Einstein? Was He a Lone Genius?

Legend and Record: two myths about relativity

Popular science often turns revolutions into simple hero stories. Two common ones surround relativity: [3, 1]

Did the Michelson–Morley experiment inspire Einstein? Textbooks often say Einstein read about the experiment, saw that the ether had failed, and invented relativity to explain it. The evidence suggests otherwise. In his 1905 paper Einstein doesn't mention Michelson and Morley by name; he refers only vaguely to "unsuccessful attempts to discover any motion of the earth relatively to the 'light medium'".

Einstein came to relativity mainly by thinking about Maxwell's theory of electricity and magnetism. At sixteen he had wondered what it would be like to chase a beam of light: if you could ride alongside it, you would see a frozen light wave, which Maxwell's equations say can't exist. He was also bothered by a lack of symmetry in the theory: moving a magnet past a wire and moving a wire past a magnet produce the same current, yet the theory explained the two cases in completely different ways. Relativity grew from a search for consistency, more than from a failed experiment.

Was Einstein a lone genius? Popular culture pictures Einstein working entirely alone. He was an outsider without a university job, but he wasn't isolated. His wife, Mileva Marić, had studied physics with him at the Zurich Polytechnic, and they discussed physics together (how much she contributed to his early work is debated). In Bern he met regularly with his friends Maurice Solovine and Conrad Habicht in their "Olympia Academy", reading philosophers and scientists such as Hume, Mach and Poincaré. The historian Peter Galison has argued that his patent-office work, at a time when railways and cities were developing electrically synchronised clocks, helped shape his thinking about how to synchronise clocks.

15. Philosophy Corner: What Is Real in the Quantum World?

Philosophy Corner: realism, chance and measurement

Few discoveries since Copernicus have shaken our picture of the world as much as quantum mechanics. For three centuries, physics had rested on two basic assumptions: [4]

  • Realism: things have definite properties (position, speed and so on) whether or not anyone measures them.
  • Determinism: if you knew the exact state of the universe now, the laws of physics would fix its entire future.

The Copenhagen interpretation, developed by Bohr and Heisenberg, gave up both. On this view, an electron doesn't have a definite position between measurements; it has only a wavefunction describing the possibilities. A definite property appears only when the electron interacts with a measuring device, and which result appears is a matter of chance.

The arguments continue today. Schrödinger invented his famous thought experiment, a cat shut in a box with a radioactive atom that might or might not trigger a poison, to show how strange this seems when applied to everyday things: is the cat really both dead and alive until someone looks? Other interpretations try to keep a definite reality. In Hugh Everett's many-worlds interpretation, the wavefunction never collapses; instead the universe branches, and every possible outcome happens in a different branch. In David Bohm's pilot-wave theory, particles always have definite positions but are guided by a wave that connects distant places. The study of decoherence explains why we never see everyday objects in strange superpositions, though not, on its own, why one particular result happens. Physicists still disagree about which picture is right. But all of them agree on one lesson: the universe doesn't have to match our everyday intuitions.

Ernest Rutherford 1871–1937
Manchester / Cambridge, UK		 Gold foil alpha scattering analysis (1911) Discovered the atomic nucleus when alpha particles bounced back from gold foil.
Niels Bohr 1885–1962
Copenhagen, Denmark		 Trilogy on the Constitution of Atoms (1913) Explained the sharp colours of hydrogen with allowed orbits and quantum jumps; led the Copenhagen interpretation.
Louis de Broglie 1892–1987
Paris, France		 Doctoral thesis on matter waves (1924) Proposed that particles such as electrons also behave as waves, explaining Bohr's allowed orbits.
Erwin Schrödinger 1887–1961
Vienna / Zurich / Dublin		 Wave mechanics papers in Annalen der Physik (1926) Found the wave equation of quantum mechanics and the shapes of atomic orbitals.
Werner Heisenberg 1901–1976
Göttingen / Leipzig, Germany		 Matrix mechanics (1925), Uncertainty Principle (1927) Built the first version of quantum mechanics and found the uncertainty principle.
Max Born 1882–1970
Göttingen / Edinburgh		 Statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics (1926) Showed that the wavefunction gives probabilities (the Born rule); helped build matrix mechanics.
Arthur Eddington 1882–1944
Cambridge, UK		 Príncipe solar eclipse expedition (1919) Led the 1919 eclipse expedition that measured starlight bending around the Sun, supporting general relativity.
