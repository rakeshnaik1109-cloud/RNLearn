How Far Are the Stars? The Two-Thousand-Year Hunt for Parallax

If the Earth goes around the Sun, the nearby stars should shift back and forth once a year. For two thousand years nobody could see it. In 1838, a third of a second of arc finally revealed how far away the stars are.

1. A Problem That Took Two Thousand Years

By the end of Part 4, astronomers knew the distance to the Sun: about 150 million kilometres. The stars were another matter. They look like points of light fixed on a sphere, and for most of history nobody could say whether they were a little beyond the planets or unimaginably far away. The only direct way to find out was parallax, the same geometry we used for the Moon in Part 3, but with the whole orbit of the Earth as the baseline.

It took more than two thousand years to see it. The ancient Greeks looked for the shift and did not find it; Tycho Brahe looked and did not find it; Hooke, Bradley and Herschel looked and found other things instead. Only in 1838 did Friedrich Bessel finally measure the parallax of a star, and the answer showed that even the nearest stars are hundreds of thousands of times farther away than the Sun. [1] Today a spacecraft has measured the distances to more than a billion stars.

3. The Missing Shift

Hold up a finger and look at it with one eye, then the other: it jumps against the background. The same should happen to the stars if the Earth goes around the Sun. In January we look from one side of the orbit, in July from the other, and a nearby star should appear to move back and forth against more distant ones once a year. This yearly wobble is stellar parallax, and its size tells you the distance.

Aristotle used the absence of this shift as an argument that the Earth stands still: if it moved, the stars should shift, and they do not. [2] A century later, Aristarchus of Samos proposed that the Earth goes around the Sun. We know this mainly from Archimedes, who reports in The Sand Reckoner that Aristarchus made the sphere of the stars so large that the Earth’s orbit was like a point compared with it. [3] That was the right answer, but at the time it looked like a convenient excuse: a theory that explains away a missing prediction by making the universe vastly bigger.

4. Tycho’s Dilemma

When Copernicus revived the moving Earth in 1543, he faced the same problem and gave the same answer: the stars must be immensely far away. In the 1580s, the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe built the most accurate instruments of the age, huge quadrants and sextants read by eye, with which he could measure positions to about one minute of arc (a sixtieth of a degree). He found no parallax at all.

Tycho drew a careful conclusion. With no shift of a minute of arc, the stars would have to be at least several thousand times farther away than the Sun. But the stars seemed to have visible disks, about a minute of arc or more across. At such distances, a star would have to be as large as the whole orbit of the Earth, or larger. Tycho found that absurd and proposed his own system, in which the Sun goes around a fixed Earth and the planets go around the Sun. [2]

His reasoning was sound; one of his premises was wrong. The disks of stars are not real: they are produced by the eye and by the blurring of the atmosphere and of telescopes. Stars really are points, as later measurements would show. But in 1600, the evidence was genuinely against the moving Earth.

5. Hunting Parallax, Finding Something Else

Telescopes made the search more precise. In 1669 Robert Hooke built a vertical telescope through the roof of his rooms at Gresham College in London and watched the star Gamma Draconis, which passes almost straight overhead. In 1674 he published An Attempt to Prove the Motion of the Earth from Observations, claiming a parallax of about half a minute of arc. [4] It was far too large and was never confirmed: he had only a handful of observations, and his instrument could not reach that precision.

In 1725 Samuel Molyneux and James Bradley repeated the experiment at Kew with a much better instrument, again on Gamma Draconis. The star did move, by about 20 seconds of arc to the north and south over a year, but in the wrong way. Parallax depends on where the Earth is in its orbit, so the star should have been farthest north or south in December and June. Instead the extremes came in March and September, three months off.

Bradley worked out why. The shift follows the Earth’s velocity, not its position. Because light travels at a finite speed, a telescope on the moving Earth must be tilted slightly forward to catch it, just as you must tilt an umbrella forward when you walk through vertical rain. He called it the aberration of starlight, published it in 1729, and from its size calculated that light takes about 8 minutes 12 seconds to reach us from the Sun (Part 5). [5]

Aberration was the first direct proof that the Earth moves. Ironically, it also told Bradley that stellar parallax must be less than about one second of arc, or he would have seen it too.

6. Herschel and the Double Stars

Galileo had suggested a cleverer method. If two stars appear close together in the sky but one is much fainter, the faint one is probably much farther away. Measuring the bright star against its faint neighbour would cancel many errors of the instrument and the atmosphere, and reveal the bright star’s parallax as a small yearly wobble in the pair.

William Herschel, the musician turned astronomer who discovered Uranus, catalogued hundreds of double stars from the 1780s with this aim. When he measured them again over the following decades, he found something unexpected: in many pairs, the two stars were moving around each other. In 1803 he reported that pairs such as Castor are true binary stars, held together by gravity. [6] This showed that Newton’s gravity works beyond the solar system, but it defeated the parallax method, because the stars in a pair are at the same distance.

7. The Race of 1838

By the 1830s, instruments had improved enough to bring the target within reach, and three astronomers succeeded almost at once. The key was choosing the right star. Bright stars are not necessarily close, but stars that move quickly across the sky over the years probably are, just as a nearby car seems to move faster than a distant aircraft. This motion is called proper motion, and the star 61 Cygni, a faint double star in the Swan, had one of the largest known: more than five seconds of arc per year.

Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel, director of the Königsberg Observatory in Prussia, observed 61 Cygni from August 1837 to October 1838 with a heliometer, a telescope whose lens is cut in two so that the halves can slide to bring the images of two stars together. He measured its distance from two faint comparison stars hundreds of times. In late 1838 he announced a parallax of 0.3136 seconds of arc, with an uncertainty of about 0.02 seconds. [7] Light, he noted, would take about ten years to cross that distance.

Two others were close behind. Thomas Henderson had measured Alpha Centauri, the brightest star in the southern constellation of the Centaur, at the Cape of Good Hope in 1832–33, but did not analyse the data until he returned to Scotland; he published a parallax of about one second of arc in early 1839. [8] At Dorpat (now Tartu), Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve had measured Vega and published a value of about an eighth of a second of arc in 1837, later revising it. [1]

8. How Parallax Works

The parallax angle p is defined as half the yearly swing: the angle that the radius of the Earth’s orbit, one astronomical unit (AU), would cover as seen from the star. Because the angle is tiny, the distance is simply the baseline divided by the angle in radians:

$$d = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{p\ (\text{in radians})}.$$

Astronomers measure small angles in seconds of arc (″): a degree has 3,600 of them, and a radian has 206,265. A star with a parallax of exactly one second of arc is therefore 206,265 AU away. That distance is called a parsec (from parallax second), and it makes the formula beautifully simple:

$$d\ (\text{parsecs}) = \frac{1}{p\ (\text{seconds of arc})}.$$

Bessel’s 0.3136″ gives 3.19 parsecs, or about 658,000 AU. One second of arc is the width of a 2 cm coin seen from about 4 kilometres; 61 Cygni’s parallax is that coin seen from 13 kilometres.

9. Light-Years and Parsecs

Distances to stars are so large that kilometres are useless. Two units are common. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.46 trillion kilometres, or 63,000 AU. A parsec is about 3.26 light-years, or 30.9 trillion kilometres. Popular writing tends to use light-years, which carry a sense of time: we see 61 Cygni as it was about eleven years ago. Professional astronomers mostly use parsecs, kiloparsecs (thousands) and megaparsecs (millions), because they come straight from the measurement.

The scale is hard to grasp. If the Sun were a grain of sand a millimetre across, the Earth would be about ten centimetres away, and the nearest star another grain of sand about 30 kilometres away. Between them is almost nothing.

10. Our Nearest Neighbours

Alpha Centauri turned out to be a double star whose two bright components orbit each other every 80 years, at a distance of about 4.4 light-years. In 1915 Robert Innes, director of the Union Observatory in Johannesburg, found a faint red star about two degrees away sharing its proper motion. [9] Its parallax turned out to be slightly larger than Alpha Centauri’s, making it the nearest star to the Sun, and it was named Proxima Centauri. Its modern parallax is 0.768″, a distance of 1.30 parsecs or 4.25 light-years. [10]

Proxima is a red dwarf, far too faint to see with the naked eye, and red dwarfs are the most common kind of star. Most of our neighbours within ten parsecs are faint red dwarfs, and many have only been found in the last century. In 2016, astronomers announced a planet orbiting Proxima at a distance where liquid water could exist on its surface.

11. From Photographic Plates to Satellites

After 1838, parallaxes came slowly: each needed months of painstaking visual measurement. From the early twentieth century, Frank Schlesinger and others photographed star fields through long telescopes and measured the plates under microscopes, and the number of known parallaxes grew to several thousand by the late twentieth century. [1] But the atmosphere blurs and shifts star images, and few ground-based parallaxes were better than about 0.01″, limiting good distances to a few tens of parsecs.

The answer was to go above the atmosphere. The European Space Agency’s Hipparcos satellite (launched in 1989, operating until 1993) scanned the whole sky with two fields of view at once, measuring angles between stars far apart in the sky. Its catalogue, published in 1997, gave parallaxes for about 118,000 stars with uncertainties of about a thousandth of a second of arc (one milliarcsecond). [11] It was a hundred times better than the ground and reached stars several hundred light-years away.

12. Gaia: A Billion Stars in Three Dimensions

Its successor, Gaia, launched in December 2013 to a point 1.5 million kilometres from the Earth, spinning slowly and sweeping its two telescopes across the sky for more than ten years. [12] Its third data release, in 2022, contained about 1.8 billion objects, about 1.5 billion of them with parallaxes and proper motions. For bright stars the uncertainty is a few hundredths of a milliarcsecond, the width of a coin on the Moon; for the faintest it is about a milliarcsecond. [10] Gaia finished its observations in early 2025, and further data releases are planned.

Gaia has turned the Galaxy from a picture into a map. Astronomers can see the shape of the spiral arms around us, streams of stars torn from small galaxies swallowed long ago, and the precise brightness of stars of known distance, which is the basis for measuring everything farther away (Part 9).

14. Try It Yourself: Parallax with Your Thumb

Try it yourself: measure a distance with your eyes
  1. Hold your thumb up at arm’s length in front of a distant object, such as a door frame across the room. Close one eye, then the other. Your thumb jumps against the background.
  2. Bring your thumb halfway towards your face and repeat. The jump is about twice as large.
  3. To measure it: ask a friend to stand against the far wall and mark where your thumb appears with each eye. Measure the gap between the marks (g), the distance from your eyes to the wall (D) and the distance between your pupils (b, about 6 cm). By similar triangles, your thumb is at a distance of about b × D ÷ (g + b). Check with a tape measure.
  4. Repeat outdoors with a pole and a distant tree, using two viewpoints a few metres apart as your “eyes”. Astronomers do exactly this, with viewpoints 300 million kilometres apart.

15. How Did They Know? Measuring a Third of a Second of Arc

How did they know? Bessel’s heliometer

Measuring the absolute position of a star to a fraction of a second of arc was impossible in 1838: the atmosphere bends starlight, the instrument flexes with temperature, and the Earth itself wobbles. Bessel avoided almost all of this by measuring relative positions: the angle between 61 Cygni and two faint stars nearby in the sky, which he assumed to be much farther away. Refraction, aberration and instrument errors affect all three stars almost equally and cancel out. [7]

His instrument, a heliometer made by Joseph von Fraunhofer’s workshop, had its main lens sawn in half. Sliding one half along the cut made two images of each star. Bessel moved the halves until the image of 61 Cygni from one half lay exactly on the image of a comparison star from the other, and read the separation from a precise screw. Repeating this night after night, he could see the separations swing back and forth once a year by a few tenths of a second of arc.

He then did something modern: he estimated his random errors from the scatter of his measurements and reported the parallax with its uncertainty. His use of probability to judge the reliability of a measurement, which built on Gauss’s method of least squares, is a large part of why his result was believed while earlier claims were not.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who measured the first star?

The legend: Bessel was the first person to measure the distance to a star.

The record: three astronomers did it within about two years. Henderson made his observations of Alpha Centauri in 1832–33, before Bessel started on 61 Cygni, but published only in 1839. Struve published a parallax for Vega in 1837, before Bessel, but based on few measurements, and his later revision made it less convincing. Bessel was the first to publish a result that astronomers found convincing, because of his careful method and stated uncertainty, and he is generally given priority for that reason. [1, 8]

A second legend: Bradley discovered aberration while sailing on the Thames, when he noticed that the wind vane on the mast changed direction every time the boat turned, although the wind had not changed. The story was told by later writers and is plausible, since it illustrates the idea perfectly, but Bradley’s own 1729 paper gives the reasoning without the boat. [5]

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: when nothing is something

For two thousand years, the failure to see stellar parallax was evidence against the Earth’s motion, and Tycho was right to take it seriously. A theory that predicts a shift, combined with an observation of no shift, should lower our confidence in the theory, unless we add an extra assumption, such as that the stars are extremely far away. Philosophers call such extra assumptions auxiliary hypotheses, and they are sometimes condemned as ways of rescuing a theory from refutation.

Yet here the auxiliary hypothesis was true, and the theory was right. What made the difference was that the hypothesis could itself be tested: it predicted that more precise instruments would eventually see a very small shift. A null result is never quite nothing: Tycho’s failure was a measurement, a lower limit on the distance to the stars. Every improvement turned the limit into a sharper one until, in 1838, the limit became a number.

Is it ever rational to believe a theory whose key prediction has not been seen? What would have made you a Copernican in 1600?
