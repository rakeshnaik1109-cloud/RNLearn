Band Theory: Why Silicon Is a Semiconductor

Copper conducts 10²⁰ times better than glass, and silicon sits in between, controllable by a trace of impurity. Quantum mechanics explains why, and that explanation is the foundation of every transistor.

At a glance

In this part: why silicon is a semiconductor. Electrons in a crystal behave as waves; their allowed energies form bands separated by gaps; whether bands are full or empty decides between metal, insulator and semiconductor. We meet holes, the intrinsic carrier concentration, doping with phosphorus and boron, and the Fermi level that ties it all together.

You need: Part 4 (current, conductivity σ = nqμ). Some familiarity with energy in electronvolts helps.

Try: four interactive figures, including a chain of atoms whose levels merge into bands, and five worked problems.

1. Conductors, Insulators and the Puzzle in Between

Copper conducts about 1020 times better than glass. Between them lies a strange family of materials, the semiconductors, whose conductivity rises with temperature (the opposite of metals), changes when light falls on them, and can be altered a million-fold by traces of impurity. In 1874 Ferdinand Braun found that a metal point pressed on a lead sulphide crystal conducted better in one direction than the other, [1] and crystal detectors built on this effect served as early radio receivers, but nobody could explain them. The explanation came only with quantum mechanics, and it is the foundation of every transistor. [2, 3]

3. Electrons as Waves in a Crystal

Drude’s electron gas (Part 4) explained Ohm’s law but failed badly elsewhere: it could not say why some solids are insulators, and it predicted the wrong heat capacity. Two quantum ideas fixed it. First, in 1925 Wolfgang Pauli proposed the exclusion principle: no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state. [4] Electrons therefore fill the available states one by one from the bottom, like water filling a jug, a filling described by Fermi–Dirac statistics. [5, 6]

Second, an electron is a wave. In 1928 Felix Bloch showed that an electron wave can travel through a perfectly periodic crystal without scattering at all; resistance comes only from imperfections and vibrations of the lattice. [7] But not every energy is allowed: waves whose wavelength matches the spacing of the atoms are reflected back and forth by the lattice, like light in a multilayer mirror, and cannot travel. These forbidden energies are the band gaps.

4. From Levels to Bands

Another way to see bands: start with isolated atoms, each with sharp energy levels. Bring two atoms together and each level splits into two, a lower bonding and a higher antibonding state. Bring N atoms together and each level splits into N. With about 5 × 1022 atoms per cubic centimetre, the levels are so close that they form continuous bands, separated by gaps where no states exist. The closer the atoms, the more their wavefunctions overlap and the wider the bands; if bands become wide enough, they overlap and the gap closes.

5. Metals, Insulators and Semiconductors

In 1931 Alan Wilson used band theory to explain the three kinds of solid. [8] The key insight is that a completely full band carries no current: for every electron moving one way there is another moving the opposite way, and there are no empty states to shift into. So:

  • Metals have a partly filled band (or overlapping bands). Electrons at the top of the filling have empty states just above them and respond to the slightest field.
  • Insulators have a full valence band and an empty conduction band separated by a large gap: about 5.5 eV for diamond and about 9 eV for silicon dioxide.
  • Semiconductors have the same arrangement with a small gap: 1.12 eV for silicon, 0.66 eV for germanium and 1.42 eV for gallium arsenide at room temperature. Thermal energy (kT ≈ 0.026 eV at 300 K) is small compared with these gaps but, because of the vast number of electrons, it lifts a few across. [3]

The gap also sets how a semiconductor interacts with light: a photon can lift an electron across the gap only if its energy hc/λ exceeds Eg. Silicon absorbs visible light, which is why solar cells and camera sensors are made of it, but is transparent beyond about 1.1 µm in the infrared.

6. Electrons and Holes

When an electron is lifted into the conduction band it leaves behind an empty state in the valence band. A neighbouring electron can move into that vacancy, leaving a vacancy behind it, and so on: the vacancy moves through the crystal like a bubble through water. It behaves exactly like a particle with positive charge +e and is called a hole. A semiconductor therefore has two kinds of carrier, electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band, and both carry current.

In a pure (intrinsic) crystal, electrons and holes are created in pairs, so their densities are equal: n = p = ni. The intrinsic concentration depends steeply on the gap and the temperature, ni ∝ T3/2 e−Eg/2kT. For silicon at 300 K, ni ≈ 1 × 1010 cm−3: about one free electron per 5 × 1012 atoms. [9] Pure silicon is therefore a poor conductor, and the conductivity rises rapidly with temperature, the signature of a semiconductor.

7. Doping: Controlling Conductivity

Silicon sits in group IV of the periodic table: each atom shares its four outer electrons in four covalent bonds with its neighbours. Replace one silicon atom by phosphorus (group V, five outer electrons) and four electrons form bonds while the fifth is held only weakly, by about 0.045 eV. At room temperature it is set free into the conduction band: phosphorus is a donor, and silicon dominated by donors is n-type. Replace a silicon atom by boron (group III, three electrons) and one bond is left incomplete; it readily captures an electron from the valence band, creating a hole. Boron is an acceptor, making p-type silicon. [10]

In thermal equilibrium the product of electron and hole densities is fixed, np = ni² (the law of mass action). So doping silicon with 1016 phosphorus atoms per cm³, one per five million silicon atoms, gives n ≈ 1016 and p ≈ 104 cm−3: electrons are the majority carriers and holes the minority carriers.

Doping only works if the crystal is otherwise extremely pure. In 1940 Russell Ohl at Bell Labs found a silicon rod that produced a voltage when light fell on it; impurities segregated during freezing had made one end p-type and the other n-type, creating the first p–n junction (Part 6). [11] William Pfann’s zone refining, described in 1952, swept impurities out of germanium to below one part in a billion and made reliable devices possible. [12, 13]

8. The Fermi Level

The probability that a state at energy E holds an electron is given by the Fermi–Dirac distribution, f(E) = 1/(1 + e(E − EF)/kT), where EF is the Fermi level: the energy at which a state has a 50% chance of being filled. In a pure semiconductor EF lies near the middle of the gap. Donors push it up towards the conduction band, by EF − Ei = kT ln(n/ni); acceptors push it down.

The Fermi level is the single most useful idea for understanding devices. In equilibrium it is flat throughout any connected system, like the water level in connected tanks. When p-type and n-type silicon touch, or when a gate voltage is applied to a transistor, the bands bend so that EF stays flat, and that bending is what creates the barriers and channels of the next three parts. [3]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): which light does silicon absorb?

Silicon’s band gap is 1.12 eV. What is the longest wavelength of light it can absorb by lifting electrons across the gap? (Use hc ≈ 1240 eV nm.)

Solution

λmax = hc/Eg = 1240/1.12 ≈ 1,110 nm, in the near infrared. All visible light (400–700 nm) is absorbed, which is why silicon looks dark and makes good solar cells; telecom light at 1,550 nm passes straight through, which is useful for silicon photonics (Part 14).

Problem 2 (easy): carriers in doped silicon

Silicon is doped with 1016 phosphorus atoms per cm³. With ni = 1010 cm−3, find the electron and hole concentrations at room temperature.

Solution

Every donor is ionised and ND ≫ ni, so n ≈ 1016 cm−3. By mass action, p = ni²/n = 1020/1016 = 104 cm−3. One impurity atom per five million silicon atoms changed the electron density a million-fold and the hole density a million-fold the other way.

Problem 3 (medium): resistivity

Using μn = 1,400 and μp = 450 cm²/(V s), compare the resistivity of pure silicon with that of the doped sample in Problem 2.

Solution

ρ = 1/[q(nμn + pμp)]. Pure: ρ = 1/[1.6 × 10−19 × 1010 × 1,850] ≈ 3.4 × 105 Ω cm. Doped: ρ ≈ 1/[1.6 × 10−19 × 1016 × 1,400] ≈ 0.45 Ω cm, about 750,000 times lower. (Copper is about 1.7 × 10−6 Ω cm.)

Problem 4 (medium): where is the Fermi level?

For the sample in Problem 2, how far above mid-gap is the Fermi level at 300 K (kT = 0.0259 eV)? In pure silicon, with EF at mid-gap, what is the occupation probability of a state at the conduction-band edge, 0.56 eV higher?

Solution

EF − Ei = kT ln(n/ni) = 0.0259 × ln(106) = 0.0259 × 13.8 ≈ 0.36 eV, about two-thirds of the way from mid-gap to the conduction band. For pure silicon, f ≈ e−0.56/0.0259 = e−21.6 ≈ 4 × 10−10: tiny, but multiplied by about 3 × 1019 states per cm³ near the band edge it gives the 1010 electrons per cm³ found above.

Problem 5 (harder): a hot chip

Estimate by what factor silicon’s intrinsic carrier concentration rises between 300 K and 400 K (127 °C), using ni ∝ T3/2 e−Eg/2kT with Eg = 1.12 eV held constant. Why does this matter for a device doped at 1014 cm−3?

Solution

Ratio = (400/300)3/2 × exp[(Eg/2k)(1/300 − 1/400)] = 1.54 × exp[(1.12/(2 × 8.617 × 10−5)) × 8.33 × 10−4] = 1.54 × e5.42 ≈ 1.54 × 225 ≈ 350. (The gap shrinks slightly with temperature, making it larger still, about 400–500.) So ni rises from 1010 to about 4 × 1012 cm−3, still below 1014, but minority-carrier densities (ni²/N) rise 100,000-fold, and leakage currents in junctions, which depend on them, grow enormously. That is one reason chips have a maximum operating temperature.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: beyond simple bands

Band theory treats each electron as moving independently in an average field from the others. It works superbly for silicon, but it fails for some materials where electrons interact strongly: certain oxides predicted to be metals are in fact insulators (Mott insulators), and high-temperature superconductors emerge from such materials. Predicting band gaps accurately from first principles is still hard: the standard computational method, density functional theory, systematically underestimates gaps, and more accurate methods are expensive. Designing new semiconductors for faster or more efficient devices by computer therefore remains an active research field.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “holes are just an accounting trick”

The myth: only electrons really move; “holes” are a bookkeeping fiction.

The reality: holes behave as genuine positive particles in experiments. In the Hall effect, a magnetic field deflects carriers sideways; in p-type material the sideways voltage has the opposite sign to that in metals and n-type material, exactly as for positive charges. Holes have their own effective mass and mobility, and devices such as p-channel transistors rely on them.

Why it matters: describing a nearly full band by its few empty states is not only convenient; it is the physically correct way to predict how the band responds.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Electrons in crystals are waves; their allowed energies form bands separated by forbidden gaps.
  • Full bands carry no current: metals have partly full bands; insulators and semiconductors have a full valence band and an empty conduction band separated by a gap (silicon: 1.12 eV).
  • Heat lifts a few electrons across the gap, creating electron–hole pairs; ni ∝ T3/2 e−Eg/2kT, about 1010 cm−3 for silicon at 300 K.
  • Donors (phosphorus) make n-type silicon; acceptors (boron) make p-type; np = ni².
  • The Fermi level marks 50% occupation; doping moves it, and in equilibrium it is flat everywhere.
