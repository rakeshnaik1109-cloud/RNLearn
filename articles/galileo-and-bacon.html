Galileo and Francis Bacon: Should We Observe or Reason?

One measured rolling balls and turned a spyglass on the sky; the other never made a great discovery but taught science how to organise itself. Together they replaced Aristotle, and one of them paid dearly for it.

1. Observe or Reason?

In the early seventeenth century, two men set out, almost simultaneously and in very different ways, to replace the science of Aristotle that had dominated European universities for four hundred years. Galileo Galilei, a mathematician in Padua and Florence, turned a new instrument on the sky and measured the motion of balls on slopes, insisting that nature is written in the language of mathematics. Francis Bacon, an English lawyer and statesman, never made a major discovery, but argued that knowledge must be rebuilt from the ground up by patient, organised collection of observations, freed from the prejudices of the mind. [1, 2]

Between them they framed a question that still divides scientists and philosophers: does science advance mainly by bold mathematical ideas tested by experiment, or by careful accumulation of facts from which general laws emerge? Both men also discovered how dangerous a new idea can be.

3. Copernicus and the Problem of a Moving Earth

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, placing the Sun near the centre of the universe and making the Earth a planet that spins daily and orbits yearly. [3] His system explained naturally why planets sometimes appear to move backwards, and fixed the order and relative distances of the planets. But it was no more accurate than Ptolemy’s, it still needed circles upon circles, and it ran into the objections Aryabhata had met a thousand years earlier (Part 3): why do we not feel the motion, why do falling stones not lag behind, and why do the stars show no annual shift? Aristotle’s physics, built on a still Earth (Part 1), seemed to rule it out. To make a moving Earth believable, someone had to provide new evidence and a new physics. Galileo tried to provide both.

4. Galileo, Professor in Padua

Galileo was born in Pisa in 1564, the son of a musician and music theorist, Vincenzo Galilei, who had tested the mathematics of musical strings by experiment. He abandoned medical studies for mathematics, taught at Pisa, and from 1592 to 1610 was professor of mathematics at the University of Padua, in the Republic of Venice, which he later called the happiest years of his life. [4] There he worked on motion, designed instruments, and supplemented his salary by selling a calculating “military compass” and taking in student lodgers.

5. Falling Bodies and the Inclined Plane

Aristotle taught that heavier bodies fall faster and that falling speed depends on the resistance of the medium (Part 1). Galileo, building on critics going back to John Philoponus in the sixth century, argued instead that in a vacuum all bodies would fall together, and that a falling body speeds up uniformly. Free fall is too fast to time with the clocks of 1600, so he “diluted” gravity by rolling bronze balls down a long, polished, gently sloping groove, timing them by weighing the water that ran from a vessel while the ball rolled. [5] He found that the distance covered grows as the square of the time: in successive equal intervals, a ball covers distances in the ratio of the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7… He had stated the rule in a letter to his friend Paolo Sarpi in 1604, and his surviving working notes show measurements behind it. [4]

Historians once doubted that such crude equipment could give the results Galileo claimed, suspecting the experiments were merely “thought experiments”. In 1961 Thomas Settle rebuilt the apparatus and showed that it works as described. [6]

6. Projectiles and the Relativity of Motion

Galileo also realised that a horizontal motion, once started, would continue unchanged on a perfectly smooth level surface, a first version of the principle of inertia. Combining steady horizontal motion with uniformly accelerated fall, he showed that a projectile follows a parabola, a result published in his last book, Two New Sciences (1638). [5]

The same idea answered the objections to a moving Earth. In his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632), Galileo asked readers to imagine themselves shut in a cabin below deck on a ship moving smoothly: butterflies fly equally in all directions, drips from a bottle fall straight down, and nothing reveals whether the ship is moving or at rest. A stone dropped from a ship’s mast lands at its foot, because it shares the ship’s motion. Likewise, falling stones share the Earth’s motion, and we cannot feel it. [7] This principle of relativity became a foundation of physics, and Einstein would extend it three centuries later (Part 10).

7. The Telescope and the Starry Messenger

In 1608 spectacle makers in the Netherlands produced a simple telescope. Hearing of it in 1609, Galileo worked out its principle and built better ones, reaching about twenty times magnification by the end of the year. [8] Turning it to the sky, he saw what no one had seen before, and in March 1610 published his findings in a short book, Sidereus Nuncius (The Starry Messenger): [9]

  • The Moon is not a perfect sphere but rough, with mountains and craters, like the Earth.
  • The Milky Way and nebulous patches are made of countless faint stars.
  • Four “stars” accompany Jupiter, changing position night by night: moons orbiting another planet, which he named the Medicean stars after his hoped-for patrons, the Medici.

Jupiter’s moons showed that not everything orbits the Earth, and that a moving planet can keep its moons, answering the objection that a moving Earth would leave its Moon behind. The book made Galileo famous, and it won him a position in Florence as mathematician and philosopher to the Grand Duke of Tuscany.

8. The Phases of Venus: A Decisive Observation?

In late 1610 Galileo observed that Venus shows phases like the Moon, from a small, nearly full disc to a large thin crescent. In Ptolemy’s arrangement, Venus always lies between the Earth and the Sun, so it could show only crescents; a full disc requires Venus to pass behind the Sun. The observation therefore refuted Ptolemy’s system. [4]

But it did not prove Copernicus right. The Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe had proposed a compromise in 1588: the planets orbit the Sun, while the Sun and Moon orbit a stationary Earth. Tycho’s system predicts exactly the same phases of Venus, and many Jesuit astronomers adopted it. Observation had narrowed the options, but could not yet choose between them.

9. Francis Bacon and the New Organon

Francis Bacon was born in London in 1561, the son of a senior official of Elizabeth I. A lawyer and politician, he rose under James I to become Lord Chancellor in 1618, before being impeached for accepting gifts from litigants in 1621 and driven from public life. [2] Throughout his career he planned a “Great Instauration”, a complete renewal of human knowledge. Its centrepiece, Novum Organum (The New Organon, 1620), was named to replace Aristotle’s logical works, collectively called the Organon (“instrument”). [10]

Bacon argued that the mind is full of distorting habits, which he called idols:

  • Idols of the tribe: errors common to all humans, such as seeing more order and pattern than exists, and noticing confirming cases while ignoring contrary ones.
  • Idols of the cave: individual biases from temperament, education and favourite ideas.
  • Idols of the marketplace: confusion caused by words that are vague or name things that do not exist.
  • Idols of the theatre: the grip of received philosophical systems, which he compared to stage plays.

Against these, he proposed a new kind of induction. Not hasty generalisation from a few cases, which he despised, but slow and systematic work: compile natural histories of facts and experiments, arrange them in tables, and climb step by step from particular observations to modest general laws, and only then to broad ones. [10]

10. Bacon’s Tables in Action

Bacon illustrated his method with heat. First, a table of instances where heat is present (the Sun’s rays, fire, friction, fermenting dung); then a table of similar instances where it is absent (moonlight, which is light from the heavens without warmth); then a table of degrees, where heat varies. Any proposed cause, or “form”, that is absent where heat is present, or present where heat is absent, must be rejected. Bacon stressed that eliminating false candidates is more powerful than accumulating confirming cases: one contrary instance can refute a claim that a thousand agreeing instances cannot prove. His tentative conclusion, his “first vintage”, was that heat is a kind of expansive, restrained motion of the small parts of bodies, a remarkable anticipation of the kinetic theory of heat. [10]

11. The Trial of Galileo

Galileo’s advocacy of Copernicus brought him into conflict with the Church. In 1616, after theologians judged the Sun-centred system contrary to Scripture, the Congregation of the Index suspended Copernicus’ book “until corrected”, and Cardinal Robert Bellarmine instructed Galileo not to hold or defend it. [11] When his admirer Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII, Galileo believed he could write about the question as a hypothesis. His Dialogue of 1632 presented the arguments as a conversation, but made the Copernican case overwhelmingly, and put the Pope’s favourite argument (that God could produce the same effects in ways beyond our understanding) into the mouth of the simple-minded character Simplicio.

In 1633 the Inquisition tried him for “vehement suspicion of heresy”. Aged 69, under threat of torture, he abjured, declaring that he abandoned the opinion that the Earth moves. He spent his last eight years under house arrest at his villa in Arcetri, near Florence, where, going blind, he completed Two New Sciences, smuggled out and published in Leiden in 1638. He died in 1642. [11, 1] In 1992 Pope John Paul II formally acknowledged that the theologians who condemned him had erred.

Galileo was not always right: he dismissed Kepler’s elliptical orbits and argued, wrongly, that the tides proved the Earth’s motion. And his evidence, strong as it was, was not yet conclusive; Bellarmine had said he would reconsider if a real demonstration of the Earth’s motion were found. The affair was as much about authority, politics and personality as about science versus religion.

12. Two Visions of the New Science

Galileo and Bacon both rejected Aristotle and both appealed to experience, yet their visions differed sharply. In The Assayer (1623) Galileo wrote that the universe “cannot be understood unless one first learns to comprehend the language and interpret the characters in which it is written. It is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures.” [12] His method was to idealise, imagining frictionless planes and motion in a vacuum, to deduce mathematical consequences, and to design experiments that tested them, following Archimedes (Part 2).

Bacon distrusted such bold leaps and gave mathematics only a supporting role; he was unconvinced by Copernicus and criticised William Gilbert, the pioneer of magnetism, for building a grand philosophy on a few experiments. William Harvey, discoverer of the circulation of the blood, reportedly said that Bacon wrote philosophy “like a Lord Chancellor”. [13] Yet Bacon grasped something Galileo underplayed: science as a collective, organised, publicly useful enterprise. In his utopia New Atlantis (1627) he imagined “Salomon’s House”, an institute of researchers dividing the labour of experiment. And he gave the new science its slogan: “knowledge itself is power”.

13. The Legacy

The two visions merged. When the Royal Society of London was founded in 1660, its members consciously took Bacon as their prophet, collecting experiments and observations and publishing them for all to check; Thomas Sprat’s 1667 history of the Society presented it as the realisation of Bacon’s plan. [14] But its greatest member, Isaac Newton, born in the year Galileo died, combined Baconian experiment with Galilean mathematics in a way that neither had achieved (Part 6). Modern science still uses both: hypotheses expressed mathematically, and systematic, often collaborative, gathering of data designed to eliminate errors.

14. Try It Yourself: Galileo’s and Bacon’s Methods

Try it yourself: measure like Galileo, tabulate like Bacon
  1. Inclined plane: raise one end of a long plank or curtain rail by a few centimetres. Mark where a marble is after 1, 2 and 3 beats of a metronome app. Are the distances in the ratio 1 : 4 : 9?
  2. Falling together: drop a coin and a flat piece of paper; then crumple the paper into a tight ball and drop them again. What does this tell you about Aristotle, Galileo and air resistance?
  3. Telescope: with binoculars held steady against a wall, look at Jupiter. Can you see some of the four moons Galileo saw? Sketch their positions on several nights.
  4. Bacon’s tables: pick a question such as “what makes bread go mouldy fastest?” Draw up tables of presence, absence and degree, and design the observations that would eliminate wrong answers.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: the Leaning Tower of Pisa

The legend: Galileo dropped balls of different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa before an audience of professors and students, proving that they fall together and humiliating the followers of Aristotle.

The record: the story comes from Vincenzo Viviani, Galileo’s last pupil, in a biography written in 1654, more than sixty years after the supposed event, and Galileo never mentions it in his own works. [15] Galileo did discuss dropping weights from towers, and noted that a heavy and a light ball do not land quite together because of air resistance. A documented experiment was made in 1586 by Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot, who dropped two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, from a church tower in Delft and heard them hit a board together.

The grain of truth: Galileo did reject Aristotle’s claim that speed of fall is proportional to weight, using both arguments and experiments, and he may well have dropped things from the tower in Pisa, where he taught. But the decisive evidence came from the inclined plane, not from a public spectacle.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: true description, or useful hypothesis?

In 1615 Cardinal Bellarmine wrote to the friar Paolo Antonio Foscarini that it was perfectly acceptable to say that the Sun-centred system “saves the appearances” better than Ptolemy’s, as a mathematical hypothesis, but dangerous to claim that the Earth really moves. [11] Galileo insisted on the stronger claim. Their disagreement anticipates a lasting debate between instrumentalism, the view that scientific theories are tools for prediction whose truth need not be asked, and scientific realism, the view that our best theories describe what really exists. The physicist and historian Pierre Duhem later argued that Bellarmine had the better logic, because observations can never strictly prove a hypothesis. [16]

Bacon’s method faces its own problem. However many hot instances share motion, can we be sure the next one will? Hume would later argue that no amount of experience logically guarantees a general law (Part 7).

Questions to think about: If Tycho’s and Copernicus’ systems predicted the same observations, was it rational in 1620 to believe the Earth moves? Is a theory that predicts perfectly but is “not true” still science?
