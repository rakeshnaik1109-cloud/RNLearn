David Hume and Immanuel Kant: Can We Ever Know That Causes Cause?

A genial Scot argued that our belief in cause and effect is only habit. A Prussian professor replied that the mind itself builds space, time and causation into every experience. Science still lives between their answers.

1. Can We Know That Causes Cause?

By the eighteenth century, Newton’s physics (Part 6) seemed to have shown that nature runs by fixed laws that reason and experience can discover. Then a cheerful Scottish philosopher asked a question that has never been fully answered. We see the Sun rise every morning and expect it to rise tomorrow; we see one billiard ball strike another and expect the second to move. But what entitles us to these expectations? David Hume argued that neither logic nor experience can justify them. They rest on habit. [1]

Immanuel Kant, a professor in distant Königsberg, said that reading Hume woke him from his “dogmatic slumber”. His answer, that the mind itself supplies the framework of space, time and causation within which experience is possible, became one of the most influential ideas in philosophy. Between them, Hume and Kant defined the problem of how science can know anything general at all. [2]

3. David Hume: An Edinburgh Sceptic

David Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711 and studied at the university there from about the age of ten. Intended for the law, he found himself instead consumed by philosophy, worked so intensely that he suffered a breakdown, and spent three years in France writing his first book. He was twice refused a university chair because of his reputation for religious scepticism, and earned his living as a librarian, secretary and diplomat. His six-volume History of England made him famous and comfortable. Friends called him “le bon David”: genial, witty and generous. [1]

4. Impressions, Ideas and Hume’s Fork

Hume’s A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), written in his twenties, set out to build a “science of man” on Newtonian lines: observation of how the mind actually works. All the contents of the mind, he argued, are either impressions (vivid perceptions and feelings) or ideas (fainter copies of impressions). Any idea that cannot be traced to an impression is empty. [3] The book, he wrote later, “fell dead-born from the press”, so he restated its arguments in the shorter Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748). [4]

In the Enquiry he divides all knowledge into two kinds, a division now called Hume’s fork:

  • Relations of ideas, such as the truths of mathematics, which are certain because denying them is a contradiction, but tell us nothing about what exists.
  • Matters of fact, such as “the Sun will rise tomorrow”, which are informative but can be denied without contradiction, and can be known only through experience.

He ends with a famous challenge to any book that contains neither abstract reasoning about quantity nor experimental reasoning about matters of fact: “Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion.” [4]

5. Causation: Constant Conjunction and Habit

Where does our idea of cause come from? Watch one billiard ball strike another. We see the first ball move, contact, and the second ball move. We do not see any power or necessity passing between them. If we had never seen such collisions, nothing in the first ball’s motion would tell us what would happen; the second ball might as well stop, or fly off sideways. All experience gives us is constant conjunction: events of one kind regularly followed by events of another. After many repetitions, the mind forms a habit of expecting the second when it sees the first, and this felt expectation is the only source of our idea of “necessary connection”. [3, 4]

This does not mean causes are unreal, only that our knowledge of them rests on observed regularities, not on insight into hidden powers. The practical problem it highlights, distinguishing genuine causes from mere correlations, is central to modern science, from medicine (Part 4) to economics. [5]

6. The Problem of Induction

Hume then asked what justifies induction: inferring from observed cases to unobserved ones, from “all observed swans are white” to “all swans are white”, or from past sunrises to tomorrow’s. Logic cannot justify it, since it is not a contradiction to suppose the future will differ from the past. Experience cannot justify it either: saying “induction has always worked before” is itself an inductive argument, so the justification is circular. Our reliance on induction, Hume concluded, is a matter of custom, not reason; yet nature has wisely ensured that we cannot help relying on it. [4]

Two responses followed quickly. In 1763 Richard Price published his late friend Thomas Bayes’s essay on how to update probabilities in the light of evidence, presenting it partly as an answer to Hume; Pierre-Simon Laplace later developed the method and applied it, notoriously, to the probability of the Sun rising. [6, 7] Probability can tell us how confident to be given assumptions about the world, but cannot prove those assumptions. In 1912 Bertrand Russell illustrated the point with a chicken (often retold as a turkey) that is fed every day and expects to be fed again, until the day its neck is wrung. [8]

7. Immanuel Kant: The Professor of Königsberg

Immanuel Kant was born in 1724 in Königsberg, a Baltic port in East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia), the son of a harness maker, and was raised in a strict Pietist household. He studied at the local university, worked for years as a private tutor and then as an unsalaried lecturer paid by students’ fees, teaching everything from logic to physical geography and fireworks, and became professor of logic and metaphysics only at 46. He never travelled more than about a hundred kilometres from his home town. [2] His early work was largely in natural science.

8. Kant the Scientist: The Nebular Hypothesis

In 1755, in his Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens, Kant proposed that the Solar System formed from a vast rotating cloud of particles that collapsed under Newtonian gravity, flattening into a disc in which the planets formed. This explained why the planets orbit in nearly the same plane and in the same direction. He also proposed that the Milky Way is a flattened disc of stars seen from inside, and that certain faint nebulae are other such systems, “island universes”, far beyond it. [9] Laplace independently proposed a nebular origin in 1796, and in the 1920s Edwin Hubble confirmed that many nebulae are distant galaxies. Today we photograph discs of gas and dust around young stars, where planets are forming.

9. The Critique of Pure Reason

Kant took Hume’s challenge seriously. Newton’s physics seemed to give universal, necessary knowledge of nature; if Hume were right, it could be no more than habit. In the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) Kant asked how such knowledge is possible. [10] He distinguished judgements that are analytic (true by the meaning of their terms, like “all bachelors are unmarried”) from synthetic ones (which add information), and knowledge that is a priori (independent of experience) from knowledge that is a posteriori (dependent on it). Hume had assumed that everything a priori is analytic. Kant argued that there are synthetic a priori truths: informative yet certain, such as the truths of geometry, “7 + 5 = 12”, and the principle that every event has a cause.

How could we know such things in advance of experience? Kant’s answer was his “Copernican revolution” in philosophy. Instead of assuming that our knowledge must conform to objects, suppose that objects, as we experience them, must conform to the structure of our minds. Space and time are the forms of our sensibility, the framework in which anything at all can appear to us; causation is one of the categories by which our understanding organises experience. We can know a priori that every experienced event has a cause, because nothing could count as an experience of an objective event without being ordered by cause and effect. The price was that we can know things only as they appear to us (phenomena), never as they are in themselves (noumena). [10, 11]

10. Kant’s Geometry Meets Curved Space

Kant’s claim that Euclidean geometry is a necessary form of all experience suffered a blow within decades of his death, when mathematicians showed that non-Euclidean geometries are logically consistent (Part 2). Defenders of Kant replied that he meant only the geometry of our perception, not of mathematics; but in 1915 Einstein’s general relativity described physical space itself as non-Euclidean (Part 10). Many philosophers concluded that geometry is either pure mathematics (analytic, in a sense) or physics (empirical), and that Kant’s synthetic a priori was a mistake. Others think his deeper idea, that knowledge always involves a framework supplied by the knower, survives. It returns in Poincaré’s conventionalism (Part 10) and in Kuhn’s paradigms (Part 12).

11. Two Deaths and a Legacy

Hume died in Edinburgh in 1776, calmly and good-humouredly, joking with friends; the economist Adam Smith wrote that he approached as nearly as possible to “the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man”, a judgement that scandalised the devout. [1] Kant lived until 1804; his later works applied his critical philosophy to ethics (the categorical imperative), aesthetics and politics, including an essay on perpetual peace that proposed a federation of nations. Hume’s empiricism inspired the logical positivists of the twentieth century; Kant’s idea of the mind’s active role shaped psychology, philosophy of science and even cognitive science, which studies the built-in structures with which brains interpret the world.

12. Living with the Problem of Induction

The problem of induction has never been “solved” to everyone’s satisfaction. Responses include probabilistic (Bayesian) accounts of how evidence raises confidence; pragmatic arguments that if any method works, induction will; and Karl Popper’s radical proposal to abandon induction altogether in favour of bold conjectures and attempted refutations (Part 12). In 1955 Nelson Goodman added a “new riddle”: invent the predicate “grue”, meaning green if observed before some future date and blue afterwards. Every emerald observed so far is both green and grue, so why do we predict green rather than grue? Which patterns we project, it seems, depends on concepts we bring to the data, a very Kantian point. [12]

13. Why It Matters for Science

Scientists rarely worry about Hume in daily work, but his questions shape practice. Because correlations do not reveal causes, medicine uses randomised controlled trials, and statisticians have developed formal tools for causal inference. [5] Because induction can fail, scientific claims are held provisionally, as Newton’s fourth rule of reasoning already required (Part 6). And because, as Kant saw, observations are always organised by concepts, scientists check that their categories, instruments and assumptions are not quietly shaping what they find.

14. Try It Yourself: Habits of Expectation

Try it yourself: Hume’s and Kant’s puzzles
  1. Constant conjunction: list three things you are sure cause one another. For each, ask: what did I actually observe? Could a hidden common cause explain it?
  2. Induction: try to justify “bread will nourish me tomorrow” without using any past experience. What happens?
  3. Curved geometry: on an orange or a ball, draw a triangle with one corner at the top and two on the “equator” a quarter of the way round. Measure the three angles with a protractor. What do they add to?
  4. Grue: invent your own grue-like word and find a pattern in data that fits both the ordinary and the strange prediction.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: Kant the human clock

The legend: Kant’s afternoon walk was so punctual that the people of Königsberg set their clocks by him, and he missed it only once, when he was absorbed in reading Rousseau’s Émile.

The record: the clock-setting story was popularised by the poet Heinrich Heine in 1834, thirty years after Kant’s death, and Heine had never known him. [2] Kant’s early biographers, who were his friends, do describe a highly regular daily routine in his later years, including a walk at the same hour, adopted partly to protect his fragile health. The Rousseau story is also told by them, though it may be embroidered.

The grain of truth: the older Kant did live by a strict routine. As a younger man, however, he was a sociable figure who played billiards and cards, dined out often and was known as “the elegant master”.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: is the order in nature found or made?

Hume found regularity in nature but no necessity, and traced our confidence to habit. Kant argued that the most basic forms of order, space, time and causation, are contributed by the mind, so that we can know them in advance, but only for the world as it appears to us. Scientific realists answer that the order is really out there, and that the success of science is the best evidence for it.

Modern physics complicates the picture: quantum events appear to happen without determining causes, and relativity shows that space and time are not the fixed stage Kant imagined. Yet the core puzzles remain: why should the world be orderly at all, and why should orderly minds be able to grasp it?

Questions to think about: Is it rational to rely on induction if it cannot be justified without circularity? Are space and time features of the world, of our minds, or of both?
