Why It Works: How Next-Word Prediction Leads to Knowledge and Reasoning

Nothing in a language model’s training mentions facts, logic or truth. Yet predicting human text well turns out to require all three, and a big enough model learns a surprising amount of them.

At a glance

In this part: the heart of the puzzle. Why does a model trained only to predict the next word end up knowing facts and following reasoning? We look at what good prediction requires; prediction as compression; scaling laws; whether abilities “emerge” suddenly; what researchers find inside models; how instruction tuning and learning from human preferences turn a text predictor into an assistant; and why writing out steps helps.

1. The Real Puzzle

Parts 1–4 explained the machinery: tokens become vectors, attention mixes in context, and a next token is drawn from a probability distribution. None of it mentions facts, logic or truth. Yet ask a large model a question about chemistry or history and it usually answers correctly. Where does that come from? The short answer is that predicting human text extremely well is a very demanding task, and the cheapest way for a big enough network to do it is to learn much of what the text is about. This part examines that answer, and its limits. [1]

3. To Predict Well, You Must Know Things

Some next words follow from grammar alone. Many do not. The next word after “The chemical symbol for gold is” requires a fact; after “17 + 25 =”, arithmetic; after a short story about a ball being moved while someone is out of the room, an idea of what that person believes. Word counts cannot supply any of these, but each is useful for predicting text, and text written by people is full of them.

Training lowers the prediction error over trillions of tokens. Every regularity that helps, from subject–verb agreement to the boiling point of water, is worth learning if the network has room for it. An early glimpse came in 2017, when Alec Radford, Rafal Jozefowicz and Ilya Sutskever trained a network only to predict the next character of product reviews and found that one of its units tracked whether the review was positive or negative: sentiment was useful for prediction, so the network had learned to represent it. [2]

4. Prediction Is Compression

There is a precise way to say “predicts better”. If a model gives the actual next word probability p, that word costs −log2 p bits to encode with the model’s help, a result going back to Shannon. [3] A word the model expected costs almost nothing; a surprising word costs many bits. A better predictor is literally a better compressor, and Grégoire Delétang and colleagues showed that large language models compress text, and even images and audio, better than standard compression programs. [4]

On the 200 test sentences of the toy world, a blind guess costs 5.3 bits per word, word frequencies 4.2, a bigram model 1.9 and a trigram 1.4. In “the hungry cat drinks the milk”, the trigram model pays 4.1 bits for “hungry”, a genuinely unpredictable choice, but only 1.9 bits for “milk” once it knows a cat is drinking. Driving the cost of real text down requires knowing what real text knows.

5. Scale: More Data, Bigger Models

How much better does prediction get with size? In 2020 Jared Kaplan and colleagues trained hundreds of transformer language models and found that their test loss fell as a power law in the number of parameters, in the amount of data and in the computation used, over many orders of magnitude, with little dependence on details such as depth versus width. [5]

On log scales the law is a straight line: every tenfold increase in parameters multiplies the loss by 0.84. Such regularity made it possible to predict a large model’s performance from small experiments before spending millions on training it. In 2022 Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues refined the recipe: for a fixed computing budget, models had been too large and trained on too little text. Their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, about 20 tokens per parameter, outperformed models four times its size. [6]

The maths: the power laws

Kaplan and colleagues fitted, for test loss L in nats per token,

$$L(N) \approx \Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N}, \quad \alpha_N \approx 0.076,\ N_c \approx 8.8\times10^{13}; \qquad L(D) \approx \Big(\frac{D_c}{D}\Big)^{\alpha_D}, \quad \alpha_D \approx 0.095,$$

where N is the number of non-embedding parameters and D the number of training tokens. Training compute is roughly C ≈ 6ND floating-point operations. A small exponent means slow but steady gains: halving the loss would take about 104 times more parameters.

6. Emergent Abilities or Smooth Progress?

Loss falls smoothly, but some abilities seemed to appear suddenly: small models scored near zero on multi-digit arithmetic or certain puzzles, and above some size, scores shot up. Jason Wei and colleagues catalogued many such emergent abilities. [7] Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda and Sanmi Koyejo then showed that many of these jumps depend on how the ability is scored. When an answer counts only if it is exactly right, small steady gains in each token’s accuracy add up to a sharp jump in the score; with a measure that gives partial credit, the same models improve smoothly. [8]

7. What Models Learn Inside

If prediction forces models to learn about the world, we should find traces of that world inside them. Researchers look with probes: simple classifiers trained to read a property from a model’s internal vectors. Kenneth Li and colleagues trained a transformer only on sequences of legal moves in the board game Othello, never showing it a board; probes could nonetheless read the state of every square from its activations, and changing those activations changed the moves it predicted. [9] Wes Gurnee and Max Tegmark found that large language models represent the geographical position of places and the dates of historical events along roughly straight directions in their activation space. [10] These are not proofs of understanding, but they show that models build structured internal pictures of what text describes, because those pictures help them predict.

8. From Text Predictor to Assistant

A model pre-trained on the internet predicts how a document would continue, not what would help you. Asked a question, it might continue with more questions, as a quiz page would. Two more stages turn it into an assistant. Instruction tuning continues training on examples of requests and good responses. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), developed by Paul Christiano and colleagues for training agents, [11] asks people to compare pairs of answers, trains a reward model to predict their choices, and then adjusts the language model to produce answers the reward model scores highly. Long Ouyang and colleagues applied this to GPT-3: people preferred the answers of a tuned model with 1.3 billion parameters to those of the original with 175 billion. [12]

Later methods replace some human judgements with written principles: in Anthropic’s Constitutional AI, a model critiques and revises its own answers according to a list of principles, and AI feedback trains the reward model. [13] None of this adds much new knowledge; it mostly changes which of the model’s possible behaviours it shows. The knowledge comes from pre-training.

9. Thinking in Tokens

Each token is produced by a fixed amount of computation: one pass through the network. A hard problem may need more steps than one pass can do. Writing out intermediate steps gives the model both more computation and a place to keep partial results. Jason Wei and colleagues found that showing a model worked examples with step-by-step reasoning, chain-of-thought prompting, greatly improved its accuracy on arithmetic word problems; [14] Takeshi Kojima and colleagues found that simply adding “Let’s think step by step” helped too. [15] Today’s reasoning models are trained to produce long chains of thought before answering, an idea taken up again in Part 6.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: is it understanding?

Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell (credited under a pseudonym) described large language models as “stochastic parrots” that stitch together linguistic forms according to probabilities, without reference to meaning, and warned of the risks of mistaking fluency for understanding. [16] Findings such as internal board states and maps of the world suggest that models build some structured representations of what text is about. [9, 10] Where the truth lies between these views, and whether “understanding” is even the right question to ask of a system so unlike a human mind, is one of the most debated issues in AI.

11. Worked Example: Reading a Scaling Law

  1. Kaplan’s law gives a loss of (8.8 × 1013 / 109)0.076 = 88,0000.076 ≈ 2.38 nats per token for a model with a billion non-embedding parameters.
  2. Ten times larger (1010): the loss is multiplied by 10−0.076 = 0.839, giving 1.99.
  3. In perplexity, e2.38 ≈ 10.8 falls to e1.99 ≈ 7.3: the model is choosing among about 7 equally likely tokens instead of 11.
  4. Chinchilla’s rule of about 20 tokens per parameter suggests training a 10-billion-parameter model on roughly 200 billion tokens, using about 6 × 1010 × 2 × 1011 = 1.2 × 1022 operations.

13. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “it is just autocomplete, so it cannot know anything”

The myth: because an LLM only predicts the next word, it is like the autocomplete on a phone and cannot contain knowledge or reasoning.

The reality: the training objective is simple, but what is needed to achieve it is not. Phone autocomplete is a small n-gram-like model; a large model reduces its prediction error on trillions of tokens by learning facts, grammar and patterns of reasoning, and probes find structured representations of games, places and times inside it. [9, 10] At the same time, the objective explains real weaknesses: the model learns what is said, which is not always what is true, the subject of Part 6.

Why it matters: “just predicting the next word” describes the interface, not the capability. Both dismissing LLMs and over-trusting them rest on misreading what next-word prediction demands.

14. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Predicting human text well requires grammar, facts, arithmetic, logic and models of the world and of people, so training pushes models to learn them.
  • Better prediction is better compression: the cost of text in bits measures how much of its structure a model has captured.
  • Loss falls as a smooth power law with parameters, data and compute; compute-optimal training uses about 20 tokens per parameter.
  • Abilities that seem to emerge suddenly often reflect all-or-nothing scoring of smoothly improving skills.
  • Probes find internal representations of board states, locations and dates: models build structured pictures because they help prediction.
  • Instruction tuning and learning from human preferences turn a text predictor into an assistant; writing out steps gives a model more computation per answer.
