The Shape of the Earth: Squashed Planets, the Metre and Mount Everest

Newton said the spinning Earth must be squashed; French surveys said it was stretched. Settling the argument took expeditions to the Arctic and the Andes, gave the world the metre, and led to the height of the highest mountain.

1. Is the Earth a Perfect Ball?

Part 1 ended in 1670 with Jean Picard's measurement of the Earth's size, accurate to about 0.1 per cent. But it assumed something nobody had checked: that the Earth is a perfect sphere. Over the next 70 years, that assumption became one of the great scientific arguments of its age, settled only by expeditions to the frozen Arctic and the high Andes. [1, 2]

The answer led to something we all use every day. When revolutionary France wanted a new unit of length "for all people, for all time", it defined the metre using the size of the Earth, and sent two astronomers to measure it. Then, in the 19th century, the same surveying methods were carried across India in one of the greatest scientific projects ever undertaken, which ended by finding the height of the world's highest mountain.

3. Squashed or Stretched? Newton against Cassini

The first clue came by accident. In 1672 the French astronomer Jean Richer travelled to Cayenne in French Guiana, near the equator, to observe Mars. He found that his pendulum clock, carefully set in Paris, lost about two and a half minutes every day. To make it keep time he had to shorten the pendulum. A pendulum swings more slowly where gravity is weaker, so gravity must be slightly weaker near the equator. [3, 2]

In 1687 Isaac Newton explained why. A spinning body is pushed outwards most strongly at its equator, so a spinning, slightly soft Earth should bulge at the equator and be flattened at the poles, like a gently squashed ball. Standing on the equatorial bulge, you are further from the Earth's centre, and the spin pushes you outwards a little, so gravity feels weaker there. Newton estimated that the Earth's diameter through the poles should be about 1 part in 230 shorter than across the equator. The Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens, using a different theory, predicted a smaller flattening of about 1 part in 578. [4, 5]

But measurements in France seemed to say the opposite. The Cassini family, who ran the Paris Observatory for four generations, extended Picard's survey north to Dunkirk and south to the Pyrenees. In 1720 Jacques Cassini announced that a degree of latitude was slightly shorter in the north of France than in the south. That meant the Earth was stretched at the poles, shaped more like a lemon or an egg, not squashed like an orange. It became a national argument: French Cartesians, who believed in Descartes' physics, against English Newtonians. [1, 2]

Why would degree lengths reveal the shape? A degree of latitude is the distance you must travel north for the Pole Star to rise by one degree. Where the Earth's surface is flatter, its direction changes more slowly, so you must travel further to see the same change. On a squashed Earth, flattest near the poles, degrees get longer as you go north. On a stretched Earth, they get shorter. The differences in France were tiny, too small to measure reliably over so short a distance. The answer was to compare places as far apart as possible: near the equator and near the Arctic Circle.

4. Two Expeditions: Peru and Lapland

In 1735 the Paris Academy of Sciences sent an expedition to the Spanish colony of Peru, to a high valley near Quito in what is now Ecuador, close to the equator. It was led by the astronomer Louis Godin, the mathematician Pierre Bouguer and the scientist and adventurer Charles-Marie de La Condamine, joined by two young Spanish naval officers, Jorge Juan and Antonio de Ulloa. [6, 7]

It became one of the most troubled expeditions in the history of science. The team triangulated along a 350 km chain between two lines of volcanoes, camping on freezing peaks above 4,000 metres, while fighting altitude sickness, lack of money, quarrels with each other and with local authorities. The expedition's surgeon was killed by a mob at a bullfight in Cuenca. The work took about nine years. La Condamine went home by rafting down the entire Amazon, and was one of the first scientists to describe rubber to Europeans. [8]

A second expedition left in 1736 for Lapland, near the Arctic Circle, led by the ambitious young Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis, with the mathematician Alexis Clairaut and the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius (later famous for his temperature scale). They triangulated along the Torne valley on the border of Sweden and Finland, through the brief summer plagued by mosquitoes, and measured their baseline on the frozen river in midwinter. They were back in Paris in August 1737, having been away for only 16 months. [9, 1]

Their result was decisive: a degree in Lapland was about 57,440 toises, roughly 111.9 km, clearly longer than Picard's 111.2 km in France. The Peru team later found a degree near the equator of about 56,750 toises, roughly 110.6 km, clearly shorter. Degrees get longer towards the poles. The Earth is flattened at the poles: Newton was right. The writer Voltaire, a fervent Newtonian, teased Maupertuis for travelling so far to confirm what Newton had worked out at home. [6, 9]

In 1743 Clairaut showed how the Earth's flattening is linked to the way gravity changes with latitude, so that pendulum measurements like Richer's could also measure the Earth's shape. This idea, using gravity to map the Earth, is the basis of modern satellite gravity missions (Part 6 of this series). [10]

5. A Muddle of Measures

Before the 1790s, France alone had hundreds of different units of length. A "foot" or an "ell" (for measuring cloth) varied from town to town; a "pint" in Paris was not a pint in Lyon. Merchants could cheat, and scientists could not easily compare results. Units were often based on local customs or the bodies of rulers. [11]

When the French Revolution began in 1789, reformers wanted a system based not on kings or customs but on nature, which would be the same for everyone. Two natural standards were proposed. One was the length of a pendulum that swings once every second. But, as Richer had found, that length changes with gravity, and so with latitude. The other was the size of the Earth itself.

6. Measuring the Metre

In 1791 the Academy of Sciences chose the Earth. The new unit, the metre (from the Greek metron, a measure), would be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, measured along the meridian through Paris. To find that distance, two astronomers would survey the meridian from Dunkirk on the North Sea to Barcelona on the Mediterranean, an arc of nearly 10 degrees, and use the known flattening of the Earth to calculate the full distance to the pole and the equator. [11, 12]

Jean-Baptiste Delambre took the northern section, from Dunkirk to Rodez; Pierre Méchain the southern, from Rodez to Barcelona. They used a new instrument, the repeating circle invented by Jean-Charles de Borda, which allowed an angle to be measured many times over on the same scale, averaging out errors. They began in 1792, just as France fell into revolutionary turmoil and war. Delambre was repeatedly arrested as a suspected spy, since his instruments and papers looked suspicious to revolutionary militias. Méchain was trapped in Spain when war broke out between France and Spain.

The survey was finished in 1798. In 1799 a platinum bar, the mètre des Archives, was made to the calculated length and placed in the national archives as the official standard.

But Méchain carried a secret to his grave. Measuring the latitude of Barcelona from two different places, he found a discrepancy of about three seconds of arc, roughly 100 metres on the ground, that he could not explain. Rather than admit it, he hid the conflicting data, and he tormented himself over it until he died of yellow fever in 1804, still trying to resurvey the southern section. Delambre later found the hidden notes. The historian Ken Alder, who retold the story in The Measure of All Things (2002), showed that the error was probably due to the limits of the instruments and the bending of light, not to carelessness. [11]

The metre ended up about 0.2 millimetres shorter than one ten-millionth of the true quarter-meridian, mainly because the value for the Earth's flattening used in the calculation was too large. But by then the platinum bar, not the Earth, was the definition.

7. From the Earth to Light

The metric system spread slowly at first, even in France. In 1875 seventeen nations signed the Metre Convention, setting up an International Bureau of Weights and Measures near Paris. In 1889 a new international prototype metre, a bar of platinum–iridium alloy with an X-shaped cross-section to keep it rigid, became the world's standard. [13]

A bar can be damaged, and can only be in one place. So scientists turned to nature again, this time to light. In 1960 the metre was redefined as a certain number of wavelengths of the orange-red light given off by krypton atoms. In 1983 it was redefined once more, in terms of the speed of light: the metre is now the distance light travels in a vacuum in exactly 1/299,792,458 of a second. The speed of light was measured so accurately (Part 5 of this series) that it became the foundation of length itself.

Today almost every country uses the metric system for science and most everyday purposes. The original dream of a measure "for all people, for all time" has largely come true, though its definition has moved from the Earth to the fundamental constants of nature.

8. The Great Trigonometrical Survey of India

The most ambitious survey of all took place in India. In 1802 an infantry officer and self-taught mathematician, William Lambton, measured a baseline of about 12 km near Madras (now Chennai) with a 100-foot steel chain laid in wooden coffers, checking its temperature constantly because steel expands in the heat. From there he began a network of triangles that would eventually cover the subcontinent: the Great Trigonometrical Survey. [14, 15]

Its spine was the Great Arc: a chain of triangles running north along the meridian at about 78° east, from near the southern tip of India towards the Himalayas, some 2,400 km. It was one of the longest arcs of meridian ever measured, and it gave a valuable new measure of the Earth's shape. Lambton's "Great Theodolite", used for measuring angles, weighed about half a tonne and needed a team of a dozen men to carry. To see over the plains, surveyors built towers, cut down trees and signalled to each other with bright lamps and mirrors at night. Many workers died of malaria and other diseases. [14]

After Lambton's death in 1823 the work was led by George Everest, a demanding perfectionist who introduced better instruments and methods, and who completed the arc to the Himalayan foothills by 1841. The survey depended heavily on Indian surveyors, labourers and mathematicians, whose contributions were often left out of official accounts. [16, 15]

9. The Height of Peak XV

From the plains of northern India, surveyors could see the great snowy peaks of the Himalayas on the horizon, more than 150 km away. Nepal was closed to foreigners, so the mountains had to be measured from a distance. Surveyors gave them numbers. Between 1849 and 1850, the summit known as Peak XV was sighted from six survey stations, at distances of around 170 to 190 km. [15, 17]

The calculations were done by the Survey's "computers", people employed to do calculations by hand, in an office led by Radhanath Sikdar (1813–1870), a brilliant mathematician from Calcutta whom Everest had recruited. Measuring a mountain that far away is not just a matter of the angle and the distance. Two corrections are essential:

  • The Earth's curve: over 175 km, the curve of the Earth drops the mountain's base more than two kilometres below the surveyor's level line, so the mountain is higher than the angle alone suggests.
  • The bending of light (refraction): light passing through the air curves slightly downwards, because air is denser near the ground. This makes the summit appear higher than it is, so the correction goes the other way. It depends on temperature and pressure, and is the hardest part to get right.

By 1852 the computations showed that Peak XV was higher than any other known mountain. After checking, Andrew Waugh, Everest's successor as Surveyor General, announced in 1856 that its height was 29,002 feet (8,840 metres). Waugh proposed naming it after Everest, who had retired in 1843. Everest himself objected, saying the name could not be written or pronounced in Hindi and that local names should be used where they existed. The name was adopted anyway in 1865. In Nepal the mountain is called Sagarmatha, and in Tibet Chomolungma. [17, 14]

10. The Shape and Height of the Earth Today

The Earth's flattening is now known to be about 1 part in 298: the polar radius (6,356.8 km) is about 21 km shorter than the equatorial radius (6,378.1 km). Newton's estimate of 1 part in 230 was in the right direction and not far off. [19]

The Earth is not quite a neat squashed ball either. Its true shape, defined by the surface of the oceans if they were extended under the continents, is called the geoid, and it has bumps and dips of up to about 100 metres, caused by uneven distribution of mass inside the Earth. Satellites map it by measuring tiny variations in gravity.

Thanks to the bulge, the point on Earth furthest from its centre is not the top of Everest but the summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador, which sits almost on the equator, a volcano the Peru expedition knew well. And Everest's height has been remeasured many times. A survey by India in 1952–54, published in 1955, gave 8,848 metres. [18] In December 2020 Nepal and China jointly announced 8,848.86 metres, after surveyors climbed to the summit with satellite receivers and ground-penetrating radar to measure through the snow. [20]

12. Try It Yourself: Measure a Tree or Building with Angles

Try it yourself: be a trigonometrical surveyor

Measure the height of a tall tree, a building or a flagpole without climbing it, just as the Survey of India measured mountains (but without needing the Earth's curve).

You need: a way to measure an angle upwards (a phone clinometer app, or a protractor with a drinking straw taped along its flat edge and a weighted string hanging from its centre), a tape measure, and a friend to help.

Method 1: one station (when you can reach the base).

  1. Stand on level ground and measure your distance d from the base of the tree.
  2. Sight the top through the straw (or phone) and read the angle of elevation a. With a protractor, the angle is 90° minus where the string crosses the scale.
  3. Height = d × tan(a) + the height of your eye above the ground.

Method 2: two stations (when you can't reach the base), as the surveyors did with mountains.

  1. Measure the angle to the top from one point (a₁), then walk directly away from the tree a measured distance b and measure again (a₂, which will be smaller).
  2. Height above your eye = b ÷ (1/tan(a₂) − 1/tan(a₁)).

Worked example: from 20 m away the top of a tree is at 40°; your eye is 1.5 m up. Height = 20 × tan 40° + 1.5 = 20 × 0.839 + 1.5 ≈ 18.3 m. Check with Method 2: step back another 10 m and measure again; you should get a similar answer. If not, which measurement do you trust least? That question is the beginning of error analysis.

13. How Did They Know? Weighing the Evidence from Six Stations

How did they know? Turning sightings into a reliable height

Each sighting of Peak XV gave a slightly different height. How did the Survey turn several imperfect measurements into one trustworthy number? [15, 17]

  1. Know your stations precisely. Each observing station was itself part of the triangulation network, so its position and height were known accurately, and so was its distance to the peak.
  2. Measure the angle many times. Observers measured the angle of elevation repeatedly with large theodolites, and recorded the time of day, temperature and air pressure.
  3. Correct for the Earth's curve. This correction can be calculated exactly from the distance and the Earth's radius.
  4. Correct for refraction. This is the uncertain part. Refraction is weakest around midday, so midday observations were preferred, and a standard correction was applied.
  5. Compare and combine. With six independent stations at different distances and directions, errors in one station's refraction or angle show up as disagreements. The final height combined the results.
  6. Check against other peaks. The same methods applied to neighbouring mountains had to give consistent relative heights.

The result, 8,840 metres, was within about 9 metres of the modern value: a remarkable achievement for observations made from 170 km away, through the hazy air of the plains, a century before anyone stood on the summit.

14. Legend and Record: "Sir, I Have Discovered the Highest Mountain!"

Legend and Record: Radhanath Sikdar and Peak XV

The legend: one day in 1852, Radhanath Sikdar burst into the office of the Surveyor General, Andrew Waugh, exclaiming: "Sir, I have discovered the highest mountain in the world!" [15, 21]

The record: this vivid scene is poorly documented. Historians have traced its popular form to retellings in the early 20th century, decades after the event, and no contemporary record of the exclamation has been found. Calculations of this kind were also a team effort over months, checked repeatedly before anyone would make such a claim. Another popular story, that the calculation came out at exactly 29,000 feet and two feet were added so it would not look like a rough guess, is also often repeated without documentary evidence.

What is well established: Sikdar was the Survey's chief computer, a mathematician of great ability whom Everest valued highly, and his office carried out the demanding calculations, including the refraction corrections, that identified Peak XV as the highest peak. Like many Indian contributors to the Survey, his role was underplayed in official accounts at the time, and it has rightly been recognised more fully since. India issued a postage stamp in his honour in 2004.

Why it matters: legends often attach a single dramatic moment to work that was really slow and collective. The truth, months of careful calculation by a team led by a talented Indian mathematician, is less theatrical, but more interesting, and more fair to everyone involved.

15. Philosophy Corner: What Makes a Good Unit?

Philosophy Corner: "for all people, for all time"

The makers of the metric system wanted units drawn from nature, not from kings. Was the Earth a good choice? [11, 13]

In one sense, it failed: the Earth turned out to be too irregular, and too hard to measure, to serve as a precise standard. Within a few years the platinum bar, a human-made object, had become the real definition. In another sense it succeeded brilliantly: the idea of a universal, rational system of units, the same for everyone, spread across the world.

This raises a deep question. What does it mean for a unit to be "natural"? The Earth is natural, but it is just one planet; aliens elsewhere would not share it. The speed of light, which now defines the metre, is the same everywhere in the universe, as far as we know. In 2019 every unit of the international system was redefined in terms of such fundamental constants, including the kilogram, which until then was defined by a metal cylinder kept near Paris (Part 12 of this series).

Yet even a definition based on the speed of light depends on human choices: why a metre, and not some other length? A unit is partly discovered and partly agreed. Its value comes not only from nature, but from everyone deciding to use it the same way.
