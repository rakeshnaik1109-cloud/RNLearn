CPUs and GPUs, Generation by Generation: From the 4004 to Exascale AI

One chip finishes a single task as fast as possible; the other finishes millions at once. How the CPU and the GPU grew up, generation by generation, and what each can do today.

1. Two Kinds of Processor

Every computer, from a phone to a supercomputer, now contains two very different kinds of processor. The CPU (central processing unit) runs the operating system, your browser and almost every program you have ever written. The GPU (graphics processing unit) began as a helper for drawing 3D games and has become the engine of modern science and artificial intelligence.

They are built from the same transistors on the same factory lines, but designed around opposite goals. A CPU tries to finish one task as quickly as possible. A GPU tries to finish millions of small, similar tasks in the least total time. In fifty years the CPU has gone from 2,300 transistors to tens of billions; the GPU, in twenty-five years, from a games accessory to chips with over 200 billion transistors that train large language models.

This article follows both, generation by generation: what changed, why, and what each can do today. The inner workings (pipelines, caches, warps and the roofline model) are explained in the Electronics series, in The Processor and GPUs and Parallel Computing; here the focus is on the story and the numbers.

3. Latency and Throughput: What Each Is Built For

Imagine a restaurant. A CPU is a small team of brilliant chefs, each able to cook any dish quickly, improvise, and handle orders that depend on each other. A GPU is a huge kitchen of line cooks who each do one simple step, all at the same time. For a banquet of 10,000 identical plates, the huge kitchen wins easily. For one complicated meal where every step depends on the last, the brilliant chefs win.

Engineers call these two goals latency (how long one task takes) and throughput (how many tasks finish per second). A modern CPU spends most of its transistors on reducing latency: large caches so that data is close at hand, branch predictors that guess which way a program will go, and out-of-order execution that finds independent work to do while waiting. A GPU spends its transistors on arithmetic units, thousands of them, and hides the delay of fetching data by switching between many threads.

The limit on the GPU’s advantage is the part of a job that cannot be split up. Gene Amdahl pointed out in 1967 that if even 5% of a program must run in sequence, no number of processors can make it more than 20 times faster. [1] So the two chips are partners, not rivals: the CPU runs the program and hands the massively parallel parts to the GPU.

4. The First Microprocessors, 1971–1985

In 1969 the Japanese calculator company Busicom asked Intel to design a set of chips. Ted Hoff proposed instead a general-purpose processor that could be programmed for each task, and Federico Faggin, with Masatoshi Shima and Stanley Mazor, turned the idea into silicon. The Intel 4004, announced in November 1971, put a whole 4-bit processor on one chip: 2,300 transistors made with 10-micrometre features, running at up to 740 kHz. [2] Adding two numbers took it about ten millionths of a second.

Six years earlier, Gordon Moore had noticed that the number of components on a chip was doubling every year and predicted it would continue. [3] In 1975 he revised the pace to a doubling every two years, [4] and “Moore’s law” held, give or take, for half a century.

The 1970s brought 8-bit chips that started the personal computer: the Intel 8080 (1974), the cheap MOS Technology 6502 (1975), which with its variants ran the Apple II, Commodore 64 and Nintendo Entertainment System, and the Zilog Z80 (1976). Intel’s 16-bit 8086 of 1978 defined the x86 instruction set; a variant, the 8088, went into the IBM PC in 1981, and programs written for it can still run on today’s PCs. Motorola’s 68000 (1979) powered the first Macintosh. The Intel 386 of 1985, with 275,000 transistors, brought 32-bit computing and paged virtual memory, the foundation of modern operating systems.

These chips executed instructions one at a time, each taking several clock cycles. Floating-point arithmetic, if needed, required a separate maths coprocessor chip.

5. The Megahertz Race, 1985–2004

For the next twenty years, CPUs got faster in two ways at once: smaller transistors switched faster, allowing higher clock speeds, and the extra transistors were spent on doing more work per tick.

  • Intel 486 (1989): 1.2 million transistors, with the floating-point unit and a cache built onto the chip, and a pipeline that finished about one instruction per cycle.
  • Pentium (1993): 3.1 million transistors and superscalar: two pipelines, so two instructions per cycle.
  • Pentium Pro (1995) and AMD’s competing designs: out-of-order execution, reordering instructions on the fly to keep the units busy.
  • MMX (1997) and SSE (1999): SIMD (single instruction, multiple data) instructions that apply one operation to several numbers at once, for multimedia.
  • 1 GHz (2000): AMD’s Athlon reached a billion cycles per second in March 2000, two days ahead of Intel’s Pentium III.
  • Pentium 4 (2000–2004): a very deep pipeline (20 stages, later 31) designed for clock speed, reaching 3.8 GHz in 2004.
  • AMD64 (2003): AMD extended x86 to 64 bits with its Opteron and Athlon 64; Intel adopted the same extension, which every PC and x86 server uses today.

Users could feel each generation: software simply ran faster on a new machine, without any change. That “free lunch” was about to end.

6. The Power Wall and the Multicore Turn, 2004–2016

In 1974 Robert Dennard and colleagues at IBM showed that as transistors shrink, their voltage and current shrink too, so power per unit area stays constant. [5] This Dennard scaling is why chips could get faster without getting hotter. By the mid-2000s it had broken down: transistors were so small that they leaked current even when switched off, and voltages could no longer fall. Pushing the clock higher made chips too hot to cool. In May 2004 Intel cancelled the successors to the Pentium 4, and clock speeds have stayed between about 3 and 6 GHz ever since.

The industry turned to putting several processor cores on one chip. IBM’s POWER4 server chip had two cores in 2001; in 2005 AMD and Intel brought dual-core processors to desktops, and quad-cores followed in 2006. In 2005 Herb Sutter warned programmers that “the free lunch is over”: from now on, software would only get faster if it was written to use many cores at once. [6]

The following decade refined the multicore CPU. Intel’s Core 2 (2006) put efficiency ahead of clock speed. Memory controllers moved onto the processor (AMD in 2003, Intel’s Nehalem in 2008), cutting the time to reach memory. Sandy Bridge (2011) added a GPU on the same chip and 256-bit AVX vector instructions. Vector units kept widening because they deliver more arithmetic per watt than extra cores. Researchers warned of dark silicon: chips with more transistors than could be powered at once. [7] Progress slowed: Intel was stuck on its 14 nm process from 2014 until 2019, and for several years new desktop CPUs were only modestly faster than the last.

7. The Modern CPU, 2017–2026

Competition returned in 2017 with AMD’s Zen architecture (Ryzen and EPYC). Its second generation (2019) introduced chiplets: instead of one large die, a processor is assembled from several small compute dies around an input/output die. Small dies are cheaper to make without defects, and the approach let AMD offer 64 cores in 2019 and 192 in its EPYC 9005 server chips of 2024.

In November 2020 Apple replaced Intel processors in the Mac with its own M1, an Arm-based system-on-chip with 16 billion transistors made on a 5 nm process. It combined high-performance and high-efficiency cores, a GPU, a neural-network engine and unified memory shared by all of them, and set a new standard for performance per watt in laptops. Intel’s Alder Lake (2021) adopted a similar mix of performance and efficiency cores for x86. AMD’s 3D V-Cache (2022) stacked an extra layer of cache memory on top of the processor, which proved especially effective in games.

Arm also entered the data centre. Amazon’s Graviton chips (from 2018) run a large share of its cloud servers, and Nvidia pairs its Grace Arm CPU with its GPUs. Today’s flagship CPUs offer:

  • Desktops and laptops: 8 to 24 cores at up to about 6 GHz, with peak vector arithmetic of one to a few teraflops (trillion floating-point operations per second).
  • Servers: up to 192 cores in one socket, twelve channels of DDR5 memory delivering over 500 GB/s, and hundreds of megabytes of cache.
  • Phones: 6 to 8 Arm cores in a chip that uses a few watts, faster than desktop PCs of a decade earlier.

The transistors themselves keep changing: FinFETs replaced flat transistors around 2011–2012, and gate-all-around transistors are arriving with the 2 nm generation, as described in Modern Transistors. Process names such as “3 nm” no longer measure any physical feature; they are labels for generations.

8. Instruction Sets: x86, Arm and RISC-V

An instruction set is the vocabulary a processor understands. Three matter today.

  • x86, from the 8086 of 1978, has hundreds of complex, variable-length instructions. Intel and AMD processors translate them internally into simpler operations. It dominates PCs and servers, largely because of the vast amount of software written for it.
  • Arm follows the RISC (reduced instruction set computer) idea that David Patterson, John Hennessy and others argued for around 1980: a small set of simple, regular instructions that are easy to pipeline. [8] The first ARM chip was designed at Acorn Computers in Cambridge by Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber and ran in 1985. Its low power made Arm the choice for mobile devices; nearly every smartphone uses it, as do Apple’s Macs and a growing number of servers.
  • RISC-V, begun at Berkeley in 2010, is an open standard that anyone can use without a licence. [9] It is spreading in microcontrollers, storage devices and research chips, and is starting to appear in larger processors.

Hennessy and Patterson, who shared the 2017 Turing Award, argue that with transistor scaling slowing, the biggest gains will now come from specialised designs and open instruction sets, a “new golden age for computer architecture”. [10] The rise of GPUs and AI accelerators is that prediction coming true.

9. Before the GPU: Graphics Chips, 1980s–1999

Drawing a 3D scene means repeating the same small calculations for every vertex of every triangle and every pixel on the screen, tens of millions of times a second. In the 1980s this was done by expensive workstations such as those of Silicon Graphics, and by special chips in arcade machines. Texas Instruments’ TMS34010 of 1986 was one of the first programmable graphics processors on a single chip.

Cheap 3D arrived on the PC with the 3dfx Voodoo card in 1996, which drew textured triangles while the CPU did the geometry. Nvidia, founded in 1993, followed with the RIVA 128 (1997) and, in August 1999, the GeForce 256, which it marketed as “the world’s first GPU”. Its advance was hardware transform and lighting: the chip itself now positioned and lit the vertices, taking that load off the CPU. ATI (bought by AMD in 2006) competed with its Radeon line from 2000.

These early GPUs were fixed-function: they could do only the operations built into them, in a fixed order called the graphics pipeline.

10. Programmable Shaders, 2001–2006

The GeForce 3 of 2001 let programmers replace parts of that pipeline with small programs called shaders: a vertex shader runs once for each vertex, a pixel shader once for each pixel. ATI’s Radeon 9700 (2002) added floating-point arithmetic in its pixel shaders. Suddenly a games chip could be programmed to do arithmetic on large arrays of numbers, very fast.

Researchers noticed. By disguising their data as images and their calculations as shaders, they ran fluid simulations, linear algebra and database searches on graphics cards, a movement called GPGPU (general-purpose computing on GPUs). In 2004 Ian Buck and colleagues at Stanford released Brook, a language that hid the graphics details and let programmers write “stream” programs directly. [11] Buck then joined Nvidia.

11. Unified Shaders and CUDA, 2006–2012

Early GPUs had separate units for vertices and pixels, so one kind often sat idle. ATI’s Xenos chip for the Xbox 360 (2005) introduced unified shaders, processors that could do either job. Nvidia’s GeForce 8800 GTX of November 2006 (the G80, or “Tesla” architecture) took the idea to PCs with 128 unified scalar cores, designed from the start for general computing as well as graphics. [12]

With it came CUDA, released in 2007: a way to program the GPU in an extended version of C, launching thousands of threads that each handle one piece of data. [13] For the first time, scientists could use a cheap graphics card as a parallel computer without pretending to draw pictures. The open standard OpenCL followed at the end of 2008. Nvidia’s Fermi architecture (2010) added the features scientists needed: fast double-precision arithmetic, caches and error-correcting memory. In 2012 the Titan supercomputer at Oak Ridge used 18,688 GPUs, and GPUs began to fill the lists of the world’s fastest machines.

12. GPUs Become AI Engines, 2012–2026

In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep neural network, now called AlexNet, on two Nvidia GeForce GTX 580 gaming cards over about a week. It won the ImageNet image-recognition contest with an error rate far below any rival. [14] Training neural networks is mostly multiplying large matrices, exactly what GPUs do best, and deep learning took off. Each GPU generation since has been designed more and more around AI.

  • Pascal P100 (2016): stacked high-bandwidth memory (HBM2) and NVLink connections between GPUs.
  • Volta V100 (2017): the first tensor cores, units that multiply small matrices in low precision, giving 125 teraflops for neural networks against 15.7 for ordinary arithmetic.
  • Turing (2018): RT cores for real-time ray tracing in games, and AI-based upscaling (DLSS).
  • Ampere A100 (2020): 54 billion transistors and new number formats (TF32, BF16) for training.
  • Hopper H100 (2022): 80 billion transistors, 8-bit floating point (FP8) and a “Transformer Engine” for language models, 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth.
  • AMD Instinct MI300X (2023): 153 billion transistors in a stack of chiplets, with 192 GB of HBM3 memory.
  • Blackwell B200 (2024): two dies acting as one GPU, 208 billion transistors, 4-bit arithmetic, 8 TB/s of memory bandwidth, and racks of 72 GPUs linked as one system.

Google took a different route with its Tensor Processing Unit, a chip built only for neural networks, deployed in its data centres from 2015. [15] Today’s largest AI models are trained on tens of thousands of accelerators at once: Meta, for example, reported training its Llama 3 models on up to 16,384 H100 GPUs. [16] Gaming GPUs share the same architectures: the GeForce RTX 5090 of 2025 has 92 billion transistors, 21,760 shader cores and about 105 teraflops of FP32 arithmetic: more, even at that lower precision, than the roughly 7 teraflops of 64-bit arithmetic of the fastest supercomputer of 2001.

13. Generation by Generation in Numbers

The explorer below collects published figures for landmark chips. Because the numbers span many orders of magnitude, the vertical axis is logarithmic: each grid line is ten times the one below, and steady exponential growth appears as a straight line.

Three trends stand out. Transistor counts have doubled roughly every two years for both kinds of chip. GPUs passed CPUs in raw arithmetic around 2006 and have pulled far ahead, because they spend their transistors on arithmetic rather than on latency-reducing machinery. And memory bandwidth, the rate at which data can be fed to the cores, has become as important as arithmetic: an H100 moves data from memory about forty times faster than a desktop CPU.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Transistor counts of some landmark chips (manufacturers' published figures)
cpus = {1971: 2_300, 1978: 29_000, 1985: 275_000, 1989: 1_200_000, 1993: 3_100_000,
        2000: 42_000_000, 2006: 291_000_000, 2008: 731_000_000, 2011: 1_160_000_000,
        2020: 16_000_000_000, 2023: 92_000_000_000}
gpus = {2001: 57_000_000, 2006: 681_000_000, 2010: 3_000_000_000, 2016: 7_200_000_000,
        2017: 21_100_000_000, 2020: 54_200_000_000, 2022: 80_000_000_000,
        2023: 153_000_000_000, 2024: 208_000_000_000}

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
for name, d, m in [("CPUs", cpus, "o"), ("GPUs", gpus, "s")]:
    years, counts = np.array(list(d)), np.array(list(d.values()))
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(years, np.log2(counts), 1)     # straight line in log2 space
    print(f"{name}: transistor count doubles every {1 / slope:.2f} years")
    ax.scatter(years, counts, marker=m, label=f"{name} (doubling every {1 / slope:.1f} years)")
    ax.plot(years, 2 ** (intercept + slope * years), lw=1)
ax.set_yscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("Year")
ax.set_ylabel("Transistors per chip")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

14. Head to Head: Compare Two Chips

Pick any two chips from the list to see how they compare. Try the Intel 4004 against the RTX 5090, a Pentium against an H100, or a desktop CPU against a gaming GPU of the same year.

Peak numbers flatter both kinds of chip. A CPU reaches its peak only with carefully vectorised code; a GPU only when there is enough parallel work and the data arrives fast enough. Real programs often achieve a fraction of the peak, and the fraction depends on the program far more than on the chip.

15. Smaller Numbers, Faster Answers

Much of the recent leap in AI performance comes not from more transistors but from smaller numbers. Scientific computing traditionally used 64-bit floating point (FP64). Graphics used 32 bits. Neural networks turned out to work with far less precision, and each halving of the bits roughly doubles how many numbers a chip can move and multiply per second.

This is why AI accelerators quote several performance figures. An H100 offers about 67 teraflops in FP32 but nearly 1,000 in FP16 on its tensor cores and about 2,000 in FP8; Blackwell adds FP4. Scientific simulations that need FP64 benefit far less. When comparing chips, always check which precision a number refers to, and whether it assumes “sparsity” (skipping zeros), which doubles the advertised figure.

import numpy as np

# Fewer bits per number means more numbers per second, but less precision and range
x = 1 / 3
for dtype in [np.float64, np.float32, np.float16]:
    info = np.finfo(dtype)
    print(f"{np.dtype(dtype).name:>8}: 1/3 = {str(dtype(x)):<20} largest = {info.max:.3g}, "
          f"about {info.precision} decimal digits")

# float16 overflows easily, one reason AI training uses bfloat16 or careful scaling
with np.errstate(over="ignore"):
    print(np.float16(300) * np.float16(300))  # inf: 90,000 is beyond float16's maximum of 65,504

# Rounding errors build up: add 0.1 ten thousand times (the answer should be 1000)
for dtype in [np.float16, np.float32]:
    total = dtype(0)
    for _ in range(10_000):
        total = dtype(total + dtype(0.1))
    print(f"{np.dtype(dtype).name}: {total}")   # float16 gets stuck at 256: the gap between numbers there is 0.25

16. Supercomputers: From Teraflops to Exaflops

The TOP500 list, published twice a year since 1993, ranks supercomputers by how fast they solve a large system of linear equations. [17] Its milestones show the shift from CPUs to GPUs:

  • 1997, one teraflop: ASCI Red at Sandia, built from thousands of Intel Pentium Pro processors.
  • 2008, one petaflop: Roadrunner at Los Alamos, combining AMD Opteron CPUs with IBM Cell processors.
  • 2012: Titan at Oak Ridge, with Nvidia GPUs doing most of the work.
  • 2020: Fugaku in Japan, a rare CPU-only leader, built from Fujitsu’s Arm-based A64FX chips with wide vector units.
  • 2022, one exaflop: Frontier at Oak Ridge, with AMD CPUs and GPUs, the first machine measured above 1018 operations per second.
  • 2024: El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore, about 1.7 exaflops, using AMD MI300A chips that combine CPU and GPU cores in one package.

A million-fold increase in 25 years. A single B200 GPU now offers, at low precision, more arithmetic than the whole of the fastest supercomputer of 2008, although for the FP64 work that supercomputers are ranked on, the comparison is far less dramatic.

You can explore your own machine with Python. First, what is there?

import os
import platform

# What processor is this code running on?
print("Machine:", platform.machine())              # e.g. x86_64 or arm64
print("Processor:", platform.processor() or "unknown")
print("Logical CPU cores:", os.cpu_count())

# Is there a GPU that Python can use? Try PyTorch, the most common route.
try:
    import torch
    if torch.cuda.is_available():
        print("CUDA GPU:", torch.cuda.get_device_name(0))
    elif torch.backends.mps.is_available():
        print("Apple GPU available through Metal (MPS)")
    else:
        print("PyTorch is installed but sees no GPU")
except ImportError:
    print("PyTorch not installed; cannot check for a GPU this way")

Even on a CPU, doing arithmetic on whole arrays at once uses its SIMD units and is many times faster than a Python loop:

import time
import numpy as np

n = 2_000_000
a = np.random.default_rng(0).random(n)
b = np.random.default_rng(1).random(n)

# One element at a time, like a single simple core
t = time.perf_counter()
c = [a[i] * b[i] + 1.0 for i in range(n)]
t_loop = time.perf_counter() - t

# Whole arrays at once: NumPy runs compiled loops that use the CPU's SIMD units
t = time.perf_counter()
c2 = a * b + 1.0
t_vec = time.perf_counter() - t

print(f"Python loop: {t_loop:.3f} s")
print(f"NumPy:       {t_vec:.4f} s  ({t_loop / t_vec:.0f}x faster)")
print("Same answer:", np.allclose(c, c2))

Independent work spreads across cores. On a 4-core laptop, four workers finish this job about four times faster than one:

import os
import time
from concurrent.futures import ProcessPoolExecutor

def count_primes(limit):
    """Deliberately slow, CPU-bound work: count primes below limit by trial division."""
    count = 0
    for n in range(2, limit):
        if all(n % d for d in range(2, int(n ** 0.5) + 1)):
            count += 1
    return count

if __name__ == "__main__":
    jobs = [60_000] * 8                      # eight independent chunks of work
    for workers in sorted({1, 2, 4, os.cpu_count()}):
        t = time.perf_counter()
        with ProcessPoolExecutor(max_workers=workers) as pool:
            results = list(pool.map(count_primes, jobs))
        print(f"{workers:>2} worker(s): {time.perf_counter() - t:.2f} s")

Large matrix multiplication is the most efficient thing a processor can do, because each number fetched from memory is used many times. A modern laptop CPU typically reaches a few hundred GFLOPS here, a few percent of what a gaming GPU manages:

import time
import numpy as np

# Measure how many floating-point operations per second this CPU achieves on matrix multiplication
for n in [256, 512, 1024, 2048]:
    a = np.random.rand(n, n).astype(np.float32)
    b = np.random.rand(n, n).astype(np.float32)
    a @ b                                    # warm up
    reps = max(1, int(2e9 / n**3))
    t = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(reps):
        a @ b
    dt = (time.perf_counter() - t) / reps
    flops = 2 * n**3                         # n^3 multiply-adds = 2 n^3 operations
    print(f"n = {n:>4}: {flops / dt / 1e9:7.1f} GFLOPS")

# For comparison, an RTX 5090's peak is about 105,000 GFLOPS at FP32,
# and an H100 reaches about 989,000 GFLOPS on FP16 tensor cores.

Simple array operations, by contrast, are limited by memory bandwidth, not arithmetic. A laptop moves something like 10–100 GB/s; a data-centre GPU, several thousand:

import time
import numpy as np

# A STREAM-style "triad": c = a + s*b reads two arrays and writes one.
n = 20_000_000                               # 20 million float64 = 160 MB per array
a = np.ones(n)
b = np.ones(n)
c = np.empty(n)
best = float("inf")
for _ in range(5):
    t = time.perf_counter()
    np.multiply(b, 3.0, out=c)
    np.add(a, c, out=c)
    best = min(best, time.perf_counter() - t)
moved = 4 * n * 8                            # bytes read and written by the two NumPy passes
print(f"Memory bandwidth: about {moved / best / 1e9:.1f} GB/s")
print(f"Arithmetic intensity: {2 * n / moved:.3f} FLOP per byte (memory-bound)")

If you have PyTorch and a GPU (Nvidia through CUDA, or Apple silicon through Metal), the same matrix multiplication shows the difference directly:

import time

try:
    import torch
except ImportError:
    torch = None

if torch is None:
    print("Install PyTorch (pytorch.org) to run this comparison.")
else:
    device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else ("mps" if torch.backends.mps.is_available() else "cpu")
    print("Using device:", device)
    n = 4096
    for dev in ["cpu", device] if device != "cpu" else ["cpu"]:
        a = torch.randn(n, n, device=dev)
        b = torch.randn(n, n, device=dev)
        a @ b                                         # warm up (first GPU call is slow)
        if dev == "cuda":
            torch.cuda.synchronize()                  # GPU work is asynchronous: wait for it
        t = time.perf_counter()
        for _ in range(10):
            c = a @ b
        if dev == "cuda":
            torch.cuda.synchronize()
        dt = (time.perf_counter() - t) / 10
        print(f"{dev:>4}: {2 * n**3 / dt / 1e12:.2f} TFLOPS")

18. Beyond CPU and GPU

  • NPUs. Phones and laptops now include neural processing units, small accelerators for AI that use far less power than a GPU for tasks such as speech recognition and photo processing. Apple’s Neural Engine first appeared in 2017.
  • Custom AI chips. Google’s TPUs, Amazon’s Trainium and others trade generality for efficiency at neural-network arithmetic.
  • CPU and GPU together. The boundary is blurring: Apple’s M-series share one pool of memory between CPU and GPU, AMD’s MI300A combines both in one package, and Nvidia’s Grace Hopper and Grace Blackwell “superchips” link an Arm CPU to GPUs with a fast connection.
  • Packaging. Chiplets, stacked memory (HBM) placed beside the processor, and 3D-stacked cache now matter as much as transistor size. Advanced packaging capacity has itself become a bottleneck for AI chips.
  • Energy. A rack of 72 Blackwell GPUs draws over 100 kilowatts, and AI data centres are now planned around their power supply. Performance per watt has become the measure that matters most.

Where the industry goes next, including new materials and new kinds of computing, is discussed in Beyond Silicon.

19. Common Misconceptions

  • “More gigahertz means faster.” A 2024 core at 5 GHz does several times more work per cycle than a 2004 Pentium 4 at 3.8 GHz.
  • “More cores always means faster.” Only if the software can use them; many programs depend mostly on one core’s speed.
  • “A GPU could replace the CPU.” GPUs are poor at branchy, sequential code, and they depend on a CPU to run the operating system and launch their work.
  • “Teraflops tell you everything.” Check the precision (FP64, FP32, FP16, FP8, FP4), whether sparsity is assumed, and whether the job is limited by memory bandwidth instead.
  • “3 nm means transistors are 3 nm wide.” Process names stopped matching physical sizes decades ago.
  • “Moving work to the GPU always helps.” Copying data across costs time; small jobs are often faster on the CPU.

20. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: was the GeForce 256 the first GPU?

The legend: Nvidia invented the GPU with the GeForce 256 in 1999.

The record: Nvidia did popularise the term with that chip, which was the first consumer graphics chip to do transform and lighting in hardware. But programmable graphics processors existed earlier, such as Texas Instruments’ TMS34010 of 1986, and Sony had already called the graphics chip of its 1994 PlayStation a “GPU”. What changed in 1999 was how much of the 3D pipeline moved off the CPU.

A second legend: Moore’s law says computing power doubles every 18 months. The record: Moore’s 1965 paper predicted that the number of components on a chip would double every year; in 1975 he revised this to every two years. [3] [4] The “18 months” figure is usually credited to his Intel colleague David House, who estimated how fast performance would grow when faster transistors were added to the count. Moore himself said he never predicted 18 months.
