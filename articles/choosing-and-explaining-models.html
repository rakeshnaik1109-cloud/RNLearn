Choosing and Explaining Models: Comparison, Tuning and Interpretability

A good model is not enough: you have to pick it fairly, tune it without fooling yourself, and understand why it predicts what it does.

At a glance

You will learn: why no model is best everywhere; how to start from a baseline; how to compare models fairly with cross-validation; grid, random and Bayesian hyperparameter search; why choosing among many models inflates their scores, and how nested cross-validation fixes it; interpretable models versus explained black boxes; permutation importance, partial dependence and ICE curves; LIME and SHAP for single predictions; explaining neural networks; shortcut learning; and fairness, data shift and model documentation.

You need: Part 1 (validation, leakage) and a passing acquaintance with the models of Parts 2–9.

1. The Last Mile

Parts 1–9 built a toolbox: linear models, neighbours and trees, ensembles, kernels, and neural networks of many shapes, with the losses and training methods behind them. This final part is about using that toolbox well. Which model should you try? How do you tune it without fooling yourself? And once it works, how do you find out why it works, so that you can trust it, debug it and explain its decisions to the people they affect?

These questions decide whether a model is useful in the real world, and they are where many projects quietly fail. [1, 2]

3. No Free Lunch

Is there a best learning algorithm? In 1996 David Wolpert proved that, averaged over all possible problems, every algorithm performs equally well: any method that wins on some problems must lose on others. [3] This “no free lunch” theorem does not mean that all methods are equal in practice, because real problems are not random. It means that a method wins exactly when its built-in assumptions, its inductive bias (Part 9), match the problem. Linear models assume additive effects; trees assume that thresholds matter; convolutional networks assume local patterns. Choosing a model is choosing which assumptions to bet on.

4. Start with a Baseline

Before anything elaborate, fit something simple: predict the average or the most common class, then fit a linear or logistic regression. A baseline tells you how hard the problem is and how much each added complication is really worth. Often it is closer to the best result than expected.

  • Tables of mixed features: gradient-boosted trees and random forests (Part 4) are hard to beat. In a comparison of 179 classifiers on 121 data sets, Manuel Fernández-Delgado and colleagues found random forests best overall; [4] Léo Grinsztajn, Edouard Oyallon and Gaël Varoquaux found tree ensembles still ahead of deep networks on typical tabular data in 2022. [5]
  • Images, sound, text: start from a pre-trained convolutional network or transformer (Part 9) and fine-tune it.
  • Few examples: prefer simpler, more constrained models, or strong pre-trained ones.
  • Need for explanation: consider models that are interpretable in themselves (Section 8).

5. Comparing Models Fairly

A single train–test split gives a noisy verdict, especially with small data sets. k-fold cross-validation, formalised by Mervyn Stone in 1974, splits the data into k parts, trains on k − 1 and tests on the remaining one, k times over, so that every example is used for testing exactly once. [6] The average estimates performance; the spread across folds shows how much to trust differences.

On the two clouds, logistic regression, naive Bayes and k-nearest neighbours tie at 94.5%, and the flexible models do slightly worse. On the moons, k-nearest neighbours (94.5%), the neural network (93.5%) and the random forest (93.0%) lead, and the linear models fall to about 86%. On the circles, logistic regression collapses to 45%, while the neural network (97.5%), k-nearest neighbours (97.0%) and naive Bayes (96.0%) excel. No model wins everywhere, as Wolpert’s theorem predicts, and the fold-to-fold scatter of about five percentage points means that differences of one or two points are not evidence of anything.

In code: cross-validated comparison and tuning in scikit-learn 
from scipy.stats import loguniform
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV, cross_val_score
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=200, noise=0.2, random_state=0)
for model in [LogisticRegression(), RandomForestClassifier(random_state=0)]:
    s = cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=10)
    print(type(model).__name__, s.mean().round(3), s.std().round(3))

# Random search inside cross-validation, wrapped in an outer CV (nested)
search = RandomizedSearchCV(SVC(), {"C": loguniform(1e-2, 1e3),
                                    "gamma": loguniform(1e-3, 1e2)},
                            n_iter=30, cv=5, random_state=0)
print(cross_val_score(search, X, y, cv=5).mean())   # honest estimate

6. Tuning Hyperparameters

Every model has hyperparameters that are set before training: the k of k-nearest neighbours, a tree’s depth, a network’s learning rate and size. The traditional approach, grid search, tries every combination of a few values per hyperparameter. In 2012 James Bergstra and Yoshua Bengio showed that random search, which samples combinations at random, usually finds good settings with far fewer trials. [7] The reason is that typically only a few hyperparameters matter much, and a grid wastes most of its trials repeating the same few values of those.

With 9 trials, a 3 × 3 grid tests only three values of the important hyperparameter and its best score is 0.757; random search beats it in 78% of draws. With 25 trials, the 5 × 5 grid happens to miss the peak again (0.757), and random search wins 99% of the time. Smarter methods go further. Bayesian optimisation fits a model, often a Gaussian process (Part 5), to the scores seen so far and chooses the most promising setting to try next; [8] Hyperband starts many settings with a small budget and gives more training only to the best. [9]

7. The Winner’s Curse

Every time a choice is made by looking at validation scores, whether of a model, a hyperparameter, a feature set or a random seed, some of the winner’s apparent advantage is luck. The more options compared, and the smaller the validation set, the larger the luck.

Among 100 random guessers scored on 50 validation examples, the best typically reaches between 64% and 74%; on fresh data its expected accuracy is 50%, like all the others. Try 1,000 models and the winner can reach 72%. With 1,000 validation examples, the best of 100 shrinks to about 54%. The cure is to keep the final test set out of every decision, and when data are scarce, to use nested cross-validation: an inner loop chooses the hyperparameters, an outer loop evaluates the whole tuning procedure on folds it never saw. Sudhir Varma and Richard Simon showed in 2006 that skipping the outer loop gives seriously optimistic error estimates, [10] and Gavin Cawley and Nicola Talbot showed that overfitting during model selection can matter as much as the choice of algorithm. [11]

Watch out: hidden model selection

Selection happens in more places than hyperparameter search: trying several feature sets, re-running with different seeds, dropping “bad” outliers after seeing results, or iterating on a public benchmark’s test set. Each look spends some of the test set’s honesty. Record every choice, and report how many were tried.

8. Interpretable Models or Explained Black Boxes?

Once a model performs well, the next question is why. There are two strategies. One is to use a model that is interpretable by design: a sparse linear model, a short decision tree, a scoring rule with a few points per factor. The other is to train any model and explain it afterwards with post-hoc explanation methods. Cynthia Rudin argued in 2019 that for high-stakes decisions, such as bail, credit or medical triage, explaining a black box is a poor substitute for a model that can be understood directly, because explanations are approximations that can mislead, and because carefully built interpretable models often lose little or no accuracy on structured data. [12] For images, sound and text, where accurate models are inevitably complex, post-hoc methods are the main tool.

9. Permutation Importance

Which features does a model rely on? Leo Breiman, introducing random forests in 2001, proposed a simple answer: shuffle one feature’s values across the test examples, breaking its link with the outcome, and measure how much the model’s error rises. [13] Permutation importance works for any model and needs no retraining; Aaron Fisher, Cynthia Rudin and Francesca Dominici later extended it to describe how much a whole class of good models could rely on a feature. [14]

The data follow y = 3x1 + 2 sin(2πx2) + 3x3(2x4 − 1) plus noise, with x5 irrelevant and x6 a near-copy of x1. For the true function, shuffling x1 raises the error by 1.45 and shuffling x6 by nothing. The forest, which cannot tell the twins apart, splits on both and gives x1 0.52 and x6 0.21. Correlated features share importance, so each looks less important than the information they carry, and shuffling one creates unrealistic combinations of values. Importance describes how the model uses the features, not how the world works.

10. Partial Dependence and ICE Curves

Importance says how much a feature matters, not how. Jerome Friedman’s partial dependence plot of 2001 sets a feature to each value in turn for every example, keeping the other features as they are, and averages the model’s predictions. [15] In 2015 Alex Goldstein and colleagues proposed plotting the curve for each example separately, individual conditional expectation (ICE) curves, because averages can hide what is happening. [16]

For x2, the average reveals a wave that no single coefficient could describe. For x3, the average curve is almost flat, as if x3 did not matter, yet its permutation importance is far from zero. The ICE curves explain the paradox: they rise for examples with large x4 and fall for those with small x4, and the two cancel. An interaction like this is invisible in the average and plain in the individual curves.

11. Explaining One Prediction: LIME and SHAP

Often what matters is a single decision: why was this loan refused, or this scan flagged? LIME, by Marco Ribeiro, Sameer Singh and Carlos Guestrin, perturbs the example, watches how the model’s predictions change, and fits a simple linear model that mimics the black box nearby. [17] SHAP, by Scott Lundberg and Su-In Lee, rests on a result from game theory: Lloyd Shapley’s 1953 rule for dividing a payout fairly among the players of a cooperative game. [18, 19] The “players” are the features, the “payout” is the difference between this prediction and the average prediction, and each feature’s Shapley value is its average added contribution over all the orders in which the features could join.

For test example 2, with a large x3 (0.92) and a large x4 (0.80), the true function predicts 4.04 against an average of 1.40, and its Shapley values credit x4 with +1.31, x2 with +0.81 and x3 with +0.36. The forest predicts 3.50, with a similar but blurrier story. Shapley values are the only way of dividing the prediction that satisfies a few natural fairness rules, including that the shares add up exactly to the prediction and that a feature the model never uses gets zero. Computing them exactly requires every subset of features, 2d of them, so in practice they are approximated, and the result depends on how the “absent” features are filled in.

The maths: the Shapley value

For features F (d of them), a prediction f(x) and the value v(S) of a subset S, here the average prediction when the features in S take their values from x and the others from background data,

$$\phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq F\setminus\{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(d-|S|-1)!}{d!}\,\big[v(S\cup\{j\}) - v(S)\big], \qquad \sum_j \phi_j = f(x) - v(\varnothing).$$

The weights are the fraction of all orderings of the features in which exactly the features in S come before j.

12. Explaining Neural Networks

For a neural network, the gradient of the output with respect to the input (Part 6) shows which input pixels or words would change the prediction most: a saliency map, as used by Karen Simonyan, Andrea Vedaldi and Andrew Zisserman in 2014. [20] Raw gradients are noisy and can be misleading where the network is saturated; Mukund Sundararajan, Ankur Taly and Qiqi Yan’s integrated gradients instead accumulate gradients along a path from a neutral baseline input to the real one, and, like Shapley values, add up to the change in prediction. [21]

These maps should be tested, not admired. In 2018 Julius Adebayo and colleagues found that some popular saliency methods produced almost the same maps after the network’s weights had been randomised, so the maps reflected the image more than the model. [22] An explanation that does not change when the model changes explains nothing.

13. Right for the Wrong Reasons

Explanations earn their keep by exposing shortcuts: features that predict the label in the training data but for the wrong reason. In an experiment in the LIME paper, Ribeiro and colleagues deliberately trained a classifier to tell wolves from huskies on photographs in which the wolves stood in snow; LIME’s explanations showed it was detecting the snow, and helped people who saw them distrust it. [17] Real shortcuts are found the same way: Sebastian Lapuschkin and colleagues found a well-performing image classifier that recognised horses largely by a copyright tag present on many horse photographs, a modern “Clever Hans”, after the horse that seemed to do arithmetic but was reading its trainer’s cues. [23]

Shortcuts can have serious consequences. Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues found in 2019 that a widely used American health-care algorithm predicted patients’ future health-care costs as a stand-in for their health needs; because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same needs, the algorithm systematically underestimated how ill they were. [24] The model did what it was trained to do; the target was the problem.

14. Beyond Accuracy

  • Calibration: check that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (Part 7).
  • Fairness: compare error rates and calibration across groups of people; aggregate accuracy can hide large gaps.
  • Data shift: the world changes after deployment. Monitor the inputs and the performance, and retrain when they drift.
  • Documentation: Margaret Mitchell and colleagues proposed model cards, short reports stating what a model is for, how it was evaluated, on whom, and where it should not be used. [25]

16. Open Questions

Open questions: can we trust explanations?

Different explanation methods applied to the same model and the same prediction often disagree about which features matter most. Satyapriya Krishna and colleagues documented this “disagreement problem” in 2022, and found that practitioners resolve it largely by intuition. [26] Explanations can also be manipulated: Dylan Slack and colleagues built models that behave in a biased way on real data while LIME and SHAP report innocent-looking explanations. [27] Which explanation is right depends on what question is being asked, about the model, about the data or about the world, and research is still working out how to evaluate explanations, how to explain the reasoning of large language models, and whether such models can explain themselves faithfully.

17. Worked Example: From Data to an Explained Model

  1. Baseline: on the moons, logistic regression scores 85.5% by 10-fold cross-validation.
  2. Candidates: k-nearest neighbours (94.5%), the neural network (93.5%) and the random forest (93.0%) are all clearly better; their differences are within the fold-to-fold scatter of about ±4–5 points.
  3. Tuning: search k and the network size by random search inside each training fold, and evaluate the whole procedure with an outer cross-validation loop.
  4. Final check: retrain the chosen model on all the training data and score it once on a held-out test set.
  5. Explain: on the regression data of Figures 6–8, permutation importance ranks x2 first; partial dependence shows its wave; ICE curves reveal that x3 acts through x4; and Shapley values break individual predictions into contributions that add up exactly.

19. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “if a feature is important to the model, it causes the outcome”

The myth: feature importance and SHAP values reveal which factors drive the outcome, so they tell us what to change.

The reality: they describe the model, which learned associations in its training data. Figure 6 shows a feature that has no effect at all, x6, earning importance because it is correlated with one that does. A feature can matter to a model because it is a proxy, as health-care costs stood in for health needs in the algorithm Obermeyer and colleagues studied, [24] or because of a shortcut such as a copyright tag. [23] Changing an important feature in the real world need not change the outcome.

Why it matters: causal questions (“what happens if we intervene?”) need causal methods and, ideally, experiments, as our series Making Sense of Data discusses. Explanations are for understanding and auditing models.

20. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • No model is best for every problem; start with a simple baseline and models suited to your data type.
  • Compare models with cross-validation on the same folds, and treat small differences with suspicion.
  • Random search beats grid search when only a few hyperparameters matter; Bayesian optimisation and Hyperband are more efficient still.
  • Choosing among many options inflates the winner’s score: keep a test set untouched, or use nested cross-validation.
  • Prefer interpretable models for high-stakes decisions; otherwise use permutation importance, partial dependence with ICE curves, and SHAP or LIME, and sanity-check them.
  • Explanations describe the model, not the world: correlated features share credit, and important features may be proxies or shortcuts.
  • Check calibration, fairness across groups and drift after deployment, and document what the model is for.
