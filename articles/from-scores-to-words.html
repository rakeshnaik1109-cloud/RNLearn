From Scores to Words: Temperature, Sampling and How Language Models Choose

A language model does not output an answer; it outputs a probability for every possible next token. How one token is picked from that list changes whether the result is dull or creative, repetitive or rambling, and even how often it is right.

1. The Last Step

After its tokens have passed through dozens of attention layers (Part 3), a language model produces, for the next position, one number for every token in its vocabulary, perhaps 100,000 or more of them. These raw scores are called logits. Turning them into an actual next token is a separate step, called decoding, and it involves choices that the model itself does not make: how adventurous to be, how much of the improbable tail to allow, and whether to look ahead. The same trained model can be dull or creative, repetitive or erratic, more or less accurate, depending on these choices. [1, 2]

3. From Scores to Probabilities

The softmax turns logits into probabilities: exponentiate each score, so that every value is positive and differences become ratios, then divide by the total so the values sum to 1 (Inside the Machine Part 7). A token whose logit is 1 higher than another’s becomes e ≈ 2.7 times as likely. The model was trained so that these probabilities match how often each token actually followed similar contexts in its training text.

$$P(\text{token}_i) = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}$$

The number T is the temperature. At T = 1 the probabilities are exactly those the model learned. The name comes from physics, by way of Boltzmann machines, where David Ackley, Geoffrey Hinton and Terrence Sejnowski used a temperature to control how random a network’s choices were. [3]

4. Temperature

Dividing every logit by T before the softmax stretches or squashes their differences. Below 1, differences grow and the top token dominates; above 1, they shrink and the distribution flattens. At the extreme T → 0, the model always takes the single most likely token.

With these illustrative scores, the right answer “Canberra” gets probability 0.75 at T = 1 and the common mistake “Sydney” 0.15. At T = 0.3, Canberra rises to 0.995; at T = 2, it falls to 0.42 while Sydney rises to 0.19. Notice that “Sydney” is likely not because the model is confused about geography, but because the phrase “capital … Sydney” is common in text written by people who make the same mistake. Low temperatures are used for factual questions and code; higher ones for brainstorming and fiction.

5. Top-k and Top-p

Even at T = 1, thousands of individually unlikely tokens together hold a noticeable share of the probability, and drawing one of them can derail a sentence. Two common fixes cut off this long tail. Top-k sampling, used by Angela Fan, Mike Lewis and Yann Dauphin for story writing, keeps only the k most likely tokens and renormalises. [4] Top-p, or nucleus sampling, introduced by Ari Holtzman and colleagues, keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, so the number kept adapts: a few when the model is confident, many when it is not. [2] In Figure 3, top-p = 0.9 keeps three tokens, and Canberra’s probability rises to 0.80.

6. Greedy, Sampling and Repetition

Always choosing the most likely token, greedy decoding, sounds safest. For short factual answers it often is. For longer texts it has a strange failure: it tends to repeat itself. Holtzman and colleagues showed that with GPT-2, greedy and beam-search outputs often fell into loops, repeating a phrase again and again, because each repetition makes the next one even more probable; human text, in contrast, is far less predictable, word by word, than what a model generates when it always picks the top choice. [2] Sampling, drawing each token at random from the probabilities, avoids loops but risks rambling.

At temperature 0, all samples are identical: “the boy eats the bread .”, the single most likely path. At temperature 0.5, 142 of 200 samples are different sentences; at 1, 171. As the temperature rises to 2, the average log-probability per word falls from −1.0 to −3.5 and 15% of samples run on for more than 14 words without a full stop. Every decoding choice trades predictability against variety.

7. Beam Search

Greedy decoding is short-sighted: the most likely first word may lead to continuations whose probability is split many ways. Beam search, used since Bruce Lowerre’s Harpy speech recogniser in the 1970s, [5] keeps the few most probable partial sequences at each step and extends all of them, so it can recover from a locally attractive but globally poor choice.

Beam search was the standard for neural machine translation, where a single faithful output is wanted. [6] For open-ended text it brings back the repetition problem, and chat models usually sample instead. Strangely, searching harder for the single most probable output can make things worse: Felix Stahlberg and Bill Byrne found that for many sentences, the most probable translation according to a translation model was the empty translation. [7]

8. Many Samples, One Answer

Randomness can be turned to advantage. For questions with a definite answer, such as a maths problem, a model can be sampled several times, each time reasoning its way to an answer, and the most common final answer chosen. Xuezhi Wang and colleagues called this self-consistency, and found that it raised accuracy substantially on arithmetic and reasoning benchmarks compared with a single greedy answer. [8]

If one sample is right half the time and its errors scatter over three wrong answers, a vote over 9 samples is right about 80% of the time, and over 40 samples 99%. If samples are right only 30% of the time, a 40-sample vote is right about half the time. But if there is a single tempting wrong answer that the model gives 60% of the time, voting reliably picks that wrong answer. Voting amplifies whatever the model is most inclined to say. Other tricks speed decoding up rather than improve it: in speculative decoding, a small fast model drafts several tokens and the large model checks them all at once, producing exactly the large model’s distribution in less time. [9]

9. Stopping and Shaping the Output

Generation stops when the model produces a special end-of-sequence token, which it learned to emit where texts end, or when a length limit is reached. Systems can also constrain decoding, for example allowing only tokens that keep the output valid JSON or a valid answer letter, by setting every other token’s probability to zero before sampling. None of these steps changes the model’s knowledge; they only change which of its possible continuations you see.

In code: temperature and top-p sampling in a few lines 
import numpy as np

def sample_next(logits, temperature=1.0, top_p=1.0, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    if temperature == 0:
        return int(np.argmax(logits))                 # greedy
    z = np.asarray(logits) / temperature
    p = np.exp(z - z.max()); p /= p.sum()             # softmax
    order = np.argsort(-p)
    keep = order[: np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p[order]), top_p) + 1]
    q = np.zeros_like(p); q[keep] = p[keep]; q /= q.sum()   # nucleus
    return int(rng.choice(len(p), p=q))

print(sample_next([9.0, 7.4, 6.0, 5.5], temperature=0.7, top_p=0.9))

10. Open Questions

Open questions: if the most probable text is not the best text, what is the model for?

A language model is trained to give each token its probability in human text. Yet the single most probable text is often bad: repetitive in conversation, sometimes empty in translation. [2, 7] Clara Meister, Ryan Cotterell and Tim Vieira argued that beam search works well precisely because it does not find the most probable output, and that its success reflects a preference, shared by human writers, for spreading information evenly. [10] What exactly makes a text good, and how to decode for it rather than for probability, remains an open research question.

11. Worked Example: Temperature by Hand

Three tokens have logits 2, 1 and 0.

  1. At T = 1: e2 = 7.39, e1 = 2.72, e0 = 1; total 11.11; probabilities 0.67, 0.24, 0.09.
  2. At T = 0.5, divide the logits by 0.5: 4, 2, 0. e4 = 54.6, e2 = 7.39, 1; total 63.0; probabilities 0.87, 0.12, 0.02.
  3. At T = 2: 1, 0.5, 0. e1 = 2.72, e0.5 = 1.65, 1; total 5.37; probabilities 0.51, 0.31, 0.19.
  4. Top-p with p = 0.8 at T = 1 keeps the first two tokens (0.67 + 0.24 = 0.91 ≥ 0.8) and renormalises them to 0.74 and 0.26.

13. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “temperature 0 gives the model’s true, correct answer”

The myth: randomness only adds errors, so the most likely answer at temperature 0 is the model’s best and most correct one.

The reality: temperature 0 makes an answer repeatable, not right. The single most likely continuation can be a common misconception, a repetitive loop or, in translation, nothing at all. [2, 7] And sampling can make a model more accurate: Wang and colleagues found that voting over many sampled reasoning paths beat greedy decoding on a range of reasoning tasks. [8]

Why it matters: the reliability of an answer comes from the model’s knowledge, not from the decoding setting. Asking several times and checking agreement is a cheap way to gauge how confident it really is (Part 6).

14. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • The model outputs a score (logit) for every token; the softmax turns scores into probabilities.
  • Temperature sharpens (below 1) or flattens (above 1) the distribution; top-k and top-p cut off the unlikely tail.
  • Greedy decoding is repeatable but can loop; sampling is varied but can ramble.
  • Beam search looks ahead to find more probable sequences, but the most probable text is not always the best text.
  • Sampling several answers and voting improves accuracy when the right answer is more likely than any single wrong one.
  • Decoding changes which of the model’s possible continuations you see, not what the model knows.
