The p–n Junction: Diodes, LEDs and Solar Cells

Press p-type silicon against n-type and something remarkable happens: a one-way valve for current appears, which can also turn electricity into light and light into electricity.

At a glance

In this part: the p–n junction, where p-type and n-type silicon meet. Diffusion builds a depletion region and a built-in voltage; applied voltage raises or lowers the barrier, giving the diode’s exponential one-way current (the Shockley equation); strong reverse fields cause breakdown. Then the uses: rectifiers, voltage references, LEDs, photodiodes and solar cells.

You need: Part 5 (bands, doping, the Fermi level) and Part 4 (circuits).

1. A One-Way Street for Current

A diode lets current flow easily in one direction and hardly at all in the other. The first electronic diodes were vacuum tubes: in 1904 John Ambrose Fleming patented a valve in which a heated filament emitted electrons towards a plate, so current could flow only one way, to detect radio signals. [1] The semiconductor version is far smaller and needs no heater: a single crystal that is p-type on one side and n-type on the other. The p–n junction is the simplest semiconductor device, and understanding it is the key to the transistors of Parts 7 and 8, to LEDs and to solar cells. [2, 3]

3. Making a Junction

Imagine joining a piece of p-type silicon, full of holes, to a piece of n-type silicon, full of electrons. Carriers diffuse down their concentration gradients: electrons spill into the p side and holes into the n side, where they meet and recombine. They leave behind the dopant atoms they came from, which are fixed in the lattice: positively charged donor ions on the n side and negatively charged acceptor ions on the p side. This thin zone, emptied of mobile carriers, is the depletion region.

The exposed ions create an electric field pointing from n to p, which pushes back against further diffusion. Equilibrium is reached when the field’s pull exactly balances diffusion’s push. In the band picture of Part 5, the bands bend across the junction until the Fermi level is flat, leaving an energy step that electrons on the n side must climb to reach the p side. William Shockley worked out the theory in 1949. [4]

4. The Built-in Voltage and the Depletion Width

The height of the step is the built-in voltage

Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni²)

about 0.7–0.9 V for typical silicon junctions. Solving Gauss’s law for the slabs of fixed charge gives the depletion width W = √[2ε(Vbi − V)/q × (1/NA + 1/ND)], typically a fraction of a micrometre. Because the two sides must hold equal and opposite charge (NAxp = NDxn), the region extends mostly into the more lightly doped side.

The depletion region is an insulating layer between two conductors, so a junction is also a capacitor, C = εA/W. Reverse voltage widens W and lowers C. Diodes designed to exploit this, called varactors, tune radio and phone circuits by voltage alone.

5. Forward and Reverse Bias: the Diode Equation

Applying a voltage V with the p side positive (forward bias) lowers the barrier to Vbi − V. The number of carriers energetic enough to cross depends on the Boltzmann factor, so the current grows exponentially. Reversing the voltage (reverse bias) raises the barrier, and only the trickle of minority carriers that happen to wander into the field is swept across. Shockley’s diode equation combines both: [4]

I = Is(eqV/nkT − 1)

The saturation current Is is tiny (around 10−14 A for a small silicon diode) and the ideality factor n lies between 1 and 2. Since kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV, each tenfold increase in current needs only about 60 mV more. A silicon diode therefore looks like a switch that “turns on” at roughly 0.6–0.7 V, although in truth it never fully switches off or on.

6. Breakdown: Zener, Avalanche and Tunnelling

Under enough reverse voltage a junction suddenly conducts heavily. This breakdown is not damage if the current is limited, and it happens in two ways. In heavily doped junctions the depletion region is so thin and the field so strong that electrons tunnel directly from the valence band on one side to the conduction band on the other, a process Clarence Zener described in 1934. [5] In lightly doped junctions, carriers accelerated by the field gain enough energy to knock new electron–hole pairs free, which are accelerated in turn: an avalanche. Zener diodes, designed to break down at a precise voltage, are used as voltage references.

In 1958 Leo Esaki, working at the company that became Sony, made junctions so heavily doped that electrons tunnelled through even under small forward bias, giving a region where current falls as voltage rises. [6] The tunnel diode won him a share of the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physics, and tunnelling returns in Part 11 as a source of leakage in tiny transistors.

7. Rectifiers and Other Uses

The most common job for diodes is rectification: turning alternating current into direct current. One diode keeps one half of each cycle; a bridge of four keeps both. A capacitor then smooths the bumps, charging at each peak and supplying the load in between. Diodes also protect circuits from reversed batteries and voltage spikes, mix and detect radio signals, and measure temperature through the roughly −2 mV/K change of their forward voltage.

8. Light In, Light Out: LEDs, Photodiodes and Solar Cells

When an electron falls from the conduction band into a hole, it can release its energy as a photon of energy close to the band gap. In silicon this is rare, because of the shape of its bands (an indirect gap), but in direct-gap materials such as gallium arsenide it is efficient. A forward-biased junction in such a material is a light-emitting diode. In 1962 Nick Holonyak made the first practical visible LED, glowing red, from gallium arsenide phosphide. [7] Bright blue LEDs, which make white light possible, arrived only in the 1990s, when Shuji Nakamura and others mastered gallium nitride; Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Nakamura shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics for them. [8]

The process also runs backwards. A photon absorbed in or near the depletion region creates an electron–hole pair, and the built-in field separates them, producing a current. Run with reverse bias, the junction is a fast photodiode, the sensor in fibre-optic receivers. Run with no external supply, it is a solar cell, delivering power. Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller and Gerald Pearson demonstrated a practical silicon solar cell at Bell Labs in 1954, at about 6% efficiency. [9] In 1961 Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser showed that a single junction under sunlight cannot exceed about 30%, because photons below the gap are lost and the energy of photons above it is partly wasted as heat. [10] The best laboratory silicon cells now exceed 26%. [11]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): built-in voltage

A silicon junction has NA = 1018 cm−3 and ND = 1016 cm−3. Find Vbi at 300 K (kT/q = 0.0259 V, ni = 1010 cm−3).

Solution

Vbi = 0.0259 × ln(1034/1020) = 0.0259 × ln(1014) = 0.0259 × 32.2 ≈ 0.83 V.

Problem 2 (easy): diode voltage

An ideal diode (n = 1) has Is = 10−14 A. What forward voltage gives 1 mA? And 10 mA?

Solution

V = (kT/q) ln(I/Is + 1) ≈ 0.0259 × ln(1011) = 0.0259 × 25.3 ≈ 0.66 V. For 10 mA, add 0.0259 × ln 10 ≈ 0.060 V: 0.72 V. A tenfold change in current changes the voltage by under 10%, which is why “0.7 V” is a good rule of thumb.

Problem 3 (medium): depletion width and capacitance

For the junction in Problem 1 (ε = 1.04 × 10−12 F/cm), estimate the depletion width at zero bias and at 5 V reverse bias, and the change in capacitance.

Solution

Since NA ≫ ND, the junction is one-sided: W ≈ √[2ε(Vbi − V)/(qND)]. At 0 V: √[2 × 1.04 × 10−12 × 0.83 / (1.6 × 10−19 × 1016)] ≈ 3.3 × 10−5 cm = 0.33 µm, almost entirely on the n side. At −5 V, W grows by √(5.83/0.83) ≈ 2.65 to 0.87 µm. Capacitance per area C = ε/W falls from about 31 to 12 nF/cm²: a voltage-tunable capacitor.

Problem 4 (medium): LED colours

Estimate the emission wavelength of LEDs with band gaps of 1.9 eV (GaAsP), 2.7 eV (InGaN) and 3.4 eV (GaN). Roughly what forward voltage does each need?

Solution

λ ≈ 1240/Eg nm: 1.9 eV → 650 nm (red); 2.7 eV → 460 nm (blue); 3.4 eV → 365 nm (ultraviolet). Each emitted photon needs an electron raised by about Eg, so the forward voltage is at least about Eg/q: roughly 2 V for red and 3 V for blue LEDs, as on a datasheet.

Problem 5 (harder): solar cell performance

A silicon cell under full sun (100 mW/cm²) produces JL = 40 mA/cm² and has J0 = 10−12 A/cm². Find the open-circuit voltage and estimate the maximum power and efficiency.

Solution

At open circuit J = 0, so Voc = (kT/q) ln(JL/J0 + 1) = 0.0259 × ln(4 × 1010) ≈ 0.63 V. Maximising P = V × J(V) numerically (or by the approximation Vm ≈ Voc − (kT/q) ln(1 + qVm/kT)) gives Vm ≈ 0.55 V and Jm ≈ 38 mA/cm², so Pmax ≈ 21 mW/cm²: an efficiency of about 21% and a fill factor Pmax/(JLVoc) ≈ 0.83. Real cells lose a little more to series resistance and reflection.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: beating the single-junction limit

The Shockley–Queisser limit applies to one junction absorbing sunlight. Stacking junctions with different band gaps (tandem cells) captures more of the spectrum: perovskite-on-silicon tandems have passed the single-junction limit in the laboratory. Whether perovskites can be made stable for 25 years outdoors, and manufactured cheaply at scale, is unresolved. For LEDs, the open problem is the “efficiency droop”: gallium nitride LEDs become less efficient at high current, and the relative roles of Auger recombination and other mechanisms were debated for years. Green LEDs also remain less efficient than blue or red, the so-called “green gap”.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a diode switches on at 0.7 V”

The myth: below 0.7 V a silicon diode is off; above it, it conducts.

The reality: the current is exponential at every voltage. At 0.5 V a small diode passes microamps; at 0.7 V, milliamps; at 0.8 V, tens of milliamps. “0.7 V” is simply where the current reaches the milliamp levels typical of circuits. The same exponential appears in transistors and is the reason they cannot be switched fully off at low voltage (Part 8).

Why it matters: treating devices as ideal switches is useful, but the exponential physics sets the limits of low-power electronics.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • At a p–n junction, diffusion leaves a depletion region of fixed ions whose field balances further diffusion.
  • Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni²); the depletion region extends mostly into the lighter-doped side and widens with reverse bias.
  • The diode equation I = Is(eqV/nkT − 1): about 60 mV per decade of current at room temperature.
  • Strong reverse fields cause Zener (tunnelling) or avalanche breakdown, used for voltage references.
  • Diodes rectify AC; direct-gap junctions emit light (LEDs); illuminated junctions generate current (photodiodes, solar cells).
