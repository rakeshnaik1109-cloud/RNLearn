Mathematics Becomes Infinite: Calculus, Rigour, and the Limits of Reason

How human intellect conquered the infinitesimal, dared to tame non-Euclidean curved space and transfinite infinities, sought absolute certainty in formal axioms, and discovered the inescapable horizons of what can be proven and computed.

1. Calculus Works, but Nobody Knows Why

In the late 1600s mathematics made a huge leap: calculus, the mathematics of change. Working separately, Isaac Newton (1642–1727) in England and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) in Germany discovered that two basic problems are opposites of each other. One is finding how steep a curve is at a single point (its slope, or rate of change). The other is finding the area under a curve. Undoing one gives you the other. This is the fundamental theorem of calculus. [6, 4]

With this tool, Newton worked out the orbits of the planets, and Leibniz and the Bernoulli brothers solved problems such as the shape of a hanging chain and the vibrations of a violin string. But underneath there was an embarrassing gap. Neither Newton nor Leibniz could say clearly what their key ingredient, the "infinitely small" quantity, actually was.

Leibniz called these quantities differentials, written $dx$: smaller than any ordinary number, yet not zero. Newton talked of tiny, vanishing pieces of time. To find the slope of the curve $y = x^2$, they would do this:

$$\frac{(x + dx)^2 - x^2}{dx} = \frac{2x\,dx + (dx)^2}{dx} = 2x + dx$$

To divide by $dx$, it can't be zero, because you can't divide by zero. But then, to get the final answer $2x$, they simply threw away the leftover $dx$, treating it as zero. How can something be not-zero in one step and zero in the next?

In 1734 the Irish philosopher and bishop George Berkeley (1685–1753) pounced on this in a pamphlet called The Analyst, aimed at a "mathematician who doubted religion" (thought to be the astronomer Edmond Halley). Mathematicians mocked religion for asking people to believe in mysteries, Berkeley said, yet their own subject rested on something just as mysterious:

And what are these fluxions? The velocities of evanescent increments? And what are these same evanescent increments? They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?

Berkeley argued that calculus got the right answers only because errors happened to cancel out. For over a century mathematicians largely ignored him, because calculus worked so well in practice. But the problem at its foundations hadn't gone away.

2. Who Invented Calculus? Newton against Leibniz

Making things worse was one of the bitterest quarrels in the history of science: Newton and Leibniz's fight over who invented calculus first. [4]

Newton had developed his "method of fluxions" in 1665–1666, during the plague years at Woolsthorpe (Part 6). But he hated criticism and was slow to publish. He showed handwritten papers to a few friends, and when his great book the Principia appeared in 1687, he mostly hid his calculus behind old-style geometry.

Leibniz developed his own version independently in Paris in 1675, and published it first, in 1684. He also invented the notation we still use: the long S, $\int$, for adding up areas (from the Latin summa, "sum"), and $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for a rate of change.

By the 1690s English mathematicians were accusing Leibniz of stealing Newton's ideas. In 1712 the Royal Society set up a supposedly fair committee to decide. In fact Newton, the Society's president, chose its members, wrote its report himself, and then anonymously reviewed the report, praising its fairness. Historians today agree that both men discovered calculus independently.

The quarrel hurt British mathematics. Out of national pride, British mathematicians stuck with Newton's awkward dot notation and cut themselves off from Europe, where Euler, Lagrange and the Bernoullis used Leibniz's far handier notation and raced ahead. Britain fell behind for about a century, until in 1812 a group of Cambridge students, including Charles Babbage and John Herschel, formed the Analytical Society to bring in the continental methods (Babbage joked about replacing Newton's "dot-age" with Leibniz's "d-ism").

3. Making Calculus Solid: Cauchy, Bolzano and Limits

In the early 1800s mathematicians began demanding rigour: proofs with no gaps. In the 1700s Euler and others had handled infinite sums boldly, sometimes writing things like $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \dots = \frac{1}{2}$. But as calculus was applied to harder problems, such as Joseph Fourier's work on how heat spreads, careless methods started giving contradictions. [5, 4]

In 1821 the French mathematician Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857) published a textbook, the Cours d'Analyse, insisting that calculus be built on numbers and careful reasoning, not on pictures or physical intuition.

His key tool was the limit, an idea earlier suggested by Jean d'Alembert. Instead of mysterious "infinitely small" numbers, Cauchy talked about quantities that get closer and closer to a value:

When the values successively attributed to the same variable approach indefinitely to a fixed value, up to the point of differing from it as little as one wishes, this fixed value is called the limit of all the others.

The slope of a curve is then defined as a limit: take two points on the curve, work out the slope of the straight line through them, and see what value this approaches as the second point moves closer and closer to the first (Figure 1 shows this). You never actually divide by zero.

Around the same time in Prague, a priest and mathematician, Bernard Bolzano (1781–1848), was working along similar lines. In 1817 he proved what is now called the intermediate value theorem: if an unbroken curve starts below a line and ends above it, it must cross the line somewhere. For two thousand years this had seemed too obvious to need proof. Bolzano realised that "obvious from a picture" isn't a proof, and showed it follows from the properties of numbers.

4. Getting Rid of "Infinitely Small": Weierstrass and Dedekind

Cauchy's definition still talked about things "approaching" and "getting closer", words that suggest motion. In Berlin in the 1860s, Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897) removed the last traces of motion and replaced them with precise statements about inequalities, the famous epsilon–delta definition. [10, 4]

The easiest way to understand it is as a game. To claim that a curve approaches a value $L$ at some point, you must win this challenge: your opponent chooses any small margin of error, called epsilon ($\epsilon$), as small as they like. You must then find a small enough distance, called delta ($\delta$), so that whenever you stay within delta of the point, the curve stays within epsilon of $L$. If you can always win, however small the epsilon, the limit is $L$. Figure 2 lets you play.

There are no "infinitely small" quantities and no motion, just a precise challenge and response. Weierstrass also shocked mathematicians in 1872 with a curve that is unbroken everywhere but so jagged that it has no slope anywhere at all, like a coastline that is crinkly at every scale. Intuition could no longer be trusted.

But this still depended on another question: what exactly is a real number? Fractions were well understood, but what about numbers like √2 or π, which can't be written as fractions (Part 4)? In 1872 Richard Dedekind (1831–1916) gave an answer.

Imagine all the fractions laid out along a line. They are packed tightly together, yet there are gaps: for example, there is no fraction whose square is exactly 2. Dedekind's idea was to "cut" the line of fractions into two groups, everything on the left and everything on the right. Sometimes the cut falls at a fraction, but sometimes it falls in a gap, as with the cut between fractions whose square is less than 2 and those whose square is more than 2. Dedekind defined √2 as that cut. Filling in every such gap gives the real numbers: a complete line with no holes, on which calculus could at last stand firmly.

5. Parallel Lines That Meet: New Geometries

While calculus was being made solid, an even bigger shock hit geometry. For over two thousand years, Euclid's Elements (Part 4) had been the model of certain knowledge, building all of geometry from a few starting rules. [4]

But one rule had always bothered mathematicians: the fifth postulate, or parallel postulate. The other rules were short and obvious; this one was long and awkward. It is easiest to understand in a version later given by John Playfair: through a point not on a line, there is exactly one line that never meets the first line (a parallel).

For two thousand years, from Ptolemy to Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam (Part 4) and the Italian priest Girolamo Saccheri, mathematicians tried to prove it from the other rules. Saccheri, in 1733, assumed it was false and tried to reach a contradiction. He derived many strange results but no contradiction, and finally declared, more out of prejudice than proof, that his strange results were "repugnant to the nature of the straight line".

Three people realised, separately, that Saccheri hadn't found a mistake. He had glimpsed a whole new geometry:

  • Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855) became convinced in the early 1800s that a geometry in which many parallels pass through one point made perfect sense. But he kept it to himself, fearing an outcry from philosophers who, following Kant, believed Euclid's geometry was a necessary truth.
  • Nikolai Lobachevsky (1792–1856), head of the university at Kazan in Russia, published his version in 1829. In this hyperbolic geometry, infinitely many parallels pass through a point; the angles of a triangle always add up to less than 180°; and the bigger the triangle, the smaller the total.
  • János Bolyai (1802–1860), a young Hungarian army engineer, found the same geometry around 1823 and wrote to his father: "Out of nothing I have created a strange new universe." When his father sent the work to Gauss, Gauss replied that he couldn't praise it, because that would mean praising himself, since he had found the same results years before. Bolyai was crushed and never published on mathematics again.

In 1868 the Italian mathematician Eugenio Beltrami showed that hyperbolic geometry is just as free of contradiction as Euclid's, by finding a curved surface (shaped a bit like a trumpet) on which it holds. Geometry was no longer simply "the truth about space". It became a family of possible systems, and which one fits the real universe became a question for measurement.

6. Curved Space: Riemann

On 10 June 1854, the shy, unwell 27-year-old Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) gave a qualifying lecture at Göttingen. In the audience sat his former teacher, the 77-year-old Gauss. [7, 4]

The lecture, "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry", is often called one of the greatest in the history of mathematics. Using almost no formulas, Riemann brought all the geometries together in one general idea: a space, which he called a manifold, could have any number of dimensions and could be curved in different ways at different places.

The key is that the geometry is decided from the inside: by a rule for measuring distances between nearby points, which can vary from place to place. You don't need to step outside the space to know it is curved. An ant living on the surface of a ball could discover the curve by measuring triangles. Figure 4 shows another sign: carry an arrow around a loop on a curved surface, always keeping it "pointing the same way", and it comes back pointing in a different direction.

Riemann's ideas also describe a third geometry, the geometry of a sphere:

  • There are no parallel lines at all: all "straight lines" on a sphere (great circles, like the Equator) meet.
  • The angles of a triangle add up to more than 180°. (Try it on a globe: a triangle from the North Pole down to two points on the Equator a quarter of the way round has three right angles, 270° in total.)

Riemann ended with a remarkable suggestion: the geometry of real space might not be fixed in advance, but might depend on the matter and forces in it. Sixty-one years later, Einstein used Riemann's mathematics to build general relativity (Part 10), in which matter curves spacetime.

7. Comparing Infinities: Cantor

For more than two thousand years, from Aristotle to Galileo and Gauss, mathematicians avoided treating infinity as a finished thing. Aristotle distinguished between potential infinity (a process that can go on forever, like counting 1, 2, 3...) and actual infinity (a completed infinite collection). Actual infinity was thought to lead to nonsense. [1]

Galileo noticed a puzzle in 1638. You can pair every whole number with its square: 1 with 1, 2 with 4, 3 with 9, and so on, with none left over on either side. So there seem to be as many squares as whole numbers, even though most whole numbers aren't squares. How can the part be as big as the whole? Gauss wrote in 1831: "I protest against the use of an infinite quantity as an actual entity; this is never allowed in mathematics."

From 1874, a German mathematician born in St Petersburg, Georg Cantor (1845–1918), working at the University of Halle, took infinity seriously and founded set theory. To compare the sizes of infinite collections, he used a simple idea.

Two collections are the same size if you can pair them up exactly, with nothing left over on either side. A shepherd who can't count can still check that every sheep has come home: drop a pebble in a bowl for each sheep that goes out in the morning, and take one out for each sheep that comes back in the evening.

By pairing, Cantor showed that there are as many even numbers as whole numbers (pair 1 with 2, 2 with 4, 3 with 6...). Even more surprising, he showed in 1874 that the fractions can be paired with the whole numbers too. A simple way to see it: lay out all the fractions in a grid (1/1, 1/2, 1/3... in the first row; 2/1, 2/2, 2/3... in the second, and so on) and zigzag through it diagonally, giving each fraction a number in turn. Every fraction gets reached eventually. Collections that can be paired with the counting numbers like this are called countably infinite, and Cantor gave their size a name: aleph-null, written $\aleph_0$.

8. Some Infinities Are Bigger: The Diagonal Argument

If even the fractions can be counted, are all infinities the same size? In 1873, and in a famous paper of 1891, Cantor showed the astonishing answer: no. Some infinities are bigger than others. [1]

He proved that the real numbers (all the decimals) between 0 and 1 can't be paired with the counting numbers. There are too many of them. The proof, the famous diagonal argument, is short enough to follow.

Suppose someone claims to have a complete numbered list of all the decimals between 0 and 1. Write them out one under another:

1st: 0.3141592...
2nd: 0.7825018...
3rd: 0.4592371...
4th: 0.1036842...
and so on forever.

Now build a new decimal by going down the diagonal (the 1st digit of the 1st number, the 2nd digit of the 2nd number, and so on) and changing every one of those digits: if it's a 5, write 6; otherwise write 5. Here that gives 0.5555...

Is this new number on the list? It can't be the 1st number, because it differs from it in the 1st digit. It can't be the 2nd, because it differs in the 2nd digit. In general it can't be the n-th number, because it differs from it in the n-th digit. So it isn't anywhere on the list, and the "complete" list wasn't complete after all. Since this works for any list, no list can contain all the decimals. The real numbers are a bigger infinity than the counting numbers; they are uncountable.

Cantor went further and showed that for any collection, the collection of all its possible sub-groups is always bigger. So there is no biggest infinity: there is an endless ladder of ever-larger infinities.

This led to a famous question, the continuum hypothesis: is there an infinity in between the counting numbers and the real numbers? Cantor spent decades trying to prove there isn't. The answer turned out to be stranger than he imagined: in 1940 Kurt Gödel showed it can't be disproved from the standard rules of set theory, and in 1963 Paul Cohen showed it can't be proved from them either. With the usual rules, the question simply can't be settled.

9. A Paradox at the Foundations: Russell

Meanwhile, the German logician Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) had spent years on a grand project: showing that all of arithmetic follows from pure logic. His two-volume work, the Basic Laws of Arithmetic, assumed that for any property, there is a set of all the things that have it. [4]

In June 1902, just as Frege's second volume was going to print, the young British philosopher Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) sent him a short, polite and devastating letter. He asked Frege to think about the set of all sets that are not members of themselves.

Most sets aren't members of themselves: the set of all teacups is not itself a teacup. So call the set of all such sets R. Is R a member of itself?

  • If R is a member of itself, then it fails the condition for being in R (it isn't a set that excludes itself), so it isn't a member.
  • If R isn't a member of itself, then it meets the condition, so it is a member.

Either way we get a contradiction. This is Russell's paradox. A popular version is the barber puzzle: in a village, the barber shaves all the men who don't shave themselves, and only them. Who shaves the barber? Frege was crushed. In an appendix to his book he wrote:

Hardly anything more unwelcome can befall a scientific writer than that one of the foundations of his edifice be shaken after the work is finished.

To avoid such paradoxes, Ernst Zermelo and Abraham Fraenkel later set out more careful rules for building sets (called ZFC), which ban the troublesome kinds of collection. But a worry remained: how could anyone be sure that even the new rules didn't hide some contradiction?

10. Hilbert's Dream: Mathematics Without Doubt

In August 1900, at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris, the leading mathematician of the day, David Hilbert (1862–1943), presented a list of 23 great unsolved problems for the new century. They guided mathematical research for generations. The first was Cantor's continuum hypothesis; the second was to prove that the rules of arithmetic can never lead to a contradiction. [4]

Some mathematicians, such as Henri Poincaré and the Dutchman L. E. J. Brouwer, distrusted Cantor's infinities. Hilbert defended them: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created for us."

To put mathematics beyond doubt, Hilbert proposed a plan, now called Hilbert's programme. Write all of mathematics as a precise system of symbols and rules, like a game, and then prove three things about it:

  1. Completeness: every mathematical statement can be either proved or disproved from the rules.
  2. Consistency: the rules can never lead to a contradiction (such as proving 0 = 1), and this can be shown using simple, safe methods.
  3. Decidability: there is a mechanical method that can decide, in a finite number of steps, whether any statement follows from the rules.

In 1930, retiring in his home town of Königsberg, Hilbert ended a famous speech with words later carved on his gravestone: "Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen." ("We must know. We will know.")

11. Gödel: True but Unprovable

The day before Hilbert's speech, at a meeting in the same city, a shy 24-year-old Austrian logician named Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) quietly mentioned something he had proved: in any consistent system of rules for arithmetic, there are true statements that can't be proved. [2, 8]

He published the full proof in 1931. It ended Hilbert's dream.

Gödel's key trick was to give every symbol, statement and proof a code number. That let statements about arithmetic be written as statements in arithmetic. Using this, he built a statement, call it G, that in effect says:

"This statement cannot be proved."

Now ask: can G be proved?

  • If G can be proved, then the system has proved something false (since G says it can't be proved). A system that proves false things is inconsistent, which is useless.
  • If G can't be proved, then what it says is true. So there is a true statement the system can't prove: the system is incomplete.

This is Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: any consistent system of rules strong enough to do basic arithmetic contains true statements that it can't prove. Adding the unprovable statement as a new rule doesn't help, because the new system will have its own unprovable truths.

His second incompleteness theorem went further: such a system can't prove its own consistency. To prove arithmetic consistent you would need a stronger system, whose own consistency is even less certain. Hilbert's hope of proving mathematics complete and contradiction-free, using only safe methods, was impossible. In mathematics, truth goes beyond proof.

12. What Computers Can't Do: Turing

Gödel had disposed of completeness and consistency, but Hilbert's third question remained: is there a mechanical method for deciding whether any statement follows from the rules? [3, 9]

In 1936 a 24-year-old Cambridge mathematician, Alan Turing (1912–1954), answered it, and in doing so laid the foundations of computer science. (The American logician Alonzo Church reached the same answer by a different route the same year.) To answer the question, Turing first had to say exactly what a "mechanical method" is.

He imagined a very simple machine, now called a Turing machine:

  • A long tape divided into squares, each holding a symbol (such as 0, 1 or blank).
  • A head that reads one square at a time, can write a new symbol there, and can move one square left or right.
  • A "state", a note of what stage the machine is at.
  • A short table of rules saying, for each state and symbol, what to write, which way to move and which state to go to next.

That's all. Yet Turing argued that anything that can be calculated by following rules can be calculated by such a machine. And he showed that one machine, the universal Turing machine, can imitate any other, if you give it the other machine's rules written on its tape. That is exactly the idea of a computer that runs programs stored in its memory.

Turing then asked a question now called the halting problem: could there be a program that looks at any other program, and the input it is given, and correctly tells you whether that program will eventually stop or run forever?

He proved there can't be. Suppose such a checker existed. Then you could write a contrary program that uses the checker on itself and does the opposite of whatever the checker predicts: if the checker says "you'll stop", it loops forever; if the checker says "you'll run forever", it stops at once. Whatever the checker says about this program is wrong. So no such checker can exist. Some questions are undecidable: no computer, however powerful, can ever answer them in general.

The search for perfectly certain foundations had led, unexpectedly, to the discovery of firm limits: there are truths that can't be proved and questions that can't be computed.

13. How Did They Know? Proving Something Can't Be Done

How did they know? The power of the diagonal argument

How can you prove that something is impossible, that no list of decimals could ever be complete, without checking every possible list, of which there are infinitely many? [1]

Cantor's answer was to give a recipe that defeats any list you are handed:

  1. Accept the challenge: suppose someone hands you what they say is a complete list of every decimal between 0 and 1, numbered 1st, 2nd, 3rd and so on.
  2. Read down the diagonal: look at the 1st digit of the 1st number, the 2nd digit of the 2nd number, the 3rd digit of the 3rd number, and so on.
  3. Change every diagonal digit: build a new decimal whose n-th digit is 4 if the diagonal digit is not 4, and 5 if it is 4. (Using only 4s and 5s avoids the one awkward case, that 0.4999... and 0.5000... are the same number.)
  4. Check: the new decimal differs from the 1st number in its 1st digit, from the 2nd number in its 2nd digit, and from every n-th number in its n-th digit. So it can't be anywhere on the list.

Because the recipe works for every possible list, no list can ever be complete. The same "do the opposite of the diagonal" trick reappears in Gödel's unprovable statement and in Turing's halting problem: each builds something that differs from every item it is compared with.

14. Legend and Record: Did Infinity Drive Cantor Mad?

Legend and Record: Cantor's illness and his critics

A popular story makes Cantor a tragic figure who stared too long into infinity, lost his mind and died in an asylum, as if punished for his daring. The real story is sadder and more human. [1]

His illness: Cantor was treated many times at a psychiatric clinic in Halle. Most historians think he had what we would now call bipolar disorder (manic depression). That is a diagnosis made long after his death, but it points to an illness, not to the mathematics he was working on. His depressions came and went, and were sometimes triggered by grief, such as the sudden death of his twelve-year-old son Rudolph in 1899.

His critics: what made things harder was hostility from some leading mathematicians, above all his former teacher in Berlin, Leopold Kronecker (1823–1891). Kronecker accepted only whole numbers and what can be built from them in finite steps ("God made the whole numbers; all else is the work of man," he is reported to have said), and he regarded Cantor's infinities as dangerous nonsense. He is said to have delayed Cantor's papers and to have called him a "corrupter of youth". Cantor believed Kronecker blocked his hopes of a post in Berlin, and he stayed at the smaller university of Halle all his life. Infinity didn't break Cantor; illness, grief and professional hostility weighed far more heavily.

15. Philosophy Corner: Is Mathematics Discovered or Invented?

Philosophy Corner: three views of mathematics

The crisis over the foundations of mathematics split mathematicians into three main camps, which we first met in Part 4: [4]

  • Platonism: held by Cantor and Gödel, among others. Numbers, sets and infinities really exist, independently of us, outside space and time. Mathematicians discover truths rather than inventing them. Platonists see Gödel's theorems as showing that mathematical truth is bigger than any system of proof.
  • Formalism: associated with Hilbert. Mathematics needn't be "about" anything; it is a precise game played with symbols according to agreed rules, like chess. A statement is "true" if it can be reached from the starting rules by legal moves.
  • Intuitionism: led by Brouwer. Mathematics is a creation of the human mind, and something exists only if we can actually construct it in a finite number of steps. Intuitionists rejected completed infinities, and even the rule that every statement is either true or false.

The argument has never been settled. As Philip Davis and Reuben Hersh joked, most working mathematicians are Platonists on weekdays and formalists on Sundays: they work as if their numbers and shapes are real, but retreat to "it's just a game of rules" when asked what those objects really are.
