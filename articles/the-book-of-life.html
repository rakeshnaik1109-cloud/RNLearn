1. A Fixed Ladder of Life, and Linnaeus's Catalogue

For more than two thousand years, people in the West pictured living things not as a family tree that changes over time, but as a fixed ladder: the Great Chain of Being. It went back to Aristotle and was developed by medieval thinkers. Minerals stood on the bottom rung, then plants, then simple animals, then more complex animals, then humans, angels and finally God. Every rung was thought to be permanently filled. No species could ever die out, because that would leave a gap in God's creation, and no new species could appear, because creation was already complete. [2]

Living things are amazingly well suited to their lives: the hawk's sharp claws, the duck's webbed feet, the eye's lens. For centuries this was taken as proof of a wise Designer. In 1691 the English clergyman and naturalist John Ray set out this view in The Wisdom of God Manifested in the Works of the Creation, and this "natural theology" dominated European biology until the mid-1800s.

Meanwhile, as European trade and empires spread, thousands of unfamiliar plants and animals poured into European collections: strange plants from southern Africa, pouched mammals from Australia and the Pacific, brilliant birds from the Amazon. Naming them was chaos. Plants were often described with long Latin phrases, such as Ranunculus foliis ovatis serratis caule erecto simplici ("buttercup with oval, toothed leaves and a single upright stem").

The Swedish doctor and botanist Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778) brought order to this. In his book Systema Naturae (first published in 1735 as a short pamphlet, and grown by its twelfth edition into three volumes), he introduced two tools still used today:

  1. Two-part names (used consistently from 1753 for plants and 1758 for animals): every species gets a two-word Latin name: its genus (with a capital letter) and its species (lower case), such as Homo sapiens (humans), Canis lupus (wolf) or Quercus alba (white oak).
  2. Groups within groups: species are grouped into genera, genera into orders, orders into classes, and classes into kingdoms, based on shared features.

Linnaeus sorted plants by counting their male and female parts (stamens and pistils). This system was artificial and sometimes lumped unrelated plants together. But his groups-within-groups had an unexpected consequence. Life no longer looked like a single ladder. It looked like a branching pattern, very like a family tree, waiting for someone to explain why.

2. The Earth Is Very Old: Cuvier, Lyell and Deep Time

Before anyone could imagine species changing, people had to realise two things: the Earth has a very long history, and most species that ever lived are gone. [9, 2]

Until the late 1700s, most Europeans dated the Earth from the Bible. In 1650 the Irish archbishop James Ussher famously calculated that creation happened on the evening before Sunday 23 October 4004 BCE. With only about six thousand years of history, there was no time for slow change in living things. Many also found it unthinkable that God would let a created species disappear. When huge fossil bones turned up in Siberia or North America, people such as Thomas Jefferson suggested that living mammoths and mastodons might still be roaming the unexplored American West.

This comforting idea was overturned in Paris by the expert anatomist Georges Cuvier (1769–1832). Comparing fossil teeth and jawbones with the bones of living elephants, he showed that the Siberian mammoth and the American mastodon were different from both Asian and African elephants.

Animals that big couldn't be hiding anywhere people lived, so Cuvier concluded that a whole world of creatures had vanished. Extinction was real. Digging through the rock layers around Paris with the geologist Alexandre Brongniart, he also found a clear pattern called faunal succession: the deeper (older) the layer, the less its fossils resembled living animals. Old layers held creatures such as sea reptiles; newer layers held mammals more and more like today's.

To explain this without evolution, Cuvier proposed catastrophism: the Earth's history was broken up by sudden disasters, such as great floods, that wiped out local life, after which new animals moved in from elsewhere. Meanwhile in Britain, a canal surveyor named William Smith (1769–1839) noticed that each rock layer could be recognised by the particular fossils it contained. This let him produce the first geological map of a whole country, in 1815.

The big change came in the 1830s with the Scottish geologist Charles Lyell (1797–1875). In his three-volume Principles of Geology (1830–1833), he argued, following the earlier Scottish geologist James Hutton, that the Earth's past should be explained by the same slow processes we can see working today: rivers wearing away rock, mud settling, earthquakes slowly lifting the land, weather breaking down stone. Given enough time, a stream can carve a valley; no catastrophe is needed. This idea is called uniformitarianism.

Lyell stretched the Earth's history from thousands of years to many millions. (The true age, about 4.5 billion years, was found only with radioactive dating in the 20th century; see Part 1.) This idea of deep time was exactly what evolution needed: without millions of generations, natural selection would have had no time to work.

3. Everything Alive Is Made of Cells

While geologists looked at the Earth's long history, microscope users looked inwards at what living things are made of. In 1665 Robert Hooke had named the tiny compartments he saw in cork "cells" (Part 6). But for over 170 years these were mostly seen as empty boxes or tubes, not as living units. [2]

At the time, many biologists believed in vitalism: the idea that living things contain a special "life force" that ordinary physics and chemistry can never explain.

The turning point came in Germany in 1838–1839. The botanist Matthias Schleiden (1804–1881) had found that every part of a plant is made of cells, each with a dark spot inside, the nucleus. Over dinner, he described this to the zoologist Theodor Schwann (1810–1882), who immediately recognised the same structures in animal tissues he had been studying.

In 1839 Schwann published a book setting out the first two ideas of cell theory:

  1. All living things are made of cells, from single-celled pond creatures to giant redwood trees and human beings.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of life: the life of a plant or animal is the combined activity of its cells.

But they got one thing wrong: they thought new cells formed out of a shapeless fluid, rather like crystals forming in a salty solution.

The fix came in 1855 from the German doctor Rudolf Virchow (1821–1902), building on the observations of Robert Remak. His famous Latin motto was Omnis cellula e cellula: "every cell comes from a cell". Cells don't form out of fluid; they come only from other cells dividing.

The consequences were huge. If every cell comes from an earlier cell, then there is an unbroken chain of cell divisions running back from every living thing today, through countless ancestors, to the very beginning of life on Earth. The tree of life isn't just a way of sorting names; it is a real, unbroken line of living cells.

4. Darwin's Voyage and the Idea from Malthus

In December 1831 a 22-year-old Cambridge graduate named Charles Darwin (1809–1882) joined the survey ship HMS Beagle, captained by Robert FitzRoy. Planned as a two-year trip to chart the coasts of South America, it turned into a five-year voyage round the world: across the Atlantic, round Cape Horn, to the Galápagos Islands, Tahiti, New Zealand and Australia, and home by the Cape of Good Hope. [3, 1]

Darwin set out with conventional beliefs, but he took Lyell's Principles of Geology with him and read it closely. Through Lyell's eyes he saw landscapes as always slowly changing. In Chile he lived through a huge earthquake that lifted the coast by a metre or more, leaving beds of mussels stranded above the tide. High in the Andes he found fossil sea shells. If the land itself was slowly rising and sinking over millions of years, he wondered, mustn't living things change too, to keep up?

In September and October 1835 the Beagle visited the Galápagos, volcanic islands about 1,000 km off the coast of Ecuador. Darwin saw giant tortoises whose shells differed from island to island: on dry islands, saddle-shaped shells let them stretch up to reach tall cactus; on lusher islands, rounded shells suited low-growing plants. He also collected some small, dull birds, later famous as "Darwin's finches".

Their beaks varied: big, heavy beaks for cracking hard seeds; slender, curved beaks for feeding on cactus; thin, sharp beaks for picking insects off bark. But Darwin did not have a flash of inspiration on the beach. He mixed up many of his bird specimens without noting which island they came from, and even mistook some for wrens and blackbirds.

Only back in London in 1837 did the bird expert John Gould examine the specimens and announce something surprising: they weren't birds from different families at all, but a group of closely related finch species, found only in the Galápagos yet clearly related to birds on the South American mainland.

Why would a Creator make so many unique species for a few small, young volcanic islands, each resembling mainland species? In July 1837, in a private notebook, Darwin sketched a branching tree and wrote above it two words: "I think." Species weren't fixed; they were branches of a family tree.

But what made them change? An earlier idea, from the French biologist Lamarck, was that animals pass on changes they acquire during life, as if giraffes stretching for leaves grew longer necks and passed them to their young. There was no good evidence for this.

The breakthrough came in October 1838, when Darwin read An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) by the economist Thomas Malthus (1766–1834). Malthus argued that populations, left unchecked, grow by doubling (1, 2, 4, 8, 16...), while food supplies grow far more slowly (1, 2, 3, 4, 5...). The result is a constant "struggle for existence", with hunger, disease and conflict.

Darwin saw that this applies even more strongly to plants and animals. He later worked out that a single pair of elephants, the slowest-breeding animals known, could have about nineteen million descendants after 750 years if all survived. Yet animal numbers stay roughly the same over time. So most eggs, seeds and young must die before they can breed.

Now add one more fact: individuals differ slightly, and many differences are inherited. If some differences help an animal find food, escape predators or resist disease, those individuals are a little more likely to survive and have young, who inherit the helpful trait. Over many generations, helpful traits build up and unhelpful ones are weeded out. This is natural selection.

5. A Letter from the Other Side of the World: Wallace

Darwin kept his theory mostly to himself for twenty years. Historians still debate how much this was fear of controversy and how much a wish to gather overwhelming evidence. At his home, Down House in Kent, he bred pigeons, studied how insects pollinate orchids, spent eight years studying barnacles, and soaked seeds in salt water to see whether plants could drift across oceans. [3]

By 1844 he had written a 230-page essay on natural selection, with instructions to his wife Emma to publish it if he died. By 1856, encouraged by Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker, he was working on a huge book called Natural Selection.

Then, in June 1858, a package arrived from the other side of the world. It contained an essay by Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), a 35-year-old self-taught naturalist, sent from the island of Ternate in what is now Indonesia.

Wallace came from a modest background and earned his living collecting insects, birds and other specimens for wealthy collectors. Exploring the Amazon (1848–1852) and the islands of South-East Asia (1854–1862), he paid close attention to where different animals live. He identified what is now called the Wallace Line, a deep sea channel between Bali and Lombok, and between Borneo and Sulawesi. West of it live Asian animals such as tigers, monkeys and rhinos; east of it, Australian-type animals such as tree kangaroos, cuscuses and cockatoos.

In February 1858, shaking with malaria on the island of Gilolo (Halmahera), Wallace thought about Malthus and suddenly saw that constant competition must keep the better-suited variations and remove the others. When the fever passed, he wrote it up and posted it to Darwin, who was known to be interested in how species change.

Darwin was stunned. Wallace had arrived at natural selection independently, using very similar ideas and even phrases. Darwin wrote to Lyell that all his originality was "smashed". Lyell and Hooker arranged a fair solution: on 1 July 1858, at a meeting of the Linnean Society in London, extracts from Darwin's 1844 essay and from an 1857 letter Darwin had written to the American botanist Asa Gray were read alongside Wallace's essay.

Neither man was there. Wallace was still in South-East Asia and didn't know about the meeting, and Darwin was at home grieving for his 18-month-old son, who had just died of scarlet fever. The reading caused little stir; the Society's president later said that 1858 had not been marked by any striking discoveries. But it spurred Darwin to boil his huge manuscript down into an "abstract". Working hard for thirteen months, he produced one of the most influential books ever written.

6. On the Origin of Species

On 24 November 1859 the publisher John Murray released On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection. All 1,250 copies of the first printing were snapped up by booksellers. Darwin called the book "one long argument". [1, 2]

The argument rests on four observations:

  1. Living things produce more young than can survive: every population could grow explosively if all its offspring lived.
  2. But populations stay about the same size over long periods.
  3. Resources are limited: food, water, shelter and mates are in short supply, so there is a constant struggle to survive.
  4. Individuals differ, and many differences are inherited: no two are exactly alike, and offspring tend to resemble their parents.

From these Darwin drew two conclusions. First, survival isn't purely a matter of luck: individuals with traits that help even slightly will, on average, leave more offspring. That is natural selection. Second, over very long periods, this will gradually change populations, and can split one species into several. Darwin called this descent with modification.

Darwin rejected the idea that evolution is a march towards "perfection" or ever-greater complexity. What counts as an advantage depends on where you live: broad leaves help in a damp rainforest but would be a disaster in a desert. The only illustration in the whole book was a branching diagram showing how one ancestral group could split into many species over time, with some branches dying out.

Darwin was honest about the problems his theory faced. The two biggest were:

  • Missing fossils: if species changed gradually, why weren't the rocks full of in-between forms? Darwin replied that fossils form only rarely, in special conditions, and that only a tiny fraction of the rocks had been explored. Just two years later, in 1861, Archaeopteryx was found in Germany: a creature with teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail like a reptile, but feathers and wings like a bird. It was just the kind of in-between form Darwin had predicted.
  • How inheritance works: natural selection needs helpful traits to be passed on reliably. Most people then believed in blending inheritance: offspring are a smooth mix of their parents, like mixing red and white paint to get pink. The Scottish engineer Fleeming Jenkin pointed out the problem: with blending, a rare helpful trait would be halved in the next generation, quartered in the one after, and soon diluted away to nothing before selection could act on it.

Darwin tried to solve this with a guess of his own in 1868, called pangenesis: every part of the body sends tiny particles ("gemmules") to the reproductive organs, so changes made during life could be passed on. In doing so he slipped back towards Lamarck's idea. He didn't know that the real answer had already been published, in 1866, in an obscure journal, by a monk who grew peas.

7. Can Life Appear from Nothing? Pasteur and Koch

Another old question remained: can living things arise from non-living matter? Since ancient times, everyday experience seemed to say yes. Maggots appeared in rotting meat, mice in dirty grain stores, and boiled broth was soon swarming with tiny creatures. This idea was called spontaneous generation. [8]

In 1668 the Italian doctor Francesco Redi struck the first blow: meat kept covered never produced maggots; they appeared only when flies could lay eggs on it. But once microscopes revealed tiny organisms, the argument moved to them. In 1745 the English priest John Needham boiled meat gravy in sealed flasks and still found microbes growing, and concluded that a "vital force" in the broth created life. Some twenty years later the Italian priest Lazzaro Spallanzani boiled broths longer in properly sealed flasks, and they stayed clear. Needham's supporters replied that the long boiling had destroyed the broth's vital force and spoiled the air inside.

The argument was finally settled around 1861 by the French chemist Louis Pasteur (1822–1895). He saw that everything hinged on the air: did air carry some life-giving force, or just dust and invisible germs?

To find out, without cutting off the air, he designed the swan-neck flask. He filled flasks with broth that spoils easily, then heated the glass neck of each and drew it out into a long, S-shaped curve, still open to the air at the end. Then he boiled the broth to kill anything living in it.

As the flask cooled, fresh air could flow freely in through the open neck. But dust and germs carried in the air settled in the low bend of the neck and never reached the broth. The broth stayed in contact with fresh air, yet remained clear and sterile for months, even years. Only when the neck was broken off, or the flask was tipped so that broth touched the dust in the bend, did microbes grow and cloud it.

The conclusion: life comes only from life. This led directly to the germ theory of disease. If microbes don't appear from nothing in wounds or in fermenting beer, then fermentation, rotting and infectious diseases must be caused by particular microbes getting in.

The German doctor Robert Koch (1843–1910) turned germ theory into a precise science. He developed ways to grow bacteria on solid surfaces (first potato slices, then gelatine, and then agar, suggested by Fanny Hesse) and to stain them with dyes so they showed up under the microscope. He identified the bacteria that cause anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera. And he set out four tests, Koch's postulates, for proving that a particular microbe causes a particular disease:

  1. The microbe is found in everyone with the disease, but not in healthy individuals.
  2. It can be taken from a sick individual and grown on its own in the laboratory.
  3. When given to a healthy individual, the laboratory-grown microbe causes the same disease.
  4. The same microbe can then be recovered from that newly sick individual.

8. The Monk and His Peas: Gregor Mendel

While others puzzled over blending inheritance, an Augustinian friar, Gregor Mendel (1822–1884), was doing careful breeding experiments in the garden of his monastery in Brünn, in the Austrian Empire (now Brno in the Czech Republic). [4, 2]

Mendel was well prepared. At the University of Vienna he had studied physics under Christian Doppler and also mathematics. He approached inheritance like a physicist: counting clear-cut traits and looking for numerical patterns.

Between 1856 and 1863 Mendel grew and studied about 28,000 pea plants. Peas were a smart choice:

  • They normally fertilise themselves, so it's easy to get plants that "breed true", always producing offspring like themselves.
  • They can also be crossed by hand: remove the pollen-making parts from one flower and dust it with pollen from another.
  • Many of their traits come in two clear forms with nothing in between.

He studied seven such traits: seed shape (round or wrinkled), seed colour (yellow or green), seed-coat colour, pod shape, pod colour, flower position and stem height (tall or short).

When he crossed true-breeding round-seeded plants with true-breeding wrinkled-seeded ones, the offspring weren't a blend. Every one had round seeds. The wrinkled trait seemed to have disappeared.

Then he let these offspring fertilise themselves. In the next generation, the wrinkled trait came back. Counting 7,324 seeds from 253 plants, he found 5,474 round and 1,850 wrinkled: a ratio of 2.96 to 1, very close to 3 to 1. All seven traits gave the same 3 to 1 pattern.

Mendel's explanation was brilliantly simple. Inheritance isn't a blending of fluids. It works through separate units, which he called "elements" and we now call genes. In modern terms:

  • Each plant carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent. The different versions of a gene are called alleles.
  • One version can be dominant (call it R, for round) and hide the other, recessive version (r, for wrinkled). A plant with one of each (Rr) has round seeds.
  • When a plant makes pollen or egg cells, the two copies separate, so each pollen grain or egg gets just one (the law of segregation).
  • When two Rr plants are crossed, each parent passes on R or r at random, like tossing a coin. The possible results are RR, Rr, rR and rr, equally likely. Three of the four contain at least one R and look round; only one (rr) looks wrinkled. Hence 3 to 1.

Following two traits at once (such as round-yellow against wrinkled-green), Mendel found that different traits are passed on independently of each other, giving a ratio of 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 among the four possible combinations (the law of independent assortment).

Mendel presented his work to the local natural history society in 1865, and it was published in 1866. Almost nobody noticed. The leading botanist Carl Nägeli was unimpressed and suggested Mendel try hawkweed instead, a plant that, unknown to both of them, usually reproduces without fertilisation and so gave confusing results. Mendel died in 1884, his discovery unrecognised.

9. Genes on Chromosomes: Sutton, Boveri and Morgan's Flies

In 1900, three botanists in the Netherlands, Germany and Austria (Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak) independently found the same ratios as Mendel, and then found his 34-year-old paper. Suddenly Mendel was everywhere. [2, 5]

But where in the cell were Mendel's units? With better microscopes and new dyes, scientists had begun to watch cells divide. In the 1880s Walther Flemming saw thread-like structures in the nucleus that soaked up dye; they were named chromosomes ("coloured bodies"). Flemming watched them split and move apart as a cell divides, a process he called mitosis. Édouard van Beneden showed that egg and sperm cells have only half as many chromosomes as other body cells, through a special kind of division later called meiosis.

In 1902–1903 the American student Walter Sutton and the German biologist Theodor Boveri independently noticed that chromosomes behave exactly like Mendel's units:

  • Chromosomes come in pairs, one from each parent, just like Mendel's two copies.
  • The pairs separate when egg and sperm cells form, just as Mendel's units do.
  • Different pairs separate independently of each other, matching Mendel's law of independent assortment.

So genes must be on chromosomes. The proof came at Columbia University in New York, from Thomas Hunt Morgan (1866–1945) and his students Alfred Sturtevant, Calvin Bridges and Hermann Muller. In their cramped, smelly "Fly Room", they bred the fruit fly Drosophila. Fruit flies were perfect: cheap to keep on mashed banana, producing hundreds of young every couple of weeks, with only four pairs of chromosomes.

In 1910, after months of searching, Morgan found a single male fly with white eyes instead of the usual red. He crossed it with red-eyed females: all the young had red eyes. When those young were bred together, all the daughters had red eyes, but half the sons had white eyes. The trait followed the sex of the fly, so the gene for eye colour had to be on the X chromosome, one of the chromosomes that decide sex.

Morgan's group also found that genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together (linkage), but not always: pairs of chromosomes can swap pieces when egg and sperm cells form (crossing over). In 1911 the 19-year-old Alfred Sturtevant realised that the further apart two genes are on a chromosome, the more often they get separated by crossing over. Working through one evening, he drew the first genetic map, showing the order of several genes along the fly's X chromosome (published in 1913). Genes were now real things, lined up along chromosomes like beads on a string.

10. What Are Genes Made Of? DNA

By the 1930s it was known that chromosomes are made of two kinds of large molecule: proteins and DNA. Almost everyone assumed genes must be made of protein. [5]

Proteins seemed the obvious choice: they are built from twenty different building blocks (amino acids) and fold into countless complicated shapes, surely enough variety to carry the instructions for life. DNA looked dull by comparison. Discovered in 1869 by the Swiss scientist Friedrich Miescher, it is made of only four chemical "letters", called bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) and thymine (T). The influential chemist Phoebus Levene thought DNA was just the same four letters repeated over and over, like ACGTACGT..., which could carry no real information.

The first crack came from research on pneumonia. In 1928 the British medical officer Frederick Griffith was studying two strains of the bacterium that causes it:

  • The smooth (S) strain has a protective coat and is deadly to mice.
  • The rough (R) strain has no coat and is harmless, because the immune system destroys it.

Griffith found that S bacteria killed by heat were harmless. But when he injected mice with a mixture of living harmless R bacteria and dead S bacteria, the mice died, and from their blood he recovered living S bacteria with coats. Something from the dead S bacteria had permanently changed the living R bacteria. Griffith called it the transforming principle.

At the Rockefeller Institute in New York, Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty spent years trying to find out what it was. They took extracts of the dead S bacteria and destroyed one kind of molecule at a time, using enzymes that break down only that kind:

  • Destroying the proteins didn't stop the transformation.
  • Destroying RNA (a molecule related to DNA) didn't stop it.
  • Destroying the sugary coat material didn't stop it.
  • Only destroying the DNA stopped it completely.

In 1944 they announced that the transforming principle was DNA: DNA carries heredity. But many scientists weren't convinced, suspecting that tiny traces of protein might be responsible.

The clinching evidence came in 1952 from Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase, in what is called the "blender experiment". They worked with a virus that infects bacteria. This virus is very simple: an outer coat of protein with DNA inside, rather like a tiny space capsule that lands on a bacterium and injects something into it.

They labelled the two parts with different radioactive atoms, so they could track them:

  • One batch of viruses was grown with radioactive sulfur, which ends up in protein but not in DNA.
  • Another batch was grown with radioactive phosphorus, which ends up in DNA but not in protein.

After letting the viruses infect bacteria, they put the mixture in an ordinary kitchen blender, which knocked the empty virus coats off the bacteria, and then spun it in a centrifuge so the heavier bacteria sank to the bottom.

The radioactive sulfur (protein) stayed outside the bacteria, in the liquid. The radioactive phosphorus (DNA) went inside them, and the new viruses the bacteria produced contained it too. What the virus injected to make copies of itself was DNA. Genes are made of DNA.

11. The Double Helix

Once DNA was known to carry genes, the big question was: what does it look like, and how does its shape let it store information and copy itself? [6, 10, 7, 5]

In the early 1950s, several groups were working on it: Linus Pauling at Caltech in California (who had already worked out a key shape in proteins by building models), Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin at King's College London, and a young American biologist, James Watson, working with the English physicist Francis Crick (1916–2004) at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge.

The solution depended on two key clues from other scientists:

1. Chargaff's rules. Around 1950 the biochemist Erwin Chargaff measured how much of each DNA letter is found in different species. The overall mix varied a lot from species to species, but two rules always held: the amount of A always equals the amount of T, and the amount of G always equals the amount of C. This showed DNA was not a simple repeat (Levene had been wrong), but Chargaff didn't know why the rules held.

2. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photographs. At King's College, the physical chemist Rosalind Franklin (1920–1958) shone X-rays through fibres of DNA and recorded the patterns they made, which can reveal a molecule's shape. She discovered that DNA has two forms, a drier "A" form and a wetter "B" form, and that mixing them up had confused earlier work. In May 1952 she and her student Raymond Gosling took an exceptionally clear picture of the B form: Photo 51.

Photo 51 shows a bold "X" pattern of spots. To someone who knew the theory, an X like this is the signature of a helix, a spiral. Details of the pattern showed that the molecule was 2 nanometres wide (tens of thousands of times thinner than a hair), that the spiral made one full turn every 3.4 nanometres, and that there were about ten "steps" per turn.

In January 1953 Maurice Wilkins showed Photo 51 to Watson, without Franklin's knowledge. Crick also saw an unpublished report containing Franklin's measurements, including details of the DNA crystal's symmetry. From these Crick worked out that DNA has two strands running in opposite directions, one "up" and one "down".

Watson and Crick began building models, using cardboard cut-outs of the four bases. At first Watson tried pairing like with like (A with A, G with G), but the result was lumpy: two large bases together stuck out too far, and two small ones pinched in. The American chemist Jerry Donohue pointed out that Watson was drawing the bases in a form that was common in textbooks but wrong; in the body they take a slightly different shape.

On the morning of 28 February 1953, Watson redrew the bases in the correct shapes, pushed them around on his desk, and suddenly saw the answer:

  • A pairs with T, held by two weak hydrogen bonds.
  • G pairs with C, held by three.

An A–T pair and a G–C pair are almost exactly the same width. In each pair, one base is large and one small, so the double helix stays the same width all the way along. The bases sit inside like the rungs of a twisted ladder, protected by the two sugar-and-phosphate "rails" on the outside.

This explained Chargaff's rules at once: A must equal T and G must equal C because each base on one strand always has its partner opposite it on the other strand. Watson and Crick announced the structure in a short paper in Nature on 25 April 1953, ending with one of the most famous understatements in science:

It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.

The idea was that the two strands unzip, and each serves as a pattern for building a new partner strand. In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl confirmed this with a clever experiment using a heavy form of nitrogen to label old and new strands: each new double helix contains one old strand and one new one.

12. Cracking the Genetic Code

The double helix showed how DNA stores and copies information. But how does a string of four letters (A, C, G, T) spell out a protein made from twenty different amino acids? [5]

In 1954 the physicist George Gamow pointed out a simple counting argument:

  • If each amino acid were coded by 1 letter, there would be only 4 possible codes: far too few.
  • With 2 letters, there are 4 × 4 = 16 possible codes: still not enough for 20 amino acids.
  • With 3 letters, there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codes: more than enough, with some to spare.

In 1961 Francis Crick and Sydney Brenner showed by experiment that the code is indeed read in groups of three letters, called codons. Adding or removing one or two letters scrambled everything after that point, like losing a letter in THE CAT SAT so that it reads THC ATS AT. But adding or removing three letters at once restored the sense.

The same year, Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei at the US National Institutes of Health cracked the first "word". They added an artificial message made only of the letter U (UUUUUU...; RNA uses U instead of T) to a mixture extracted from bacteria that can make proteins. It produced a protein made of just one amino acid, phenylalanine. So UUU means phenylalanine.

Within five years, with methods developed by Har Gobind Khorana, Philip Leder and others, all 64 codons had been worked out, including three that mean "stop". The code turned out to have spare codons (several codons can mean the same amino acid) and to be almost the same in every living thing on Earth, from bacteria to oak trees to humans. That is why a suitably prepared human insulin gene put into bacteria makes real human insulin, which is how much of the world's insulin is now produced.

Crick summed up the flow of information in cells as the central dogma (1958, refined in 1970): DNA is copied into DNA; DNA is "transcribed" into a messenger molecule, RNA; and RNA is "translated" into protein:

$$\text{DNA} \rightarrow \text{RNA} \rightarrow \text{protein}$$

Information can also go from RNA back to DNA (Howard Temin and David Baltimore discovered in 1970 that some viruses do this), but once information is in a protein, it can't flow back into DNA. So changes your body acquires during life can't be written back into your genes this way, which rules out Lamarck's simple mechanism. (Epigenetics, discovered later, involves chemical marks on DNA that can sometimes be passed on, but it is a separate and more limited effect.)

In the 1960s Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling realised that if changes build up in DNA and proteins at a fairly steady rate, the number of differences between two species works as a molecular clock, showing how long ago they shared an ancestor. Comparing molecules found in all life, Carl Woese discovered in 1977 a whole third branch of life, the Archaea, alongside bacteria and organisms like us. Darwin's tree of life could now be read directly in the letters of DNA.

13. How Did They Know? Pasteur's Swan-Neck Flask

How did they know? Changing just one thing at a time

How did Pasteur convince doubtful scientists that germs in the air, not a mysterious "vital force", made broth go bad? [8]

The secret of his experiment was to change just one thing while keeping everything else the same. Believers in spontaneous generation had two objections to earlier experiments:

  1. Sealing a flask cuts off the fresh air that life needs.
  2. Boiling for a long time ruins the broth, so it can no longer support life.

The swan-neck flask answered both:

  • Fresh air got in: the end of the neck was never sealed, so air could move in and out freely.
  • The broth wasn't ruined: when Pasteur broke the neck off a flask that had stayed clear for many months, microbes grew in it within a day or two. So the broth could still support life perfectly well.
  • The bend trapped the dust: air entering the long curved neck slowed down in the bend, so dust and germs settled there instead of reaching the broth.

His most convincing test was simple: he tipped a clear flask so that a little broth ran into the dusty bend and back into the flask. Within a day it clouded with microbes. A long argument about mysterious forces had been settled by a carefully designed experiment.

14. Legend and Record: Darwin's Finches and Rosalind Franklin

Legend and Record: the real stories behind two famous discoveries

Popular accounts love sudden flashes of genius: Darwin seeing finch beaks in the Galápagos and instantly understanding evolution, or Watson and Crick working out DNA's structure all by themselves. The real history is more complicated. [3, 7]

The finch "flash" that never happened: in popular legend, the finches inspired natural selection. In fact Darwin doesn't mention the finches at all in On the Origin of Species. During his five weeks in the Galápagos he didn't realise their importance, and he recorded the islands of so few specimens that he later had to rely on the better-labelled collections of Captain FitzRoy and his own servant Syms Covington.

The real trigger for natural selection was a book about human populations: Malthus's essay, which Darwin read in October 1838. The finches became a famous example of evolution only in the 20th century, thanks especially to David Lack's work in the 1940s and Peter and Rosemary Grant's long study from the 1970s onwards, which has actually watched beak sizes change from one generation to the next.

Rosalind Franklin: Watson's 1968 memoir The Double Helix painted Franklin as difficult and unhelpful, someone who didn't understand her own data. Historians now see her very differently.

Franklin was a first-rate scientist whose X-ray work was the best in the field. She separated the two forms of DNA, which had confused everyone else. Her notebooks show that by February 1953 she had worked out key measurements of the B form, realised that the backbones are on the outside and the bases inside, and was close to the structure herself.

Watson and Crick's insight into how the bases pair was their own, and crucial. But they relied on Franklin's photo and measurements, which they saw without her knowledge, and her role was barely acknowledged in their 1953 paper. She died of ovarian cancer in 1958, aged just 37. The Nobel Prize for the structure went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins in 1962; it is never given to people who have died.

15. Philosophy Corner: Purpose Without a Planner

Philosophy Corner: how can living things look designed?

Before Darwin, living things seemed to resist the machine-like picture of nature built by Galileo, Descartes and Newton (Part 6). A clockwork solar system could be explained by masses and forces. But how could you explain an eye or a heart without saying it exists for something, the "what is it for?" cause that Aristotle called the final cause (Part 3)? [2]

In 1790 the philosopher Immanuel Kant wrote that there would never be a "Newton of a blade of grass": no one would ever explain how living things are organised by natural laws alone, without design. Darwin became that Newton.

Darwin turned purpose around. The eye's lens wasn't made in advance in order to see. Instead, over countless generations, animals with slightly better light-focusing tissue survived and bred a little more often than those with worse. Natural selection doesn't look ahead; it is a blind filter that keeps what has worked in the past.

To describe this "purpose without a purposer", the biologist Ernst Mayr and others used the word teleonomy. Living things really do act in goal-directed ways (a bacterium swims towards food; an orchid's flower mimics a female wasp to attract male wasps that carry its pollen), but not because anyone planned it. Their behaviour has been shaped by natural selection over many generations. Living things show purpose without a planner.
