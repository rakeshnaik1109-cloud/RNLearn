How Far Is the Moon? Shadows, Parallax and Mirrors on the Moon

Nobody can stretch a rope to the Moon. Yet Greek astronomers found its distance with shadows and geometry more than 2,000 years ago, and today we time laser light bounced off mirrors left by astronauts.

1. How Far Away Is the Moon?

In Parts 1 and 2 we followed people who measured the Earth: first its size, then its shape. Now we leave the ground. The Moon is the nearest object in the sky, yet nobody could walk to it, touch it or stretch a rope to it. How could anyone possibly know how far away it is?

Remarkably, Greek astronomers worked out a good answer more than 2,000 years ago, using nothing but shadows, angles and careful thinking. They found that the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away, roughly 30 times the Earth's width. Since 1969 we have checked that answer by firing lasers at mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts, timing the light's round trip to a fraction of a billionth of a second. Today the distance is known to within a few millimetres. [3, 14]

As in every part of this series, you will find interactive figures, a "How Close Did They Get?" table, and a "Try It Yourself" experiment you can do on any clear night.

3. Shadows on the Moon: Aristarchus

Around 270 BC, Aristarchus of Samos, a Greek astronomer best remembered for suggesting that the Earth goes round the Sun, wrote a short book called On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon. It is the oldest surviving attempt to measure the heavens with geometry. [1, 2]

His key idea used a lunar eclipse, when the Moon passes through the Earth's shadow. Watch one and you can see the round edge of that shadow creeping across the Moon, and you can judge how big the shadow is compared with the Moon. Aristarchus took the shadow at the Moon's distance to be about two Moon-widths across.

Why does that tell you the Moon's size? Because the Sun is so far away, its light arrives in nearly parallel rays, and the Earth's shadow narrows only slowly, like a long cone. Here is the clever part: the shadow narrows at exactly the rate set by the Sun's apparent size in the sky, and the Sun and Moon look almost the same size (which is why the Moon can just cover the Sun in a solar eclipse). So, by the time the shadow has travelled out to the Moon, it has lost about one Moon-width from its edges. If the shadow is two Moon-widths wide there, the Earth must be about two plus one, so three Moon-widths wide. The Moon is roughly a third the size of the Earth. [3, 4]

Once you know how big the Moon is, its distance follows from how big it looks. The Moon covers about half a degree of sky, which means it is about 110 times further away than it is wide. A Moon a little over a quarter of the Earth's width, 110 Moon-widths away, is about 60 Earth radii from us.

Aristarchus's reasoning was excellent, but one of his starting numbers was not. In his book he assumed the Moon looks 2 degrees wide, four times too big. His conclusions put the Moon's diameter between about 0.32 and 0.40 of the Earth's, a good result, but with 2 degrees his geometry places the Moon only about 20 Earth radii away. Archimedes later reported that Aristarchus had found the correct half-degree value, which would have given about 80 Earth radii instead. Why he used 2 degrees in the book is still debated; it may have been a round number chosen to demonstrate the method. [1, 2, 3]

4. Two Places, One Eclipse: Hipparchus

About 140 years later, Hipparchus of Nicaea, perhaps the greatest astronomer of the ancient world, tried a completely different method. His book on the distances is lost, but later writers, especially Ptolemy and Pappus, described what was in it. [5, 6]

Hipparchus used a solar eclipse, when the Moon passes in front of the Sun. Records showed that one eclipse had been total near the Hellespont (the strait by modern Istanbul), while in Alexandria, far to the south, only about four-fifths of the Sun had been covered. The Moon could not be in two places at once, so the difference must come from looking at it from two different places on the Earth. Because Hipparchus knew how far apart the two cities were, he could work out how far away the Moon must be for it to shift by that amount. The historian G. J. Toomer identified the eclipse as probably that of 14 March 190 BC, though some historians prefer an eclipse in 129 BC. [5]

According to Pappus, Hipparchus found that the Moon's distance varies between 71 and 83 Earth radii. Knowing that the Sun's own distance affected the answer, he did a second calculation in another book and found a least distance of 62 and a mean of 67⅓ Earth radii. The true average is about 60.3. That is impressive for a calculation built on an eclipse seen with the naked eye. [5, 3]

5. Ptolemy, Aryabhata and a Moon That Should Have Doubled

Around AD 150, Claudius Ptolemy in Alexandria gave a mean distance at new and full Moon of about 59 Earth radii in his great astronomy book, later known as the Almagest, close to the true value. He worked it out from the Moon's parallax, its small shift against the stars caused by our viewpoint on the Earth (section 6). [6, 4]

But Ptolemy's model of the Moon's motion had a strange flaw. To predict the Moon's position in the sky correctly, it swung the Moon between roughly 33 and 64 Earth radii from us. If that were true, the Moon would sometimes look nearly twice as wide as at other times. Anyone could see that it does not. The model predicted where the Moon would be very well, but not how far. It was a reminder that a theory can give the right answers to one question and the wrong answers to another. [4, 3]

In India, the mathematician-astronomer Aryabhata wrote the Āryabhaṭīya in AD 499. It gives the diameters of the Earth and the Moon as 1,050 and 315 yojanas, a traditional unit of distance. Whatever the length of a yojana, the ratio is what matters: it makes the Moon 0.30 of the Earth's width, close to the true 0.27 and better than Aristarchus's range. [7]

6. Parallax: Seeing the Moon Shift

Hold up a finger at arm's length and look at it first with one eye, then the other. It seems to jump against the background. That jump is called parallax, and the closer the finger, the bigger it is. Two observers far apart on the Earth looking at the Moon at the same moment see it in slightly different places among the stars, for the same reason. Measure the shift and the distance between the observers, and you have a long, thin triangle whose length is the distance to the Moon.

The Moon's parallax is large for an astronomical object, about a degree at most, roughly two Moon-widths. Ancient astronomers could detect it. But measuring it precisely needed accurate telescopes, clocks, and two observers a very long way apart.

The classic measurement came in 1751–52. The French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille sailed to the Cape of Good Hope at the southern tip of Africa, while the 19-year-old Joseph-Jérôme Lalande was sent to Berlin, almost due north of the Cape on nearly the same line of longitude. On agreed nights, both measured the Moon's position against the stars as it crossed their meridian. Berlin and the Cape are about 86 degrees of latitude apart, a baseline of nearly 10,000 km. Comparing their results, Lalande found the Moon's parallax to be about 57 minutes of arc, which puts the Moon at about 60 Earth radii. (By convention, astronomers quote the parallax as the shift that would be seen between the centre of the Earth and a point on its equator; two real observers' results are converted to this standard.) [9, 3]

7. An Egg-Shaped Orbit and a Falling Moon

The Moon does not stay at one distance. In 1609 Johannes Kepler showed that planets move in ellipses, slightly squashed circles, and the Moon's orbit is one too. At its closest point, called perigee, the Moon is about 363,000 km away; at its furthest, apogee, about 405,000 km. That is why a "supermoon", a full Moon near perigee, looks about 14 per cent wider than a full Moon near apogee. The difference is real but too small for most people to notice without a photograph. (Ptolemy's model, remember, predicted a near doubling.) [19, 3]

Knowing the Moon's distance then made possible one of the great calculations of science. Isaac Newton wondered whether the force that makes an apple fall also keeps the Moon in orbit. If gravity weakens with the square of distance, then at 60 Earth radii it should be 60 × 60 = 3,600 times weaker than at the Earth's surface. Newton calculated how fast the Moon is actually falling towards the Earth (curving away from a straight line) from its distance and its orbit of 27.3 days. The answer: about 2.7 millimetres per second squared, very close to the Earth's surface gravity, 9.8 metres per second squared, divided by 3,600. The same force rules the apple and the Moon. Without a good distance to the Moon, this test would have been impossible. He published it in his Principia in 1687. [8]

8. Echoes from the Moon: Radar

After the Second World War, engineers had powerful new radar sets, built to detect aircraft by bouncing radio waves off them. Could they bounce a signal off the Moon? On 10 January 1946, a US Army Signal Corps team at Camp Evans, New Jersey, led by John DeWitt, did exactly that. The project was named Project Diana after the Roman goddess of the Moon. Each pulse went out, and about 2.5 seconds later a faint echo came back. That was the time radio waves, travelling at the speed of light, needed for the round trip of about 770,000 km. [10]

A few weeks later, in February 1946, the Hungarian physicist Zoltán Bay detected Moon echoes too, using a different and very clever technique: because his signal was so weak, he added up many echoes to lift them above the noise. [11]

Radar turned the problem of distance into a problem of timing. If you know the speed of light and measure the round-trip time, the distance is simply the speed multiplied by half the time. This idea would later be used to measure the distance to the Sun (Part 4).

9. Mirrors on the Moon

Radar beams spread out enormously and reflect from a large patch of the Moon's surface, so the echoes are smeared out in time. Lasers produce a much narrower, sharper beam. In 1962 scientists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology bounced laser light off the bare surface of the Moon for the first time. But the rough lunar surface scattered the light in all directions, and very little came back. [12]

The solution was to put mirrors there. On 21 July 1969, Apollo 11 astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin set up a panel of 100 small glass prisms called corner-cube retroreflectors on the Moon. A corner cube, like the reflectors on a bicycle, sends light straight back towards wherever it came from, whatever angle it arrives at. On 1 August 1969, astronomers at Lick Observatory in California, using a 120-inch (3-metre) telescope and a ruby laser, detected the first returns. [13, 14]

Apollo 14 (1971) left an identical panel, and Apollo 15 (1971) a larger one with 300 prisms. Two Soviet robotic rovers, Lunokhod 1 (1970) and Lunokhod 2 (1973), carried French-built reflectors too. Lunokhod 1's reflector was lost for decades because nobody knew exactly where the rover had parked; in 2010, after NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed it, a team at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico found it again on their first attempt. [14, 17]

10. What the Mirrors Taught Us

Lunar laser ranging has continued for more than half a century, and its precision has improved from tens of centimetres in the early years to a few millimetres today. A distance of 384,400 km known to a few millimetres is like knowing the distance from Delhi to Mumbai to the width of a single bacterium. It is the only Apollo experiment still producing results. [14, 16]

What has it revealed?

  • The Moon is drifting away at about 3.8 centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. The tides the Moon raises in the Earth's oceans slowly pass energy to the Moon, pushing it outwards and gradually lengthening our day. [15]
  • The Moon has a liquid core. The tiny wobbles in the Moon's rotation, measured by the reflectors, show that it does not turn like a solid ball. [21]
  • Einstein passes another test. Lunar laser ranging confirms that the Earth and Moon fall towards the Sun in exactly the same way, as general relativity predicts, and that the strength of gravity has not changed measurably over decades. [18]

Meanwhile, the average distance stands at about 384,400 km, or 60.3 Earth radii. Light takes about 1.28 seconds to get there. Hipparchus and Ptolemy, 2,000 years ago, were within 10 to 20 per cent of that. [19]

12. Try It Yourself: Measure the Moon with a Hole and a Ruler

Try it yourself: how many Moon-widths away is the Moon?

You can find how many times further away the Moon is than it is wide, the key step in Aristarchus's method, with things you have at home.

You need: a piece of card, a paper hole punch (or a sharp pencil to make a small round hole), a ruler or tape measure, a friend, and a clear night with the Moon well up in the sky (a full Moon is easiest).

  1. Punch a clean round hole in the card and measure its width. A standard hole punch makes a hole about 6 mm across.
  2. Hold the card up and look at the Moon through the hole with one eye, keeping the other closed. Move the card towards or away from your eye until the Moon just fills the hole, touching its edges.
  3. Keep the card still while your friend measures the distance from your eye to the card.
  4. Divide that distance by the width of the hole. That is how many Moon-widths away the Moon is.

Worked example: a 6 mm hole is just filled by the Moon when the card is 66 cm (660 mm) from your eye. 660 ÷ 6 = 110. So the Moon is about 110 Moon-widths away.

Now combine it with Aristarchus. Suppose the Earth's shadow during an eclipse is about 2.6 Moon-widths wide. Then the Moon is about 1 ÷ (2.6 + 1) ≈ 0.28 of the Earth's width, about 3,500 km across. Distance ≈ 110 × 3,500 km ≈ 385,000 km. Using only a card, a ruler and a shadow, you have measured the distance to the Moon.

Extra challenge: repeat the measurement when the Moon is low near the horizon, where it looks huge, and again when it is high. Do you get a different answer? (See section 14.)

13. How Did They Know? Catching Single Photons from the Moon

How did they know? Timing light to a fraction of a nanosecond

Firing a laser at a mirror the size of a suitcase, 384,000 km away, sounds impossible. How is it done? [14, 16]

  1. Aim with a big telescope. The laser is sent out through a large telescope, which also collects the return. Even so, by the time the beam reaches the Moon it has spread to a patch a few kilometres wide, so only a tiny fraction hits the reflector.
  2. Expect almost nothing back. The returning light spreads again on the way home. Each pulse contains around a hundred million billion (1017) photons, yet usually only about one, and often none, is detected back at the telescope.
  3. Filter out everything else. The detector only "listens" for a few billionths of a second around the expected return time, and only for the exact colour of the laser, to shut out moonlight and starlight.
  4. Time each photon. Each detected photon's round trip, about 2.5 seconds, is timed to within a fraction of a nanosecond. A nanosecond of round-trip time corresponds to 15 cm of distance.
  5. Add up thousands. A single photon's timing is uncertain by a few centimetres, but averaging thousands of photons over a few minutes shrinks the uncertainty to millimetres.
  6. Correct for everything. The final distance must allow for the air the light passes through, the Earth's rotation and its rising and falling crust (yes, the ground moves with tides too), and even the effects of relativity on clocks and light.

The result is a series of distance measurements, each precise to a few millimetres, that astronomers fit with a detailed model of the Earth–Moon system. Each new measurement either agrees with the model or reveals something new.

14. Legend and Record: The Giant Moon on the Horizon

Legend and Record: is the rising Moon really bigger?

The legend: the Moon is bigger when it is near the horizon, perhaps because it is closer to us then, or because the air magnifies it like a lens. Aristotle proposed the magnification idea, and many people still believe something like it. [20]

The record: measurements show the opposite. When the Moon is on the horizon, it is actually further away from you by almost one Earth radius than when it is overhead, because you are looking at it across the Earth rather than straight up. So the horizon Moon is very slightly smaller, by about 1.5 per cent. Photographs and angle measurements confirm this: the camera sees no giant Moon. The air does not magnify it either; if anything, refraction squashes the low Moon slightly from top to bottom. [20]

What is well established: the "Moon illusion" happens in our brains. Ptolemy and, later, the Arab scholar Ibn al-Haytham already suggested that it is a trick of perception. Exactly how the brain does it is still argued over: one popular explanation is that we judge the horizon to be further away than the sky overhead, so the brain "enlarges" an object it thinks is far off.

Why it matters: the Moon illusion is a powerful reminder of why measurement matters. Our eyes and brains can be fooled even by something we have seen thousands of times. A hole in a card (section 12) cannot.

15. Philosophy Corner: Measuring What You Can Never Touch

Philosophy Corner: chains of reasoning

No ruler has ever been laid from the Earth to the Moon. Every measurement of its distance, from Aristarchus to laser ranging, is indirect: it measures something else (a shadow, an angle, a time) and uses a theory to turn it into a distance.

Aristarchus relied on geometry and the assumption that light travels in straight lines. Parallax needs the size of the Earth (Part 1). Laser ranging needs the speed of light, and relativity to correct the clocks. Each method stands on a chain of earlier knowledge. Philosophers say such measurements are theory-laden: what we measure depends partly on what we already believe.

Does that make the results doubtful? Not necessarily. The strongest evidence that the Moon is about 384,000 km away is that completely different methods, built on completely different theories, all agree. If geometry, parallax and the timing of light were all wrong, it would be an extraordinary coincidence for them to be wrong in exactly the same way.

Ask yourself: what do you know only indirectly, by a chain of reasoning, rather than by seeing it for yourself? How do you decide which chains to trust?
