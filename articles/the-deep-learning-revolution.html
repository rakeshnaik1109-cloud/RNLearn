The Deep Learning Revolution: ImageNet, AlphaGo and AlphaFold

In 2012 a neural network trained on two gaming graphics cards won a photo-recognition contest by a huge margin. Within a decade, deep learning had mastered speech, translation and Go, and solved a fifty-year-old problem in biology.

1. The Year Everything Changed

At the end of Part 4, three things were ready: deep neural networks that could at last be trained, fast graphics chips, and huge collections of labelled data. In 2012 they came together in a contest to recognise photographs, and the result was so dramatic that within a few years almost the whole of AI had switched to the new approach, known as deep learning. [1, 2]

This part covers roughly 2010 to 2021, the decade in which AI went from a research topic to something in everyone's pocket. In those years deep networks learned to:

  • recognise objects in photos about as accurately as people, and recognise faces;
  • understand speech well enough for voice assistants;
  • translate between languages far better than before;
  • create realistic images of things that never existed;
  • beat the world's best players at Go, a game long thought too subtle for computers;
  • solve a 50-year-old problem in biology.

It also brought new worries: systems that were biased, easily fooled, power-hungry and controlled by a handful of giant companies. Both sides of the story matter for understanding the AI of today.

3. ImageNet and AlexNet

In 2010 Fei-Fei Li's ImageNet project (Part 4) launched an annual contest, the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge. Programs were given 1.2 million training photos sorted into 1,000 categories, from "tabby cat" to "container ship" to "strawberry", and then tested on photos they had never seen. A program was allowed five guesses per photo; it scored a mistake only if none of the five was right. In the first two years, the best systems, built with hand-designed image features, got about a quarter of the photos wrong. [3]

In 2012 a team from Geoffrey Hinton's lab in Toronto entered a deep convolutional network, designed by his students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever. It had 8 learned layers and about 60 million weights, and it was trained for about a week on two gaming graphics cards in Krizhevsky's bedroom at his parents' house. Now known as AlexNet, it got about 15 per cent of the test photos wrong. The next-best entry got about 26 per cent wrong. In a field where progress usually came a point or two at a time, it was a stunning leap. [4, 5]

The computer-vision community took notice immediately. Within two years nearly every serious entry used a deep network. Tech companies scrambled to hire the people who knew how to build them. Hinton, Krizhevsky and Sutskever formed a tiny company, and after a bidding war between several tech giants, Google acquired it in 2013. [5]

4. Why Now? Data, Chips and Tricks

The basic design of AlexNet was close to LeCun's networks of the 1990s (Part 4). Why did it suddenly work so much better? Three reasons. [1]

Data. LeCun's digit-readers learned from tens of thousands of small black-and-white images. AlexNet learned from 1.2 million colour photos. Deep networks have millions of weights to set, and they need vast numbers of examples to set them well without overfitting.

Chips. Graphics processing units (GPUs) were designed to draw video-game scenes, which means doing the same simple calculation on millions of pixels at once. Training a neural network needs exactly that kind of arithmetic: huge numbers of simple multiplications that can be done side by side. A GPU has thousands of small, slowish workers rather than a few fast ones. Researchers had begun using GPUs for neural networks in the late 2000s, finding them dozens of times faster than ordinary processors, and the chip maker NVIDIA had released software that made them easier to program. [6]

Tricks. A few simple changes made deep networks much easier to train. One was a new way for each neuron to respond, called a ReLU ("rectified linear unit"): output nothing for negative inputs and pass positive inputs straight through. Unlike the older S-shaped responses, it doesn't squash the learning signal, which eased the vanishing gradient problem. Another was dropout: during training, randomly switch off half the neurons each time, so the network can't rely on any one of them and is forced to learn more robust patterns. It sounds strange, but it greatly reduced overfitting. [4, 7]

5. Deeper and Deeper

If 8 layers worked, would more work better? Each year the ImageNet winners went deeper: 22 layers for Google's GoogLeNet in 2014, then 152 layers for Microsoft's ResNet in 2015. ResNet's key idea was a "shortcut" that lets each block of layers pass its input straight through and learn only an adjustment to it, so that signals could travel through a very deep network without fading away. Its error rate, 3.6 per cent, was lower than that of a trained human tester (section 13). [8, 3]

What were all these layers learning? In 2013 Matthew Zeiler and Rob Fergus found ways to show which patterns made each neuron respond. The first layers responded to edges and patches of colour, much like the filters in Part 4. The next layers responded to textures and simple shapes, such as grids and circles. Deeper layers responded to parts of objects, such as eyes, wheels or faces of dogs, and the final layers to whole objects. Nobody had told the network to build this hierarchy: it emerged from training, as the family-tree network's features had in 1986. [9]

6. Hearing, Seeing and Recognising

Deep learning swept through one field after another. Speech recognition was first. Around 2009–2012, groups at Toronto, Microsoft, Google and IBM found that replacing parts of their speech systems with deep networks cut errors substantially. Within a few years, the voice assistants on phones were built on deep learning, and speaking to a device became normal. [10]

In 2012 a Google team led by Andrew Ng and Jeff Dean trained a huge network, running on 16,000 processor cores, on 10 million frames from YouTube videos, without telling it what any of them showed. One of its neurons ended up responding strongly to cats' faces. The press loved the "cat neuron". The serious point was that a network could discover meaningful categories for itself from unlabelled data. [11]

In 2014 Facebook's DeepFace system matched pairs of face photos with 97.35 per cent accuracy on a standard test, close to human performance. Face recognition soon spread to unlocking phones, tagging photos, border control and police work, which raised difficult questions of privacy and fairness (section 12). [12]

7. Words as Numbers

Language was harder. Images are already numbers (pixel brightnesses), but words are symbols. How do you feed a word into a neural network in a way that captures its meaning? [13]

The answer came from an old idea in linguistics: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words that appear in similar surroundings tend to have similar meanings. In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google released word2vec, which learned to place every word as a point in a space of a few hundred dimensions, by training a network to predict which words appear near each other in billions of words of text. Words with similar meanings ended up close together.

More surprisingly, directions in the space captured relationships. The step from "man" to "woman" was roughly the same as the step from "king" to "queen", and the step from "France" to "Paris" was roughly the same as from "Italy" to "Rome". So simple arithmetic could solve analogies: take the point for "king", subtract "man", add "woman", and the nearest word is "queen". [13]

Word vectors also absorbed the prejudices in the text they learned from. Researchers found, for example, that some trained vectors completed "man is to computer programmer as woman is to ?" with "homemaker". A model learns the world as described in its data, including its stereotypes. [14]

8. Translation and Attention

In 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le at Google showed that one network could read a whole sentence in one language, squeezing it into a list of numbers, and a second network could write it out in another language. This "sequence to sequence" learning used the LSTM networks of Part 4. It worked, but cramming a long sentence into one fixed list of numbers lost information. [15]

The same year, Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio in Montreal added a crucial idea: attention. As the network writes each word of the translation, it looks back over all the words of the original sentence and decides which ones matter most right now, much as a human translator glances back at the relevant part of the text. Attention greatly improved translations of long sentences. [16]

In late 2016 Google switched its translation service to neural networks. In side-by-side ratings by people, the new system cut translation errors by about 60 per cent on several major language pairs compared with the old statistical system. And in 2017 a Google team asked a bold question: what if attention was all you needed? Their answer, the transformer, is the subject of Part 6. [17]

9. Machines That Imagine: GANs

Networks could recognise images. Could they create them? In 2014 Ian Goodfellow, then a PhD student in Montreal, came up with an ingenious idea, reportedly during a discussion with friends in a bar: set two networks against each other. [18, 5]

In a generative adversarial network (GAN), one network, the generator, tries to create fake images, starting from random noise. The other, the discriminator, is shown a mix of real photos and the generator's fakes, and tries to tell which is which. Each learns from the other: as the discriminator gets better at spotting fakes, the generator is pushed to make more convincing ones. It is like a contest between a forger and an art expert, in which both keep improving.

Early GANs produced blurry, small images. Within five years, systems such as NVIDIA's StyleGAN (2019) could produce photographs of faces of people who had never existed, convincing enough to fool most viewers. The same techniques made "deepfakes" possible: realistic fake videos of real people, which raised serious worries about fraud and misinformation.

10. Learning to Play: Atari and AlphaGo

In London, a company called DeepMind, founded in 2010 by Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman, combined deep learning with the reinforcement learning of Parts 2 and 4. In 2013 it showed a single network learning to play classic Atari video games, such as Breakout and Space Invaders, from nothing but the screen pixels and the score. Its 2015 version, tested on 49 games, scored at least three-quarters of a professional games tester's score on more than half of them, and far better than the tester on some. Google bought the company in 2014. [19]

DeepMind's next target was Go, an ancient board game in which two players place black and white stones on a 19 × 19 grid. Its rules are simple, but the number of possible positions, around 10170, dwarfs that of chess, and good play depends on an intuitive sense of shape and balance that players struggle to put into words. The brute-force search that worked for Deep Blue was hopeless. Many experts thought a computer champion was at least a decade away.

AlphaGo combined three things: a "policy" network that suggested promising moves, trained first on about 30 million positions from games between strong human players; a "value" network that judged who was winning; and a search method that explored the most promising lines of play. It then improved by playing millions of games against versions of itself. In October 2015 it beat the European champion, Fan Hui, 5–0. In March 2016 in Seoul it played Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players of the previous decade, in a match watched by many millions, and won 4–1. [20]

Two moves became legendary. In game 2, AlphaGo's move 37 was so unusual that commentators first thought it was a mistake; AlphaGo itself estimated a human would play it about 1 time in 10,000. It proved brilliant. In game 4, Lee Sedol replied with his own extraordinary move 78, which confused AlphaGo and won him the game, the only one a human would win against it in a formal match.

In 2017 DeepMind went further. AlphaGo Zero learned entirely by playing against itself, starting from random moves with no human games at all. After three days it beat the version that had defeated Lee Sedol, 100 games to 0. A general version, AlphaZero, taught itself chess, shogi (Japanese chess) and Go, and beat the strongest existing programs at each. In 2019 Lee Sedol retired from professional play, saying that AI was "an entity that cannot be defeated". [21, 22]

11. AlphaFold: Solving a Problem in Biology

Games were a proving ground. DeepMind's most important achievement was in science. [23]

Proteins are the molecular machines of life: they digest food, carry oxygen, fight infections and build cells. Each is a long chain of building blocks called amino acids, which folds up into a precise three-dimensional shape, and its shape determines what it does. Reading the order of amino acids from DNA is easy. Working out the shape it folds into had been one of biology's great unsolved problems for 50 years. Doing it by experiment, using techniques such as X-ray crystallography, can take months or years for a single protein.

Since 1994, scientists had held a contest every two years, called CASP, in which teams predicted the shapes of proteins whose structures had just been measured but not yet published. In 2020 DeepMind's AlphaFold 2 produced predictions so accurate that for most proteins they were comparable to experimental results. The contest's organisers said the problem had, in large part, been solved. [23, 24]

DeepMind and the European Bioinformatics Institute then released predicted structures for free, and by 2022 the database covered over 200 million proteins, nearly every one known to science. Researchers worldwide now use it in work on drugs, vaccines, enzymes and diseases. In 2024 Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of DeepMind shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, together with David Baker, who designs new proteins. That year's physics prize, to Hopfield and Hinton (Part 4), made it a remarkable year for AI.

12. Growing Pains

Success brought problems that the field is still grappling with. [2]

Fragility. In 2013 Christian Szegedy and colleagues discovered that tiny, carefully chosen changes to an image, invisible to people, could make a network give a completely wrong answer with high confidence. In a famous example, a photo of a panda, altered by an imperceptible pattern, was labelled "gibbon" with 99 per cent confidence. These adversarial examples showed that networks can be right for reasons quite unlike ours. [25, 26]

Bias. In 2018 Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial systems that guessed people's gender from photos. They found error rates below 1 per cent for lighter-skinned men but up to about 35 per cent for darker-skinned women. The systems had learned from datasets dominated by lighter-skinned faces. When such systems are used by police or employers, these errors can do real harm. [27]

Cost and concentration. Training the largest networks began to need enormous computing power, energy and money. A 2019 study estimated that developing one large language model, including the search for its best design, could emit as much carbon dioxide as five cars over their lifetimes. Only a few giant companies, such as Google, Facebook, Microsoft, Amazon and Baidu, could afford the hardware and the data, and they hired many of the field's top researchers from universities. [28]

In 2019 the Turing Award went to Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun for their decades of work on deep learning. The backwater of the 1990s had become the main road of computing.

13. How Did They Know? Measuring "Human-Level" Accuracy

How did they know? A researcher competes against a network

When networks' ImageNet error rates fell below 10 per cent, people began asking: how well do humans do? It sounds obvious that people can recognise photos, but ImageNet's 1,000 categories include around 120 breeds of dog and many obscure objects. In 2014 Andrej Karpathy, then a PhD student at Stanford, measured it himself. [3]

  1. Build a fair interface: a tool showing a test photo and the 1,000 categories, with example images of each, so a person could pick five guesses just as the networks did.
  2. Train the human: Karpathy practised on 500 images to learn the categories, since an untrained person wouldn't know the difference between, say, a Norwich terrier and a Norfolk terrier.
  3. Test on unseen images: he then labelled 1,500 test images, taking about a minute each.
  4. Analyse the errors: he got 5.1 per cent wrong. Many of his mistakes were fine-grained (dog breeds) or simply categories he didn't know existed; the network's mistakes were often different, such as failing on small, thin or oddly drawn objects.

So "about 5 per cent" became the human benchmark, and when ResNet reached 3.6 per cent in 2015, headlines said machines had surpassed humans at image recognition. The careful reading is narrower: one trained, patient human, on this specific test, made more errors than the best network. Networks still failed in ways no person would, as adversarial examples showed. Benchmarks measure what they measure, and no more.

14. Legend and Record: Did AlphaGo Teach Itself?

Legend and Record: what AlphaGo learned, and from whom

The legend: AlphaGo taught itself to play Go, from nothing, and surpassed thousands of years of human knowledge on its own. [20, 21]

The record: the AlphaGo that beat Lee Sedol in 2016 began by learning from people. Its policy network was first trained to predict human moves, using about 30 million positions from games between strong amateur players on an internet Go server. Only then did it improve through self-play and reinforcement learning. Its designers also chose its overall structure, including the search method.

The claim of learning "from scratch" belongs to its successor, AlphaGo Zero (2017), which was given only the rules of the game and learned everything else by playing itself, and became even stronger. Even so, "from scratch" needs a footnote: the system's design, the choice of what to learn and how to search, all came from human researchers, and it used vast amounts of computing power.

Why it matters: the difference is real and important. AlphaGo Zero showed that, for a game with fixed rules and a clear winner, self-play alone can go beyond human knowledge. But most real-world problems lack fixed rules and a clear score, and there, learning from human data remains essential, as the next part shows.

15. Philosophy Corner: Was Move 37 Creative?

Philosophy Corner: Lovelace's objection, 170 years on

In 1843 Ada Lovelace wrote that a machine "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform" (Part 1). Was AlphaGo's move 37 an answer to her? [29, 20]

Yes, it was creative: no one ordered AlphaGo to play move 37, and no one knew it was good. It was new, surprising and valuable, which is how many people define creativity. Top Go players have since studied AlphaGo's games and changed how they play. The mathematician Marcus du Sautoy has argued that such moments show machines can contribute genuinely new ideas.

No, it was search: AlphaGo was built to choose whichever move its networks and search rated as most likely to win. Move 37 was surprising to humans, but for AlphaGo it was simply the highest-scoring option. It didn't intend to surprise, take a risk or express anything. On this view, creativity needs understanding and intention, not just novelty.

A middle view: perhaps creativity comes in kinds. Exploring a well-defined space of possibilities in new ways, as AlphaGo did, is one kind, and machines can clearly do it. Inventing a new game, or deciding that Go is worth playing at all, may be another. Lovelace was right that the machine did what it was built to do. But what it was built to do turned out to include producing ideas its builders never had.
