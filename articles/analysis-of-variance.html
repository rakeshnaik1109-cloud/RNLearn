Analysis of Variance: Comparing Many Groups at Once

Three catalysts, four fertilisers, five adhesives: do the groups really differ? Here is the analysis of variance, the workhorse of experimental science, from the idea of signal against noise to finding which groups differ.

1. More Than Two Groups

A chemist tries four catalysts and measures the yield of a reaction five times with each. A biologist grows seedlings under three light colours. An engineer tests the strength of joints made with five adhesives. In each case the question is the same: do the groups really differ, or is the variation between their averages just the ordinary scatter of measurements?

For two groups, Part 4 compared means with a t-based interval or test. For three or more, the standard tool is the analysis of variance, or ANOVA, invented by Ronald Fisher in the 1920s to make sense of agricultural field trials. [1, 2] It is one of the most widely used methods in experimental science. This part explains why it is needed, how it works, what the F ratio means, how to check its assumptions and what to do when they fail, how to find out which groups differ, and how to report the result.

3. Why Not Lots of t-Tests?

The obvious approach is to compare every pair of groups with a t-test. With four catalysts that is six tests; with five groups, ten; with ten groups, forty-five. The trouble is that each test has a 5% chance of a false positive when there is no real difference, and those chances add up. Run enough tests and you are almost guaranteed to “find” something.

The simple formula 1 − 0.95m for m independent tests gives 26% for six tests and 40% for ten. Pairwise tests are not quite independent, because each group takes part in several of them, so the real rate is a little lower, but still far above 5%: in simulations with 10 measurements per group and no true differences, at least one pairwise t-test comes out “significant” about 12% of the time with three groups, 28% with five and over 60% with ten. This is the multiple comparisons problem, and it is one of the main routes by which false findings enter the scientific literature.

ANOVA avoids the problem by asking a single question first: is there any difference at all among the group means? Only if the answer is yes do we go on to ask which groups differ, using methods that keep the overall error rate under control (Section 10).

4. The Big Idea: Signal Against Noise

Despite its name, the analysis of variance is a test about means. It gets there by comparing two kinds of variation.

  • Variation within groups: how much individual measurements scatter around their own group’s mean. This is the noise: the measurement error and natural variability that would be there even if every group were treated identically.
  • Variation between groups: how far the group means sit from the overall (grand) mean. Even with no real effect, sample means differ a little by chance; if the treatments really matter, they differ more.

If the between-group variation is much larger than you would expect from the within-group noise, the groups probably differ. The word variance itself, and the idea of splitting it into parts due to different causes, come from a 1918 paper by Fisher on inheritance. [3] The following year he joined the Rothamsted Experimental Station, where decades of crop records gave him the problems that shaped the method.

5. Sums of Squares and the ANOVA Table

The calculation partitions the total variation exactly. For k groups with N measurements in total, grand mean x̄, group means x̄j and group sizes nj:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(x_{ij}-\bar{x})^2}_{SS_{\text{total}}} = \underbrace{\sum_j n_j(\bar{x}_j-\bar{x})^2}_{SS_{\text{between}}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j)^2}_{SS_{\text{within}}}$$

Each sum of squares is divided by its degrees of freedom to give a mean square: k − 1 for between groups (k means around one grand mean) and N − k for within groups (N values around k group means). The ratio of the two mean squares is the F statistic:

$$F = \frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} = \frac{SS_{\text{between}}/(k-1)}{SS_{\text{within}}/(N-k)}.$$

MSwithin estimates the noise variance σ² whatever the group means are. MSbetween also estimates σ² if the groups have the same true mean, but is inflated if they do not. So F is near 1 when there is no effect, and large when there is. The results are traditionally set out in an ANOVA table. Fisher and Winifred Mackenzie published the first analysis of variance in recognisably modern form in 1923, for an experiment on how potato varieties respond to fertilisers. [4] For the catalyst experiment worked through in Section 13:

Notice that the sums of squares and the degrees of freedom both add up: 62.09 + 10.63 = 72.72, and 3 + 16 = 19. The within-groups mean square, 0.664, is the pooled estimate of the measurement variance; its square root, 0.82 percentage points, is the typical scatter of a single run.

6. The F Distribution

How large must F be before we believe the groups differ? If the groups have the same true mean and the assumptions hold, F follows a known distribution, the F distribution, with two degrees of freedom: k − 1 and N − k. Fisher worked out its form in the 1920s, but he tabulated a transformed quantity, z = ½ ln F. In 1934 the American statistician George Snedecor published tables of the ratio itself and named it F in Fisher’s honour. [5] The p-value is the area of the distribution beyond the observed F.

For the catalysts, F(3, 16) = 31.1, far beyond the 5% critical value of 3.24: p is less than one in a million. A significant F says that at least one group mean differs from the others. It does not say which, or by how much.

With only two groups, the ANOVA F equals the square of the pooled two-sample t statistic, and the two tests give identical p-values. ANOVA is the generalisation of the t-test to any number of groups.

7. How Big Is the Effect?

As with any test, a tiny p-value can come from a trivial effect measured very precisely. ANOVA results should therefore be reported with an effect size: [6, 7]

  • Eta squared, η² = SSbetween/SStotal: the share of the total variation explained by the groups. It is simple, but slightly overestimates the effect in the population, especially for small samples.
  • Omega squared, ω² = (SSbetween − (k − 1)MSwithin)/(SStotal + MSwithin): a less biased version, preferred for reporting.
  • Cohen’s f = √(η²/(1 − η²)), used in power calculations. Cohen suggested 0.10, 0.25 and 0.40 as rough guides to small, medium and large effects in the behavioural sciences, a convention that should not be applied blindly to physical measurements.

For the catalysts, η² = 0.85 and ω² = 0.82: the choice of catalyst explains over 80% of the variation in yield, a very large effect, as is common for well-controlled laboratory experiments.

8. Checking the Assumptions

The classical one-way ANOVA assumes three things:

  1. Independence. Each measurement is independent of the others. This is the most important assumption and the one no test can check; it depends on how the experiment was done. Repeated measurements on the same sample, or runs done in batches on different days, violate it and need the methods of Part 6.
  2. Normality. Within each group, the measurements (equivalently, the residuals, each value minus its group mean) are roughly normal. Check with a histogram or Q–Q plot of the residuals (Part 2). ANOVA is fairly robust to non-normality, especially with equal group sizes of moderate size. [8]
  3. Equal variances (homogeneity of variance). The scatter within each group is similar. Plot the data by group, or the residuals against the group means. A formal check is Levene’s test (1960), an ANOVA on the absolute deviations of each value from its group centre; the Brown–Forsythe version uses the median as the centre and is more robust. [9, 10]

How much do violations matter? It depends on the design. With equal group sizes, ANOVA tolerates moderately unequal variances and skewed data well. With unequal group sizes and unequal variances, it can fail badly: if the smallest group is the noisiest, the false-positive rate can be several times the nominal 5%. Figure 5 lets you see this.

A caution about formal tests of assumptions: with small samples they have little power to detect real problems, and with large samples they flag trivial departures. Plots and knowledge of the measurement process are usually more informative than a p-value from a normality or variance test.

9. When Assumptions Fail: Welch and Kruskal–Wallis

Welch’s ANOVA (1951) extends Welch’s unequal-variance t-test to several groups. It weights each group by n/s² and adjusts the degrees of freedom, so it does not assume equal variances. [11] When the variances are in fact equal, it loses very little power, so many statisticians now recommend it as the default for one-way comparisons.

The Kruskal–Wallis test (1952) replaces the values by their ranks and performs a rank-based analysis, so it does not assume normality and is resistant to outliers. [12] It is the many-group version of the Mann–Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) test. It is often described as a test of medians, but strictly it tests whether the groups come from the same distribution. If the groups differ in spread, it can reject even when the medians are equal, as Figure 5 shows.

A third option, often the best, is to transform the data. If the spread grows with the mean, as with concentrations, counts or growth rates, taking logarithms often makes the variances equal and the distributions more symmetric at the same time (Part 2).

10. Which Groups Differ?

A significant ANOVA invites the next question. Methods for answering it while controlling the familywise error rate, the probability of at least one false positive among all the comparisons, include:

  • Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD), for comparing all pairs. It uses the distribution of the range of several means (the “studentised range”), so the whole set of intervals has a joint 95% confidence. [13] It is the standard choice for all pairwise comparisons.
  • Bonferroni’s correction: test each of m comparisons at level 0.05/m. Simple and always valid, but conservative when m is large. [14]
  • Holm’s method: sort the p-values from smallest to largest and compare them with 0.05/m, 0.05/(m − 1), and so on, stopping at the first that fails. It controls the same error rate and is always at least as powerful as Bonferroni. [15]
  • Dunnett’s test, for comparing several treatments with one control, without spending error rate on treatment-versus-treatment comparisons that are not of interest. [16]
  • Planned contrasts: if before the experiment you had a specific question, such as “do the two new catalysts together beat the two old ones?”, test it directly. A few planned comparisons need little or no correction.

When there are hundreds or thousands of comparisons, as in genomics, imaging or screening many machine-learning models, controlling the chance of even one false positive becomes too strict. Benjamini and Hochberg’s false discovery rate (1995) instead controls the expected proportion of false positives among the results declared significant. [17]

11. ANOVA Is Regression in Disguise

A one-way ANOVA can be written as a linear regression. Code the groups with indicator (dummy) variables: xB = 1 for catalyst B and 0 otherwise, and similarly for C and D. Then

$$y = \beta_0 + \beta_B x_B + \beta_C x_C + \beta_D x_D + \varepsilon,$$

where β0 is the mean of the reference group A and each other coefficient is a group’s difference from A. Fitting this regression gives exactly the same F test. For the catalysts, β0 = 72.96, βB = 2.74, βC = 0.40 and βD = 4.32.

This is more than a curiosity. ANOVA, t-tests and regression are all special cases of the general linear model, which is how statistical software actually computes them. [18] It also shows how to extend ANOVA: add more factors, add continuous covariates, or allow for repeated measurements, which is the subject of Part 6. Part 7 treats regression in its own right.

12. Reporting an ANOVA

  • Describe the design: the groups, the number of measurements in each, and how units were assigned to groups.
  • Give the group means with standard deviations or confidence intervals, ideally in a plot showing the individual data points.
  • Report the test with both degrees of freedom and an effect size: “F(3, 16) = 31.1, p < 0.001, ω² = 0.82”. Say whether you used the classical or Welch’s ANOVA.
  • State how you checked the assumptions and what you found.
  • Name the follow-up method (Tukey, Holm, Dunnett or planned contrasts) and give the differences with their adjusted confidence intervals, not just stars.

13. Worked Example: Four Catalysts

A reaction is run five times with each of four catalysts, in random order. Yields (%):

  • A: 72.1, 74.3, 73.0, 71.8, 73.6 (mean 72.96, SD 1.04)
  • B: 75.2, 76.8, 74.9, 76.1, 75.5 (mean 75.70, SD 0.76)
  • C: 73.4, 72.6, 74.1, 73.9, 72.8 (mean 73.36, SD 0.66)
  • D: 77.0, 78.3, 76.4, 77.8, 76.9 (mean 77.28, SD 0.76)
  1. Look at the data. D and B look higher; A and C lower. The spreads are similar.
  2. ANOVA. Grand mean 74.825. SSbetween = 62.09 (df 3), SSwithin = 10.63 (df 16), so F = 20.70/0.664 = 31.1, p = 6.5 × 10⁻⁷. The catalysts differ.
  3. Effect size. η² = 0.85, ω² = 0.82.
  4. Assumptions. Brown–Forsythe test for equal variances: p = 0.78. Shapiro–Wilk test on the residuals: p = 0.39. The residual plots show nothing alarming. Welch’s ANOVA agrees: F(3, 8.8) = 28.8, p = 7 × 10⁻⁵; so does Kruskal–Wallis: H = 15.9, p = 0.001.
  5. Which differ? Tukey’s HSD with MSwithin = 0.664 and n = 5 gives a critical difference of 4.05 × √(0.664/5) = 1.48 percentage points. Every pair differs except A and C (difference 0.40). The closest call is D versus B: 1.58, 95% Tukey interval 0.11 to 3.06.
  6. Report. “Yield differed between catalysts (F(3, 16) = 31.1, p < 0.001, ω² = 0.82). Tukey comparisons showed D gave the highest yield, 1.6 points (95% CI 0.1 to 3.1) above B, which in turn exceeded A and C; A and C did not differ detectably.”
Try it in Python: ANOVA, assumption checks and Tukey’s HSD 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.multicomp import pairwise_tukeyhsd
from statsmodels.stats.oneway import anova_oneway

A = [72.1, 74.3, 73.0, 71.8, 73.6]; B = [75.2, 76.8, 74.9, 76.1, 75.5]
C = [73.4, 72.6, 74.1, 73.9, 72.8]; D = [77.0, 78.3, 76.4, 77.8, 76.9]

print(stats.f_oneway(A, B, C, D))                  # F = 31.14, p = 6.5e-07
print(stats.levene(A, B, C, D))                    # Brown-Forsythe: p = 0.78
print(anova_oneway([A, B, C, D], use_var="unequal"))  # Welch: F = 28.8
print(stats.kruskal(A, B, C, D))                   # H = 15.87, p = 0.0012

y = np.concatenate([A, B, C, D]); g = np.repeat(list("ABCD"), 5)
print(pairwise_tukeyhsd(y, g))                     # all pairs differ except A-C

14. Try It Yourself: Paper Helicopters

Try it yourself: does wing length change the flight time?
  1. Make paper helicopters (a strip of paper cut and folded into two rotor blades, with a paper clip as weight) with three rotor lengths: short, medium and long. Make at least two of each, so differences between individual helicopters are part of the noise.
  2. Drop each from the same height (2 m or more) and time the fall. Get 6–10 timings per rotor length, in a random order rather than all short ones first.
  3. Plot the times by group. Compute the group means and SDs, then the ANOVA table by hand or with the Python code above.
  4. Is F large? Which lengths differ (use Tukey)? Calculate η²: how much of the variation is due to rotor length, and how much to timing error and wobble?

The paper helicopter is a classic teaching experiment in design of experiments. Think about what else varies: the drop height, the person timing, air currents. Part 6 shows how to include such factors in the analysis, and Part 9 how to plan around them.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did Fisher invent the F-test?

The legend: Fisher invented the F-test and named it after himself.

The record: Fisher developed the analysis of variance and derived the distribution needed to test it. But he did not work with the ratio of mean squares directly: he used z = ½ ln(MSbetween/MSwithin), which suited his mathematics and his tables. In 1934 George Snedecor of Iowa State College, who did much to spread Fisher’s methods in the United States, published tables of the plain ratio, which is easier to use, and called it F in Fisher’s honour. [5, 2]

The grain of truth: the ideas are Fisher’s; the letter and the convenient tables are Snedecor’s. The analysis of variance itself grew out of very practical work at Rothamsted, where Fisher needed to separate the effects of fertilisers and varieties from the natural variation of soil across a field.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: should we correct for multiple comparisons?

Corrections for multiple comparisons seem like obvious good practice. Yet in 1990 the epidemiologist Kenneth Rothman argued that “no adjustments are needed”: nature does not know how many tests you ran, and a correction that makes each test stricter also makes you miss more real effects. [19] Whether a result is surprising should depend on the evidence for it, not on how many other questions happened to be asked in the same paper.

Others reply that without corrections, a scientist who tests twenty hypotheses will almost always find something to publish, and that is exactly how false findings accumulate. A different answer comes from Bayesian statistics: Andrew Gelman and colleagues argue that fitting all the comparisons together in one multilevel model, which pulls extreme estimates towards the overall mean, deals with the problem more naturally than adjusting p-values afterwards. [20]

What everyone agrees on is honesty about the number of comparisons. The real danger is not the correction method but hidden flexibility: trying many analyses and reporting only the one that worked.

Questions to think about: If two scientists test the same hypothesis on the same data, but one also tests nine other hypotheses, should their conclusions about the first hypothesis differ? What would you need to know to decide?
