1. Why Logarithms?

A whisper and a jet engine differ in sound power by a factor of about a hundred billion. The smallest bacterium and the observable universe differ in size by a factor of about 1033. A loan, a bacterial culture or an epidemic can grow from a handful to millions in weeks. Ordinary numbers and ordinary graph paper cannot cope with ranges like these. The logarithm can: it measures a number by how many times you must multiply to reach it, so that a factor of a hundred billion becomes a step of just 11.

Logarithms began in 1614 as a trick for saving labour, turning the long multiplications of astronomers and navigators into simple additions. They became one of the most useful ideas in science. They run the scales of acidity, loudness, earthquakes and starlight; they describe growth, decay and doubling; they measure information in bits; they make computer algorithms fast and machine learning stable; and they even seem to shape how we perceive the world.

This article starts from the definition and works up to logarithms in calculus, statistics, information theory and computing, with interactive figures and short Python snippets you can run yourself.

3. What Is a Logarithm?

Start with powers. 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000, which we write 103 = 1000. The logarithm asks the reverse question: to what power must 10 be raised to give 1000? The answer, 3, is the logarithm of 1000 to base 10:

Definition
$$\log_b x = y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^{\,y} = x \qquad (b > 0,\ b \neq 1,\ x > 0)$$

So log10 100 = 2, log2 8 = 3, and log10 0.01 = −2, because 10−2 = 1/100. Logarithms need not be whole numbers: log10 2 ≈ 0.301, because 100.301 ≈ 2. A logarithm is simply an exponent, found backwards.

Three bases are used almost everywhere:

  • Base 10, the common logarithm, written log10 or just log. It counts factors of ten, matching our decimal number system. Scientific scales such as pH and decibels use it.
  • Base e ≈ 2.71828, the natural logarithm, written ln. It arises naturally in calculus, growth and decay (Section 7). In most mathematics, physics and programming, “log” with no base means ln.
  • Base 2, the binary logarithm, written log2 or lg. It counts doublings and halvings, so it rules computer science and information theory.

Some facts hold for every base. logb 1 = 0, because b0 = 1. logb b = 1. Numbers between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms. And the logarithm of zero or of a negative number is undefined among the real numbers: no power of a positive base ever reaches zero or goes below it. The logarithm also grows very slowly. Going from 1 to a million moves log10 only from 0 to 6.

import math
import numpy as np

# The three logarithms you will meet most often
print(math.log10(1000))      # 3.0   common log, base 10
print(math.log2(1024))       # 10.0  binary log, base 2
print(math.log(math.e))      # 1.0   natural log, base e ≈ 2.71828
print(math.log(81, 3))       # 4.0   any base: math.log(x, base)

# Change of base: log_b(x) = ln(x) / ln(b)
print(math.log(81) / math.log(3))        # 4.0 again

# NumPy works on whole arrays at once. Careful: np.log is the NATURAL log!
x = np.array([1, 10, 100, 0.01])
print(np.log10(x))           # [ 0.  1.  2. -2.]
print(np.log(x))             # natural logs, not base 10

# Logs undo powers, and powers undo logs
print(10 ** np.log10(42.0), np.exp(np.log(42.0)))   # 42.0 42.0

4. The Rules of Logarithms

Because logarithms are exponents, they inherit the rules of exponents. Since bm × bn = bm+n, multiplying numbers adds their logarithms. Everything else follows:

The rules
$$\log(ab) = \log a + \log b \qquad \log\frac{a}{b} = \log a - \log b \qquad \log a^{n} = n\log a$$ $$\log_b x = \frac{\log_k x}{\log_k b} \quad \text{(change of base)}$$

These four lines are the whole reason logarithms were invented. To multiply 3,456 by 7,891 before calculators, you looked up their logarithms in a table (3.5386 and 3.8971), added them (7.4357), and looked up the number with that logarithm: about 27,270,000. The exact answer is 27,271,296. A square root became a division by 2; a cube root, a division by 3.

The change-of-base rule means any base can be computed from any other. Calculators and computers usually compute ln, and get log10 x as ln x / ln 10 and log2 x as ln x / ln 2. Logarithms to different bases are all proportional to one another: switching base just rescales them, like converting metres to feet.

What the rules do not say

There is no rule for log(a + b). log(2 + 8) = log 10 = 1, but log 2 + log 8 = log 16 ≈ 1.204. Likewise (log a)2 is not log(a2) = 2 log a, and log a / log b is not log(a/b).

5. Napier, Bürgi and Briggs

In the late 1500s astronomy and navigation demanded enormous calculations with trigonometric tables, and mathematicians looked for ways to replace multiplication with addition. The Scottish landowner John Napier spent some twenty years building one. In 1614 he published Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio (A Description of the Wonderful Canon of Logarithms), with 90 pages of tables. [1] He coined the word logarithm from the Greek logos (ratio) and arithmos (number): “ratio-numbers”.

Napier pictured two points moving along lines: one at constant speed, the other slowing in proportion to its remaining distance. His logarithms were not to any base in the modern sense; in today’s notation his logarithm of x was 107 ln(107/x), chosen so that the sines in his tables, scaled to ten million, gave whole numbers. [2] [3]

Independently, the Swiss clockmaker Jost Bürgi, instrument-maker to the emperor in Prague and a colleague of the astronomer Johannes Kepler, computed a similar table by repeatedly multiplying by 1.0001. He published it only in 1620, after Napier. [4] [3]

Henry Briggs, professor of geometry at Gresham College in London, read Napier’s book with delight and travelled to Edinburgh in 1615 to meet him. Together they agreed that tables would be easier to use if the logarithm of 1 were 0 and the logarithm of 10 were 1. Briggs computed these common logarithms: first for 1 to 1,000 in 1617, then in his Arithmetica Logarithmica of 1624 for 1 to 20,000 and 90,000 to 100,000, each to 14 decimal places, by hand. [5] The gap was filled by the Dutch publisher Adriaan Vlacq in 1628. Kepler used logarithms to compute his Rudolphine Tables of planetary motion, published in 1627, and had earlier praised Napier in a dedicatory letter printed in his Ephemerides for 1620, not knowing that Napier had died in 1617. For the next 350 years, a book of log tables sat on every scientist’s and engineer’s desk.

6. The Slide Rule

If multiplying numbers means adding logarithms, and adding is just putting lengths end to end, then a ruler marked with numbers placed at distances proportional to their logarithms can multiply. In 1620 Edmund Gunter, professor of astronomy at Gresham College in London, drew such a scale and used it with a pair of dividers. Around 1622 William Oughtred, an English clergyman and mathematician, slid two of Gunter’s scales against each other, inventing the slide rule; he described it in print in 1632. [6] [2]

On a log scale the distance from 1 to 2 equals the distance from 2 to 4, from 3 to 6 and from 5 to 10: every equal ratio is an equal length. A slide rule gives about three significant figures, and the user must keep track of the decimal point. That was enough to design bridges, aircraft and the Apollo spacecraft. Slide rules were standard equipment for engineers until cheap electronic calculators replaced them in the 1970s.

7. The Natural Logarithm and e

Why would anyone choose a base as odd as 2.71828…? Because it appears whether you start from money or from geometry.

From money. Invest 1 at 100% interest for a year. Paid once, you end with 2. Paid as 50% twice, (1.5)2 = 2.25. Paid monthly, (1 + 1/12)12 ≈ 2.613. Paid ever more often, the total does not grow without limit; it approaches a fixed number. Jacob Bernoulli posed this problem in 1683 and showed the limit lies between 2 and 3. [3] Leonhard Euler named it e and, in his Introductio in analysin infinitorum of 1748, defined logarithms as exponents and gave e to 23 decimal places. [7]

Euler’s number
$$e = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n} = 2.718281828459\ldots$$

From geometry. In 1647 the Flemish Jesuit Grégoire de Saint-Vincent found that the area under the hyperbola y = 1/x grows by equal amounts when x grows by equal ratios: the area from 1 to 2 equals that from 2 to 4 and from 4 to 8. In 1649 his student Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa pointed out that this area therefore behaves exactly like a logarithm. [3] It is the natural logarithm: the area from 1 to t is ln t, and it reaches exactly 1 at t = e.

The two roads meet because ex is the one function that is its own rate of change, and ln is its inverse. That makes e the natural base wherever something changes in proportion to its size: populations, radioactive atoms, interest, cooling coffee, charging capacitors. A quantity growing continuously at rate k is N(t) = N0ekt, and its logarithm, ln N = ln N0 + kt, is a straight line in time.

8. Orders of Magnitude and Log Scales

The integer part of log10 x tells you how many digits a number has, minus one: its order of magnitude. A virus (about 10−7 m) and a person (about 1 m) are seven orders of magnitude apart; a person and the Earth (about 107 m) another seven. Scientists think in orders of magnitude to check whether an answer is even roughly right.

A logarithmic axis places values by their logarithm, so each step along the axis is the same factor, usually ten. It lets one chart show tiny and huge values together and turns exponential growth into a straight line.

A log axis answers different questions from a linear one. It shows growth rates and relative changes: a fall from 1,000 to 500 looks the same as one from 10 to 5. A linear axis shows absolute amounts: 500 extra cases looks bigger than 5. Neither is wrong, but readers must notice which one they are looking at. The plots series covers log axes in charts in more detail in Scientific Plots and Choosing the Right Chart.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

things = {"atom": 1e-10, "virus": 1e-7, "cell": 1e-5, "ant": 3e-3, "person": 1.7,
          "Everest": 8.8e3, "Earth": 1.27e7, "Sun": 1.39e9, "light-year": 9.46e15}
names, sizes = list(things), np.array(list(things.values()))

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(9, 3.6))
ax1.scatter(sizes, np.zeros_like(sizes))
ax1.set_title("Linear axis: everything except the last point is squashed at zero")
ax2.scatter(sizes, np.zeros_like(sizes))
ax2.set_xscale("log")                                  # one tick per factor of ten
ax2.set_title("Logarithmic axis: 26 factors of ten fit comfortably")
for n, s in zip(names, sizes):
    ax2.annotate(n, (s, 0), textcoords="offset points", xytext=(0, 8), ha="center", fontsize=8)
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_yticks([])
ax2.set_xlabel("Size (metres)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. Doubling Times and Half-Lives

Whenever a quantity grows or shrinks by a fixed percentage per step, the question “how long until…?” needs a logarithm, because the unknown is in the exponent. Solving (1 + r)t = 2 for t by taking logs of both sides gives the doubling time:

Doubling time and half-life
$$t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln(1+r)} \approx \frac{0.693}{r} \qquad\qquad t = T_{1/2}\,\frac{\ln(N_0/N)}{\ln 2}$$

Because ln 2 ≈ 0.693 and ln(1 + r) ≈ r for small r, a quantity growing at 7% a year doubles in about 70 / 7 = 10 years: the rule of 70. Bankers use 72, which divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12.

Decay works the same way in reverse. Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years, so a sample with 30% of its original carbon-14 is 5,730 × ln(1/0.3) / ln 2 ≈ 9,950 years old. Doubling time also gives a feel for exponential growth: 10 doublings is a factor of 1,024, and 20 doublings a factor of about a million. A pond weed that doubles daily and covers the pond on day 30 covered half of it on day 29.

import math

# Money growing at 7% a year: when does it double?
r = 0.07
t_exact = math.log(2) / math.log(1 + r)          # solve (1 + r)^t = 2 for t
print(f"Doubling time at 7%: {t_exact:.2f} years (rule of 70 says {70 / 7:.0f})")

# Radioactive decay: carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years.
# A sample has 30% of its original carbon-14 left. How old is it?
half_life = 5730
age = half_life * math.log(1 / 0.30) / math.log(2)   # solve (1/2)^(t/T) = 0.30
print(f"Age: {age:,.0f} years")

# Growth from two measurements: 1,200 cases on day 10, 9,600 on day 25
k = math.log(9600 / 1200) / (25 - 10)                 # continuous growth rate per day
print(f"Growth rate: {k:.3f} per day, doubling every {math.log(2) / k:.1f} days")

10. Logarithmic Scales in Science

Several of the best-known scales in science are logarithms in disguise. On each, a step of one means multiplying by a fixed factor, not adding a fixed amount.

  • pH. In 1909 the Danish chemist Søren Sørensen, working at the Carlsberg Laboratory, proposed measuring acidity by the negative logarithm of the hydrogen-ion concentration. [8] Pure water has pH 7; lemon juice, about 2.5, has some 30,000 times more hydrogen ions.
  • Decibels. The Bell Telephone System measured signal loss in units based on the logarithm of the power ratio, and in 1929 named the tenth part of such a unit the decibel, after Alexander Graham Bell. [9] Every 10 dB is a factor of ten in power.
  • Earthquake magnitude. In 1935 Charles Richter defined the magnitude of Californian earthquakes as the logarithm of the largest amplitude on a standard seismograph at a standard distance. [10] Each step of one is ten times the amplitude and roughly 32 times the energy. Because the original scale saturates for great earthquakes, seismologists now use the moment magnitude of Hanks and Kanamori, based on the area and slip of the fault. [11]
  • Star magnitudes. Hipparchus and Ptolemy sorted stars into six classes by brightness. In 1856 Norman Pogson made the scale exact: five magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in brightness. [12]

The danger of these scales is that they hide the size of a change. A magnitude 7 earthquake sounds a little worse than a magnitude 6; it releases about 32 times as much energy. A sound at 90 dB carries 1,000 times the power of one at 60 dB.

11. Perception and the Mind

In the 1830s the German physiologist Ernst Weber found that the smallest change in weight a person can feel is a fixed fraction of the weight already held: about the same fraction whether it is light or heavy. In 1860 Gustav Fechner built on this to propose that the strength of a sensation grows with the logarithm of the stimulus, so that equal ratios feel like equal steps. [13] The Weber–Fechner law is only an approximation (later psychologists such as S. S. Stevens argued for power laws instead), but it explains why the decibel and star-magnitude scales feel natural: they match how we hear and see.

Our sense of number may work the same way. When children are asked to mark numbers on a line from 1 to 100, young ones space small numbers far apart and squash the large ones together, a roughly logarithmic pattern; older children shift towards the accurate linear placement. [14] In 2008 Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues found that adults of the Mundurucu people of the Amazon, whose language has few number words and who had little formal schooling, also placed quantities on a logarithmic line. [15] Linear spacing seems to be something we learn with measurement and schooling.

12. Log–Log Plots and Power Laws

Many relationships in nature are power laws, y = a·xb: the area of a circle grows as the radius squared, the period of a planet as its distance to the power 3/2 (Kepler’s third law), and the metabolic rate of mammals roughly as body mass to the power 3/4 (Kleiber’s law). [16] Taking logs of both sides turns any power law into a straight line:

Power law on log–log axes
$$y = a\,x^{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log y = \log a + b\,\log x$$

So plotting log y against log x, a log–log plot, straightens a power law, and the slope of the line is the exponent. An exponential, y = a·ekx, is straightened instead by a semi-log plot (log y against plain x). Which plot gives a straight line tells you which kind of law you have. Be careful, though: almost any curve looks fairly straight over a short range on log–log axes, and claims of power laws in data such as city sizes or network links need proper statistical tests, not just a ruler.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
# synthetic animals: metabolic rate follows a power law of body mass, rate ≈ 3.4 · mass^0.75
mass = np.exp(rng.uniform(np.log(0.02), np.log(4000), 60))      # 20 g to 4 tonnes
rate = 3.4 * mass**0.75 * np.exp(rng.normal(0, 0.25, 60))

# Taking logs turns y = a·x^b into log y = log a + b·log x: a straight line
b, log_a = np.polyfit(np.log(mass), np.log(rate), 1)
print(f"Fitted exponent b = {b:.3f}, coefficient a = {np.exp(log_a):.2f}")

grid = np.logspace(np.log10(0.02), np.log10(4000), 100)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4.5))
ax.scatter(mass, rate, s=15)
ax.plot(grid, np.exp(log_a) * grid**b, color="#D55E00", label=f"rate = {np.exp(log_a):.1f} · mass^{b:.2f}")
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_yscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("Body mass (kg)")
ax.set_ylabel("Metabolic rate (W)")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

13. Information, Entropy and Bits

How much information is there in a message? In 1928 the Bell Labs engineer Ralph Hartley argued that it should be measured by the logarithm of the number of possible messages, so that two messages carry twice as much as one, even though the number of possibilities multiplies. [17] In 1948 Claude Shannon extended the idea to messages whose outcomes are not equally likely. [18] The information in an outcome of probability p is −log2 p, measured in bits, a word Shannon credited to his colleague John Tukey. The average over all outcomes is the entropy:

Shannon entropy
$$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad \text{bits}$$

A fair coin gives 1 bit, a fair die log2 6 ≈ 2.58 bits, and a random letter from 26 about 4.7 bits. Entropy sets the limit on how far data can be compressed. The same quantity, with natural logs, appears in physics as Boltzmann’s entropy, and in machine learning as the cross-entropy loss that trains classifiers (Section 15).

import numpy as np

def entropy_bits(probs):
    """Shannon entropy in bits: H = -sum(p log2 p)."""
    p = np.asarray(probs, dtype=float)
    p = p[p > 0]                                 # 0 · log 0 is taken as 0
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

print(entropy_bits([0.5, 0.5]))                  # 1.0 bit: a fair coin
print(entropy_bits([0.9, 0.1]))                  # 0.47 bits: a biased coin is more predictable
print(entropy_bits([1 / 6] * 6))                 # 2.585 bits = log2(6): a fair die
print(np.log2(26))                               # 4.70 bits to pick one letter out of 26 at random

# Cross-entropy loss used to train classifiers: -log(probability given to the right answer)
for p_right in [0.99, 0.6, 0.1]:
    print(f"model gives the correct class p = {p_right}: loss = {-np.log(p_right):.3f}")

14. Logarithms in Computing

Halving and algorithms. log2 N is the number of times you can halve N before reaching 1. Any method that cuts a problem in half at each step therefore takes about log2 N steps. Binary search finds an item in a sorted list of a billion in about 30 comparisons. Balanced search trees, efficient sorting (N log N comparisons), and the fast Fourier transform all owe their speed to logarithms. A number N needs about log2 N bits to store, which is why 32 bits count to about four billion.

Working in log space. Computers store numbers with limited range: a standard double-precision number loses precision below about 10−308 and cannot go below about 10−324 at all. The probability of a long sequence of events, a product of many small numbers, quickly underflows to zero. Statistics and machine learning software therefore works with log-probabilities and adds them instead of multiplying. To add probabilities stored as logs, the log-sum-exp trick subtracts the largest value first so that nothing overflows or underflows:

Log-sum-exp
$$\log\sum_i e^{a_i} = m + \log\sum_i e^{a_i - m}, \qquad m = \max_i a_i$$ 
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

# Multiplying many small probabilities underflows to zero...
p = np.full(1000, 0.01)
print(np.prod(p))                          # 0.0  (the true value is 1e-2000)

# ...so work with log-probabilities and ADD them instead
log_p = np.log(p)
print(log_p.sum())                         # -4605.17 = log(1e-2000), no problem

# Adding probabilities stored as logs: log(e^a + e^b) without overflow
a = np.array([-1000.0, -1001.0, -1002.0])
print(np.log(np.sum(np.exp(a))))           # -inf: exp(-1000) underflows
print(logsumexp(a))                         # -999.59, correct
# logsumexp subtracts the maximum first: max + log(sum(exp(a - max)))
m = a.max()
print(m + np.log(np.sum(np.exp(a - m))))   # the same trick by hand

15. Logarithms in Statistics and Machine Learning

Log transforms. Quantities produced by many multiplicative factors (incomes, concentrations, city sizes, reaction times, gene expression) are often strongly right-skewed and roughly log-normal. Taking logs makes them symmetric, so that means, standard deviations, t-tests and regression behave well, and multiplicative effects become additive. The mean of the logs, converted back, is the geometric mean.

Log-odds. Logistic regression models the log-odds of an event, ln(p / (1 − p)), as a straight line in the predictors. The log-odds run from −∞ to +∞ while p stays between 0 and 1, and each coefficient is a log of an odds ratio.

Log-likelihood. Fitting a model by maximum likelihood means maximising a product of probabilities, one for each observation. Its logarithm, the log-likelihood, is a sum, which is easier to differentiate and does not underflow, and it has its maximum in the same place. Information criteria such as AIC and the deviance of generalised linear models are built from it.

Cross-entropy. A classifier that gives probability p to the correct answer is charged −ln p. Confident correct answers cost almost nothing, a probability of 0.1 costs 2.3, and a confident wrong answer costs a great deal. Averaged over a data set, this is the cross-entropy loss used to train most neural networks, including large language models.

16. Benford’s Law

In 1881 the astronomer Simon Newcomb noticed that the early pages of books of logarithm tables, for numbers beginning with 1, were far more worn than the later ones. He proposed that the first digit d of everyday numbers occurs with probability log10(1 + 1/d). [19] In 1938 the physicist Frank Benford, apparently unaware of Newcomb, found the same pattern in 20,229 numbers from river areas, populations, physical constants, newspaper figures and addresses. [20] About 30% of such numbers start with 1, and under 5% with 9.

The explanation is logarithmic. Numbers that range over several orders of magnitude and arise from growth or multiplication tend to be spread evenly on a log scale, and on a log scale the stretch from 1 to 2 takes up 30% of each decade. The law is also the only first-digit pattern that stays the same when the units change, from metres to feet or rupees to dollars, and, as the mathematician Theodore Hill showed in 1995, it emerges when numbers are drawn from a random mixture of different distributions, as in a newspaper’s pages. [21] Auditors use departures from Benford’s law to flag possibly invented accounts, though a departure is a reason to look closer, not proof of fraud.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Quantities that grow by multiplying (here, random growth over many years) follow Benford's law
values = 100 * np.prod(rng.lognormal(0, 0.3, size=(20000, 30)), axis=1)
first_digit = (values / 10 ** np.floor(np.log10(values))).astype(int)

digits = np.arange(1, 10)
observed = np.array([(first_digit == d).mean() for d in digits])
benford = np.log10(1 + 1 / digits)                      # P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.bar(digits, observed, color="#0072B2", alpha=0.7, label="simulated data")
ax.plot(digits, benford, "o-", color="#D55E00", label="Benford: log10(1 + 1/d)")
ax.set_xticks(digits)
ax.set_xlabel("First digit")
ax.set_ylabel("Share of numbers")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

17. Logarithms in Calculus

The natural logarithm has the simplest possible rate of change:

Derivative and integral
$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x} \qquad\qquad \int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C$$

This fills a famous gap: the power rule ∫xn dx = xn+1/(n+1) fails for n = −1, and the logarithm is exactly the missing function. For any other base, d/dx logb x = 1 / (x ln b); only base e gives a clean 1/x, which is why mathematicians call it natural.

Near x = 0, Nicholas Mercator’s series of 1668 gives ln(1 + x) = x − x2/2 + x3/3 − …, so for small changes, ln(1 + x) ≈ x. That is why small differences of natural logs read as percentage changes.

Logarithmic differentiation, taking logs before differentiating, turns products and powers into sums and makes messy derivatives easy. The logarithm also measures the slowest kinds of growth that still have no limit: the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n grows like ln n, and the number of primes up to N is about N / ln N, the prime number theorem.

18. Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up bases in code. In Python, NumPy, R, Excel’s LN and most programming languages, log means the natural logarithm. Use log10 or log2 when you need those bases. (Excel’s LOG defaults to base 10, a common source of confusion.)
  • Inventing a rule for log(a + b). There is none. Only products, quotients and powers simplify.
  • Taking the log of zero or negative values. They have no real logarithm. Data with zeros are often transformed as log(x + 1), but the result depends on the units, so choose the offset with care, or use a method designed for counts.
  • Reading a log axis as linear. Equal heights on a log axis are equal ratios. A curve that “levels off” on a log axis may still be growing enormously in absolute terms.
  • Averaging on the wrong scale. The mean of the logs, converted back, is the geometric mean, which is smaller than the ordinary mean for skewed data. Say which one you report.
  • Forgetting what a log-scale step means. One pH unit, one earthquake magnitude and 10 dB are each a factor of ten, not a small increment.

19. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did Napier invent natural logarithms?

The legend: Napier invented natural logarithms, which is why they are sometimes called Napierian logarithms.

The record: Napier’s logarithms decreased as numbers increased and had no base in the modern sense; written in today’s notation they are 107 ln(107/x), close to base 1/e after scaling. [2] Logarithms to base e emerged later, from the areas under the hyperbola studied by Saint-Vincent and de Sarasa, and from Mercator’s series, and the number e itself was named by Euler more than a century after Napier. [3] [7] Napier’s great idea was the logarithm itself, not its natural base.

A second legend: Frank Benford discovered Benford’s law in 1938. The record: Simon Newcomb stated the same law, with the same formula, 57 years earlier, after noticing the worn first pages of logarithm tables. [19] Benford’s contribution was the large body of evidence, and the law took his name. It is a textbook case of what the statistician Stephen Stigler called his “law of eponymy”: no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer. [22]
