Before Science: How a Curious Ape Learned to Know

Long before universities, telescopes, or written words, hominins were meticulous experimentalists. From the fractured basalt cores of Turkana and the controlled flames of Wonderwerk Cave to geometric ochre and notched tally bones under the night sky, this is the story of how our lineage learned to read the physical world.

The Story at a Glance

This chapter covers a very long stretch of time, so here is the whole story on one line before we start. The coloured bars show roughly when each species lived, judging by when its fossils turn up; several of them lived at the same time. The pictures below the line mark key moments. Tap any picture to read about it, and use the buttons to zoom in on the last 400,000 or 100,000 years, where most of the action happens [1, 8, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25].

1. A Wrong Turn in Kenya

In July 2011 a team of archaeologists in northern Kenya took a wrong turn. They were driving across the dry riverbeds west of Lake Turkana, led by Sonia Harmand and Jason Lewis, and ended up on a ridge at a place called Lomekwi. Walking down the slope, team member Sammy Lokorodi noticed a large lump of dark volcanic rock lying on the surface. It had scooped-out scars where someone had knocked thick chips off it [1].

The team dug carefully at the spot, now called Lomekwi 3, and found 149 worked stones. They lay in a layer of sediment sandwiched between two layers of volcanic ash. Volcanic ash can be dated very precisely (section 10 explains how), and it put the tools at about 3.3 million years old [1].

That date was a surprise. Our own group of species, the genus Homo, does not appear until later: the oldest fossil anyone has assigned to Homo is a piece of jawbone from Ledi-Geraru in Ethiopia, about 2.8 million years old [22]. So the toolmakers at Lomekwi were not "humans" in the usual sense. They were earlier members of the human family, with brains about the size of a chimpanzee's. The likely candidates are Kenyanthropus platyops or Australopithecus afarensis, the species of the famous "Lucy" skeleton [21] [1].

Wild chimpanzees use stones too: in the Taï forest of West Africa they crack nuts by hitting them with a stone against a root. But they use the stone as it comes. The Lomekwi toolmakers did something different. They took heavy rocks (the largest about 15 kg), either banged them against a big stone on the ground or hit them with a hand-held stone, and did it with one aim: to break off thin, sharp flakes.

This is where the story of this series begins. An ape was no longer just using the world as it found it. It was learning how rock breaks, predicting what a blow would do, and making a cutting edge that nature had not provided.

2. How Stone Breaks

You can't make a tool from just any rock. Hit a lump of granite and it crumbles along the boundaries between its grains. Toolmakers need rocks that break the same way in every direction, with no grain or built-in weak planes. Flint, chert, fine-grained basalt and volcanic glass (obsidian) are like this [2]. Breaking such stone on purpose is called knapping.

When you strike flint with a hammerstone, the shock spreads out from the point of impact as a cone, a bit like the cone-shaped chip you see when a small stone hits a car windscreen. Physicists call this a Hertzian cone. If the blow lands near the edge of the stone, the cone runs out through the side and a flake comes away. The broken surface is smooth and curved like the inside of a shell, which is why archaeologists call it a conchoidal fracture, from the Greek word for a mussel shell [2].

Why the angle matters

Whether you get a good flake depends mostly on the angle between the surface you hit (the "platform") and the side of the stone. If that edge is fairly sharp, less than about 80°, the crack runs smoothly out through the side and a thin, razor-sharp feather flake comes off. If the edge is too blunt or the blow too steep, the crack stops partway and leaves an ugly step fracture, or the edge simply crushes. Every knapper, ancient or modern, learns this by feel.

The oldest tools in a continuous tradition, called Oldowan after Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, come from Gona in Ethiopia (2.6 million years ago) and Olduvai itself (1.9 million years ago). They are simple: a stone with a few flakes knocked off, and the sharp flakes themselves. But each one shows a maker who had learned, by trial and error, how stone behaves under a blow [2].

About 1.76 million years ago, at Kokiselei in Kenya, a new kind of tool appeared: the Acheulean handaxe [3]. A handaxe is a teardrop-shaped tool, worked on both faces and roughly the same on both sides. You can't make one by just knocking off flakes and seeing what happens. You have to hold a picture of the finished shape in your head and keep turning the stone, trimming one edge and then the other to keep it even. Long before anyone wrote down geometry, people were working to a mental plan with symmetry built in.

3. Fire and Cooking

Stone tools gave our ancestors sharper hands. Fire did something even bigger: it did part of their digesting for them.

Every animal has an energy budget, and for apes digestion is expensive. Raw roots and seeds are tough and fibrous, and much of their starch is locked up in a form our gut can barely break down. Raw meat takes a lot of chewing. A wild chimpanzee spends 5 to 7 hours a day just chewing to get enough food [6].

Cooking changes the food itself. Heated with water at around 60–80 °C, starch grains swell and burst (this is called gelatinisation, the same thing that thickens a sauce), so our digestive juices can get at them. Heat also turns the tough connective tissue in meat into soft gelatine. Cooked food takes far less chewing and gives more energy per mouthful [6].

The "expensive tissue" idea

In 1995 Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler pointed out a trade-off [7]. A few organs (brain, heart, kidneys, liver and gut) use most of the energy a resting body burns, even though together they are only a small part of its weight. Gram for gram, brain and gut burn energy at about the same rate. So an animal can't easily grow a much bigger brain unless it either eats a lot more or gets by with a smaller gut.

Cooking may be what made the smaller gut possible. Aiello and Wheeler found that the human gut is only about 60% of the size you would expect for a primate of our body size. Richard Wrangham later argued that cooking is what allowed this, freeing energy for a bigger brain [6, 7]. It is an attractive idea, but it is still a hypothesis and still debated.

When did people first control fire? This is hard to prove, because wildfires can scorch bones too. One of the strongest early cases comes from Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, published in 2012 [4]. The cave runs 140 metres into a hillside. In layers about a million years old, deep inside where no lightning-started fire could reach, Francesco Berna, Michael Chazan and their colleagues found tiny fragments of burned bone and plant ash [4].

They checked the ash with infrared spectroscopy, which shows how hot a material was heated, and found it had been burned right there, at the modest temperatures of a small fire of grass, leaves and twigs [4]. So about a million years ago someone, probably Homo erectus, was making fires inside the cave, even though no built hearth was found. By about 790,000 years ago, at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel, burned seeds, wood and flint are found clustered in patches, a sign of fire used again and again in the same places [5].

4. Growing a Bigger Brain

With sharp tools and cooked food, the fossil record shows a striking change: the brain kept getting bigger.

Over about six million years, the brain of our line roughly tripled in size. Early australopiths had brains of about 380–450 cm³, close to a chimpanzee's. Homo erectus reached about 850–1,100 cm³, and Neanderthals and modern humans average roughly 1,300–1,500 cm³. (A cm³ is about the size of a sugar cube, so a modern brain fills roughly a 1.4-litre bottle.)

Bigger animals tend to have bigger brains anyway, so size alone can mislead. To allow for body size, biologists use the encephalisation quotient (EQ), an idea worked out by Harry Jerison. It compares an animal's brain with the brain you would expect for a typical mammal of the same body weight: $$EQ = \frac{\text{actual brain mass}}{\text{expected brain mass for that body size}}$$ A typical mammal scores about 1 and a chimpanzee about 2.2–2.5. Modern humans score about 7 on Jerison's scale.

5. Where Our Species Began

For a long time textbooks told a tidy story: our species, Homo sapiens, appeared suddenly in one place in East Africa about 200,000 years ago (the evidence was skulls from Omo Kibish and Herto in Ethiopia) and then spread around the world, replacing all the other human species.

In June 2017 a team led by Jean-Jacques Hublin of the Max Planck Institute changed that picture [8]. At Jebel Irhoud, an old mine in Morocco on the far side of Africa, they found face bones, jaws and stone tools from at least five people. Some of the flint tools had been dropped in a fire, which let the team date them by a method that measures the energy stored in heated crystals (thermoluminescence, explained in section 10). Teeth gave a second date. Both pointed to about 315,000 years ago, more than 100,000 years earlier than the old story [8].

The Jebel Irhoud people had faces much like ours: small brow ridges and a flat, tucked-in face. But their skulls were long and low, not round like ours [8]. The lesson: Homo sapiens did not appear overnight in one Garden of Eden. Our body and mind took shape gradually, across many groups spread over Africa that met, mixed and swapped ideas whenever wetter climates opened green corridors between them.

6. Planning Ahead in Stone: the Levallois Method

Around 300,000 years ago, at about the time early Homo sapiens and the Neanderthals appear, stone toolmaking took a new turn. Archaeologists call it the Levallois method, after a suburb of Paris where such tools were first described.

In older methods you shaped the tool directly by trimming the stone. Levallois works the other way round. The knapper first shapes the remaining block so that one final blow will knock off a flake of a shape decided in advance. The block is a mould, and the flake is the product [2].

The steps go like this:

  1. Trim around the edge. Take a large flint nodule and knock flakes off all the way around it, so there is a usable edge to strike from.
  2. Shape the top into a dome. Remove overlapping flakes from the top face until it looks like a tortoise shell. These curves will guide the crack when the final blow comes.
  3. Prepare the striking spot. At one end, trim a small angled surface so the final blow lands at the right angle (about 75°).
  4. Strike once. One firm blow with a stone or antler hammer, and a broad, sharp-edged flake comes off. Its width, thickness and outline were all set by the earlier preparation.

This takes real foresight. The knapper has to resist using the good flakes that come off along the way, and put dozens of careful blows into shaping a block whose only job is to control one final break.

7. The First Glue: Making Tools with Handles

A sharp flake held in the hand has limits. Press hard and it cuts your fingers, and you can't throw it like a spear or swing it like an axe. The answer was the composite tool: a stone point fixed to a wooden handle or shaft.

Fixing stone to wood is harder than it sounds. Wood swells and shrinks with damp; stone doesn't. A hard blow cracks brittle glues such as plain dried tree resin. At Sibudu Cave in South Africa, about 70,000 years ago, people solved this with glues made from several ingredients [11].

Archaeologist Lyn Wadley and her colleagues studied the traces left on the bases of stone points and found that the glue followed a recipe:

  • Plant gum or resin, from trees such as acacia, to do the sticking.
  • Ground ochre, a red or yellow earth rich in iron, mixed in as a filler.
  • Sometimes fat or beeswax, to keep the glue from going brittle [11].

The ochre was not just for colour. Plain gum shrinks as it dries and develops tiny cracks that give way on impact. Fine, gritty ochre powder works like the sand in concrete: it spreads the stress and makes the glue tougher [11]. When Wadley's team made the glue themselves, they found it also had to be warmed carefully over embers. Too cool and the ingredients wouldn't mix; too hot and the resin bubbled and burned. Getting it right meant controlling several things at once, which is a lot like following a lab method.

8. Symbols: Putting Ideas into Objects

Animal signals are about the here and now. A vervet monkey's alarm call means "eagle, now"; a honeybee's dance points to flowers that exist today. Something new happens when people start to store meaning in lasting objects, marks and ornaments that mean something to others and outlast the moment.

In 2002 Christopher Henshilwood's team reported finds from Blombos Cave, on the southern coast of South Africa, about 77,000 years old [10]. They included two pieces of red ochre. Someone had rubbed a face of each piece flat and then scratched a pattern of crossing diagonal lines into it, framed by two parallel lines: a deliberate cross-hatched design [10].

Looking at the grooves under a microscope, Francesco d'Errico showed they were not accidental scratches from scraping off pigment. They had been cut on purpose with a pointed stone, one stroke after another [10, 12]. In 2018 the same cave produced something even more striking: a small stone flake with a cross-hatch of nine lines drawn on it in ochre "crayon", about 73,000 years old, the oldest known drawing [16].

From the same period come the first beads: small sea-snail shells, deliberately pierced and worn on strings (the holes are worn smooth where a cord rubbed), sometimes coloured with ochre. They have been found at Blombos (about 75,000 years old) and at Taforalt in Morocco (about 82,000 years old). A bead doesn't help you survive. Its value is in what it says, perhaps who you are, which group you belong to, or your standing in it. At this point knowledge no longer lived only inside one person's head. It was stored in objects, where it could outlive the person who made them.

9. Counting the Moon: Notched Bones

For hunter-gatherers, the sky was a calendar. Fish runs, animal migrations and the ripening of nuts all follow the seasons. Near the tropics, though, day length hardly changes through the year, so the most obvious clock in the sky is the Moon.

The Moon is easy to follow. Every 29.5 days or so it goes through its full set of phases, from a thin crescent to full and back to dark. Astronomers call this the synodic month.

The oldest possible example of counting is the Lebombo bone, found in the 1970s in Border Cave in the Lebombo Mountains, on the border of Eswatini and South Africa [12, 13]. It is about 44,000 years old: a broken piece of baboon leg bone with 29 notches cut across it. Under the microscope the notches turn out to have been made with different stone tools, which suggests they were added over time rather than all at once [12]. Twenty-nine is close to the number of days in a lunar month, so some have suggested it was a Moon count. But the bone is broken, so we don't know how many notches it once had, and the idea remains a guess.

About 20,000 years ago, on the shore of Lake Edward in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, someone made the Ishango bone [13]. It is another baboon bone, with a small piece of quartz fixed in one end, and three columns of notches. One column has groups of 11, 13, 17 and 19 (the prime numbers between 10 and 20, adding up to 60). Another has 11, 21, 19 and 9 (also adding up to 60). The middle one has 3, 6, 4, 8, 10, 5, 5 and 7 (adding up to 48) [13]. Scholars still argue about what it means: a calendar, a simple tally, or just grip marks on a tool handle. Either way, the deliberate grouping shows people keeping track of numbers outside their heads.

How Do We Know How Old Things Are?

When archaeologists say a stone tool is 3.3 million years old or a carving is 77,000 years old, how do they know? Rocks don't come with dates on them. The answer is natural "clocks" inside the materials themselves [14].

1. Radiocarbon: good for the last 50,000 years

High in the atmosphere, cosmic rays turn a little nitrogen into a radioactive form of carbon, carbon-14. Plants take it in as they grow, and animals take it in by eating plants, so every living thing carries about one carbon-14 atom for every million million ordinary carbon atoms. When a plant or animal dies, it stops taking in new carbon, and the carbon-14 it holds slowly decays. Every 5,730 years, half of what is left disappears [15]: $$N(t) = N_0 \, 2^{-t / 5730}$$ Here $N_0$ is the amount at death and $N(t)$ the amount left after $t$ years. Measure how much carbon-14 is left and you know how long ago it died. The catch: after about 50,000 years (nine halvings) only about 0.2% is left, so little that a trace of modern contamination swamps it. Radiocarbon is no use for the older parts of this story.

2. Potassium–argon: dating volcanic ash

For older sites such as Lomekwi (3.3 million years) or Olduvai (1.9 million years), geologists date the layers of volcanic ash above and below the finds. Some crystals in volcanic rock contain a radioactive form of potassium that slowly turns into argon gas, with a half-life of 1.25 billion years. When a volcano erupts, the heat drives out any argon already there, setting the clock to zero. After that, new argon builds up trapped inside the crystals. Measure the argon (labs use a refined version called the argon–argon method) and you can date the eruption to within about half a percent [1, 14].

3. Luminescence: when did this sand last see daylight?

Some sites, such as Blombos Cave or Jebel Irhoud, have no volcanic ash. There, scientists use luminescence dating [8, 14]. Grains of quartz and feldspar in the ground are exposed to a weak, steady natural radiation from the soil. This knocks electrons loose, and some get stuck in tiny flaws in the crystal. While the grain stays buried in the dark, trapped electrons build up at a steady rate, like a rain gauge filling.

Sunlight or strong heat empties the traps and sets the clock back to zero. So in the lab, scientists shine light on a buried sample (or heat it) and measure the faint glow as the trapped electrons escape. The brighter the glow, the longer it has been buried. Age = total dose received ÷ dose per year. For sand this is called optically stimulated luminescence (OSL); for burned flint it is thermoluminescence (TL), and it tells you how long ago the flint was last in a fire [8, 14].

Legend and Record: Was There a "Big Bang" of the Mind?

The popular story: For much of the late 20th century, many anthropologists believed modern human behaviour switched on suddenly, in Europe, about 40,000–50,000 years ago. In this story a single genetic change gave one group of people full language, cave art such as Chauvet and Lascaux, music and religion all at once, and they quickly pushed out the Neanderthals.

What the evidence shows: In a famous 2000 paper titled "The Revolution That Wasn't", Sally McBrearty and Alison Brooks challenged this Europe-centred view [9]. Looking at the African record of the previous 300,000 years, they showed that the signs of "modern" behaviour turned up bit by bit, long before 50,000 years ago. Finds since then have backed them up:

  • Small, finely made blades, made from stone that had been heat-treated to knap better, at Pinnacle Point, South Africa, by about 71,000 years ago [17].
  • Collecting and grinding pigment in Kenya, at Kapthurin by about 285,000 years ago [9] and at Olorgesailie by about 300,000 years ago [19].
  • Eating seafood: shellfish collected at Pinnacle Point by about 164,000 years ago [18].
  • Barbed bone harpoons at Katanda, Democratic Republic of the Congo, about 90,000 years ago (a date still debated) [9].
  • Trading over long distances: obsidian carried tens to hundreds of kilometres in East Africa [9, 19].

There was no sudden switch 50,000 years ago. The modern mind was built up over hundreds of thousands of years, as skills were passed on, populations rose and fell, and climates changed across Africa [9].

Philosophy Corner: What Counts as Knowing?

Prehistory raises a basic question: where is the line between clever animal behaviour and human knowledge?

New Caledonian crows cut leaves into hooks to fish out grubs. Sea otters crack shellfish on stones balanced on their chests. Termites build mounds with built-in air conditioning. So why don't we say crows, otters or termites do science?

The philosopher Karl Popper offered one answer. An animal's "knowledge" of the world is mostly built into its genes, and when that knowledge is wrong, the animal dies. Humans found a way round this: we let our ideas die instead of us. By putting ideas into tools, words and marks, people created a shared store of knowledge outside any one head, which Popper called World 3. And it adds up over time. A young toolmaker doesn't have to rediscover how flint breaks. They learn what earlier knappers worked out, improve it a little by trial and error, and pass it on.

Prehistory also shows how hard it is to be sure. Most of the evidence has rotted away: wood, string, skins, and above all speech. The same few surviving objects can fit several different stories. Philosophers call this underdetermination: the evidence doesn't settle which explanation is right. When we find a baboon bone with 29 notches, how could we tell a Moon calendar from a count of kills, or from idle scratches? Good archaeology tries to test such ideas rather than just tell a nice story. For example, it looks inside each notch under a microscope to see whether different tools made the marks at different times [12]. Knowledge of the past grows the same way as knowledge of physics: by finding tests that could prove our favourite ideas wrong.

13. Where It Happened

Africa's climate swung back and forth over this long period, and that shaped where people could live and travel. About 125,000 years ago, small changes in Earth's orbit brought stronger summer monsoons. The Sahara turned green, with rivers and huge lakes (such as "Lake Mega-Chad"), and people could move between North Africa, the Middle East and the south of the continent. In colder, drier times the desert spread again and cut those routes [8, 9].

15. What They Passed On

Farming began only about 12,000 years ago. By then, without schools or writing, our hunter-gatherer ancestors had already worked out a great deal about the world:

  • How stone breaks: enough to make edges that, in obsidian, are only a few millionths of a millimetre thick.
  • How to use heat: cooking food to get more out of it, and warming ingredients to make tough glues.
  • How to plan a shape: holding a symmetrical design in mind and working a raw stone towards it.
  • How to keep count: following the Moon and the seasons, and recording numbers as marks on bone.

When people later settled in the great river valleys of the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, and the Indus and built the first cities, they did not start from nothing. They inherited minds that had spent three million years testing how the world works.
