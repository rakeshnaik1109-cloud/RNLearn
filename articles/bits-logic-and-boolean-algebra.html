Bits, Logic and Boolean Algebra: How Computers Think in Ones and Zeros

Every computer is built from switches that are either on or off. The logic that combines them was worked out on paper long before the first computer, and it is where our journey from switch to supercomputer begins.

1. From Switches to Supercomputers

A smartphone contains more than ten billion tiny switches. Each one is either on or off, and each flips billions of times a second. Everything the phone does, from showing a photo to running a chatbot, is built from that single idea: a switch that can control other switches. This series follows the idea all the way. We start with the logic of switches, go down into the physics of what a switch made of silicon actually is, and build back up through chips and processors to the graphics processors (GPUs) that train today’s artificial intelligence. 3. Why Binary?

Computers could in principle work in decimal, and some early machines did. But electronic signals are never perfectly clean: voltages wander, wires pick up interference, components age. If a wire must distinguish ten levels between 0 and 1 volt, the levels are only 0.1 V apart and a little noise causes errors. If it only has to tell “low” from “high”, a large amount of noise can be tolerated. Two states are the most reliable choice, and two states are exactly what a switch provides. One two-state digit is a bit, a word introduced in Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper on information theory, where he credited it to John Tukey. [3]

Binary numbers use the same place-value idea as decimal, but each place is worth twice the one to its right instead of ten times. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz described binary arithmetic in 1703, long before computers. [4] Eight bits make a byte, which can hold 28 = 256 different values. Long binary numbers are hard to read, so programmers write them in hexadecimal (base 16), where each hex digit (0–9, A–F) stands for exactly four bits.

4. Logic Gates

A logic gate takes one or more bits in and produces a bit out according to a fixed rule, written as a truth table listing the output for every combination of inputs. The basic gates are AND (1 only if all inputs are 1), OR (1 if any input is 1) and NOT (the opposite of its input), plus their combinations NAND (“not AND”), NOR (“not OR”) and XOR (“exclusive OR”, 1 when the inputs differ).

The link between logic and electricity is old. In 1886 the American philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce noted in a letter that logical operations could be carried out by electrical switching circuits. [5] In 1938 Claude Shannon, then a master’s student at MIT, showed systematically that the algebra of logic describes circuits of relays and switches, and can be used to design and simplify them: switches in series behave like AND, switches in parallel like OR. [6] That paper is often called the most important master’s thesis of the twentieth century.

5. Boolean Algebra

In 1854 George Boole showed that logical reasoning can be written as algebra, with variables that take only two values. [7] In the notation engineers use, A·B (or AB) means A AND B, A + B means A OR B, and A̅ (A with a bar) means NOT A. Many rules look like ordinary algebra (A·(B + C) = A·B + A·C), and some do not: A + A = A, A·A̅ = 0, and A + A̅ = 1.

The most useful rules are De Morgan’s laws, named after Augustus De Morgan, who stated them in his 1847 Formal Logic: [8]

$$\overline{A\cdot B} = \overline{A} + \overline{B}, \qquad \overline{A + B} = \overline{A}\cdot\overline{B}.$$

In words: “not (A and B)” is the same as “not A or not B”. They let you push a NOT through a bracket by swapping AND and OR, and they are the key to building every gate from NANDs.

Watch out: + is not addition

In Boolean algebra, 1 + 1 = 1, because “true OR true” is true. Binary arithmetic, where 1 + 1 = 10 (two), is a different operation, built in Part 2 from XOR and AND gates. Keep the two meanings of “+” apart.

6. One Gate to Rule Them All: NAND

In 1913 the logician Henry Sheffer showed that all of Boolean algebra can be built from a single operation, now called NAND (the “Sheffer stroke”). [9] NAND is universal: connecting both inputs of a NAND gives NOT; a NAND followed by a NOT gives AND; and by De Morgan’s law, OR is a NAND of the two inverted inputs. NOR is universal too. This matters in practice, because a chip factory can build everything from one well-optimised cell, and, as Part 9 shows, NAND is especially cheap to make from transistors.

7. From Truth Table to Circuit

Any truth table can be turned into a circuit by a recipe called sum of products: for every row whose output is 1, write an AND of the inputs (inverting those that are 0 in that row), then OR all these terms together. For example, the majority function of three inputs, which outputs 1 when at least two inputs are 1, has 1s in rows 011, 101, 110 and 111:

$$M = \overline{A}BC + A\overline{B}C + AB\overline{C} + ABC = AB + BC + AC.$$

The simplified form on the right uses 3 two-input ANDs and one three-input OR instead of four three-input ANDs and inverters. Simplifying circuits saves area, power and time, and Part 2 introduces a systematic way to do it, the Karnaugh map.

In code: generating any truth table in Python 
from itertools import product

def majority(a, b, c):
    return (a and b) or (b and c) or (a and c)

for a, b, c in product([0, 1], repeat=3):
    print(a, b, c, int(majority(a, b, c)))

# Check De Morgan's law for every input
assert all((not (a and b)) == ((not a) or (not b))
           for a, b in product([False, True], repeat=2))

8. Real Gates: Voltages, Not Numbers

Inside a chip there are no 1s and 0s, only voltages. A circuit family defines a voltage range read as “low” (0) and a range read as “high” (1), with a forbidden band between them. Each gate restores weak signals to clean levels, so noise does not accumulate as signals pass through thousands of gates; that restoring action, explained in Part 9, is the deep reason digital electronics is so reliable. Gates are also not instantaneous: each takes a short propagation delay to respond, today a few picoseconds to tens of picoseconds per gate, which ultimately limits how fast a processor can run (Part 12).

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): convert 156 to binary and hexadecimal
Solution

Repeatedly divide by 2 and read the remainders upwards: 156 → 78 r0 → 39 r0 → 19 r1 → 9 r1 → 4 r1 → 2 r0 → 1 r0 → 0 r1. Reading the remainders from last to first gives 10011100. Check: 128 + 16 + 8 + 4 = 156. Grouping into fours, 1001 1100 = 9C in hexadecimal.

Problem 2 (easy): simplify A·B + A·B̅
Solution

Factor out A: A·(B + B̅) = A·1 = A. The output does not depend on B at all, so the circuit of two ANDs, an OR and an inverter can be replaced by a plain wire.

Problem 3 (medium): negate (A + B)·C̅
Solution

Apply De Morgan to the AND first: NOT[(A + B)·C̅] = NOT(A + B) + NOT(C̅) = (A̅·B̅) + C. So the answer is A̅B̅ + C. Check one case: A = 0, B = 0, C = 0 gives the original (0)·1 = 0, so its negation should be 1; the answer gives 1·1 + 0 = 1 ✓.

Problem 4 (medium): design a three-way light switch

A corridor light is controlled by three switches A, B, C. Flipping any one switch must change the light’s state. Find a logic expression.

Solution

Flipping one input must flip the output, so the output depends on whether an odd number of switches are up: Y = A ⊕ B ⊕ C (XOR of all three). The truth table has 1s for 001, 010, 100 and 111. This “parity” function is also used to detect single-bit errors in transmitted data.

Problem 5 (harder): how many NAND gates for XOR?
Solution

Let N = NAND(A, B). Then XOR = NAND(NAND(A, N), NAND(B, N)): 4 NAND gates. Check A = 1, B = 0: N = 1, NAND(1, 1) = 0, NAND(0, 1) = 1, NAND(0, 1) = 1 ✓. Using the textbook expression A·B̅ + A̅·B directly would need 2 NOTs, 2 ANDs and an OR, or 5 NANDs after conversion, so clever factoring saves a gate.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: what is the minimum energy of a logic operation?

In 1961 Rolf Landauer argued that erasing one bit of information must release at least kBT ln 2 of heat, about 3 × 10−21 J at room temperature, because erasure reduces the number of possible states. [10] In 2012 Antoine Bérut and colleagues measured this limit with a single microscopic bead in a laser trap. [11] Today’s logic gates use somewhere between ten thousand and a million times more energy per operation than this limit, depending on how it is counted. Gates that never erase information, called reversible logic, could in principle go below it; whether practical reversible computers can be built is still open.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “binary was invented for computers”

The myth: the binary system was invented by computer engineers in the twentieth century.

The reality: Leibniz published binary arithmetic in 1703, and he noted its resemblance to the hexagrams of the ancient Chinese I Ching. [4] Boole’s algebra of logic dates from 1854. [7] What was new in the twentieth century was the realisation, by Shannon and others, that this old mathematics was exactly what was needed to design machines built from switches. [6]

Why it matters: many technologies rest on mathematics developed long before anyone knew it would be useful.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Computers use binary because two well-separated voltage levels are robust against noise.
  • Binary is place value with powers of 2; a byte (8 bits) holds 256 values; hexadecimal writes 4 bits per digit.
  • Logic gates compute outputs from inputs according to truth tables: AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR.
  • Boolean algebra simplifies logic; De Morgan’s laws swap AND and OR under a NOT.
  • NAND (and NOR) alone can build any logic circuit.
  • Any truth table becomes a circuit by sum of products; simplification saves gates, power and time.
