At a glance

You need: Parts 1 and 2 (loss, regularisation, linear classifiers). The fold-outs use inner products and a little optimisation.

1. The Widest Street

If two groups of points can be separated by a straight line, there are usually infinitely many such lines. Which is best? A line that grazes some points may misclassify the next point that lands nearby. Intuitively, the safest choice runs down the middle of the widest empty “street” between the classes. That simple idea, the maximum margin, combined with a mathematical trick for drawing curved boundaries, produced the support vector machine (SVM), which in the late 1990s and 2000s was the leading general-purpose classifier. [1, 2]

This part completes the classical half of the series. Kernels connect it to what follows: they are another way, besides neural networks, of letting a linear method learn nonlinear patterns, and the theory linking the two is an active area of research.

3. The Maximum-Margin Classifier

A linear classifier predicts with the sign of w⊤x + b (Part 2). The margin is the distance from the boundary to the nearest training points. The maximum-margin classifier chooses w and b to make it as large as possible while classifying every training point correctly. Vladimir Vapnik and Alexander Lerner described such an “optimal hyperplane” in 1963. [3]

The solution has a remarkable property. It depends only on the few points that lie exactly on the edges of the street, the support vectors. Every other point could be moved, or deleted, without changing the boundary at all. A model defined by a handful of critical examples is compact, and the number of support vectors gives a rough indication of how complex the boundary is.

The maths: maximising the margin

With labels yi = ±1, scale w and b so that the closest points satisfy yi(w⊤xi + b) = 1. The margin width is then 2/‖w‖, so maximising it means minimising ‖w‖:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1 \ \text{for all } i.$$

This is a convex quadratic programme: it has a single best solution and no local minima to get stuck in, unlike the neural networks of later parts.

4. The Soft Margin and the Hinge Loss

Real data are rarely perfectly separable, and a single outlier can drag a hard margin badly out of place. In 1995 Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik introduced the soft margin: points may sit inside the street, or even on the wrong side, at a cost. [1] A penalty parameter C sets the price of each violation. Small C buys a wide street that tolerates many violations; large C narrows the street to violate as little as possible, which risks overfitting.

On the two clouds, C = 0.01 gives a street 2.7 units wide in which 104 of the 140 training points are support vectors; C = 1 narrows it to 0.76 with 24 support vectors; C = 100 to 0.44 with 15. Test accuracy barely changes (88–92%), because the boundary itself moves little.

The maths: the soft margin is regularised hinge loss

The soft-margin SVM minimises

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b)\big).$$

The second term is the hinge loss: zero for points correctly classified beyond the margin, growing linearly for violations. So an SVM is a linear classifier, like logistic regression, with a different loss (hinge rather than cross-entropy) and an L2 penalty, with C playing the role of 1/λ in Part 2. Unlike cross-entropy, the hinge loss is exactly zero for confident correct predictions, which is why those points drop out of the solution.

5. Solving It: the Dual Problem and SMO

SVMs are usually solved in a rearranged form, the dual problem, in which each training point gets a weight αi between 0 and C. Points beyond the margin get αi = 0; support vectors get αi > 0. The prediction becomes a weighted sum over support vectors:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i\in\text{SV}} \alpha_i\,y_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x} + b.$$

Notice that the training points enter only through inner products xi⊤x. That is the door to the kernel trick. For training, John Platt’s sequential minimal optimisation (SMO, 1998) solves the dual by repeatedly optimising just two α’s at a time, each step with a simple closed-form solution. [4] Refined versions power the LIBSVM library behind scikit-learn’s SVC, and the figures on this page use the same method.

6. The Kernel Trick

Part 2 showed that a linear model can draw curves if we add features such as x². The same works for SVMs: map each point into a richer feature space, then find a maximum-margin linear boundary there. Figure 4 shows the idea in one dimension. The two classes cannot be split by one threshold on the line, but after adding x² as a second coordinate, a straight line separates them.

The trouble is that rich feature spaces are huge. All products of 100 features up to degree 3 number over 176,000. The kernel trick sidesteps this. Because the SVM needs only inner products between points, we can replace xi⊤x by a kernel function K(xi, x) that computes the inner product in the feature space directly from the original features, without ever constructing the features. Mark Aizerman, Emmanuel Braverman and Lev Rozonoer used this interpretation of kernels in 1964; [5] in 1992 Bernhard Boser, Isabelle Guyon and Vapnik combined it with maximum margins to create the modern SVM. [6]

The maths: a kernel in action

For two-dimensional points a and b, the kernel K(a, b) = (a⊤b)² expands as

$$(a_1b_1 + a_2b_2)^2 = a_1^2b_1^2 + 2a_1a_2b_1b_2 + a_2^2b_2^2 = \phi(\mathbf{a})^\top\phi(\mathbf{b}),$$

with the feature map φ(x) = (x1², √2 x1x2, x2²). Computing (a⊤b)² takes three multiplications; it gives exactly the inner product in the three-dimensional space of products. For the RBF kernel, the corresponding feature space is infinite-dimensional, yet the kernel is a single exponential.

7. Kernels and How to Tune Them

Not every function is a valid kernel. It must correspond to an inner product in some feature space, a condition characterised by James Mercer’s theorem of 1909 and the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces developed by Nachman Aronszajn in 1950. [7, 8] The common choices are:

  • Linear: K(a, b) = a⊤b. The plain SVM; best for very many features, such as text.
  • Polynomial: K(a, b) = (γ a⊤b + 1)d. All products of features up to degree d.
  • RBF (radial basis function, or Gaussian): K(a, b) = exp(−γ‖a − b‖²). Similarity that decays with distance; the default choice for nonlinear problems.

With C = 1, an RBF kernel with γ = 1 scores 95% on the moons and 98% on the circles, where the linear kernel manages 88% and 53%. Raising γ to 100 makes each support vector’s influence tiny: training accuracy approaches 100% but test accuracy falls to 83% on the moons, a classic case of overfitting with nearly every training point (131 of 140) a support vector. C and γ interact, so they are tuned together, usually by a grid search with cross-validation on logarithmic scales (Part 10).

Watch out: scale features for kernel methods

RBF and polynomial kernels depend on distances and inner products, so features on different scales distort them, as with k-NN in Part 3. Standardise features, inside the cross-validation folds.

8. What an SVM Costs

Kernel methods compare every pair of training points: the kernel matrix has n² entries, and training typically takes between n² and n³ operations. For a few thousand examples that is fast; for millions it is impractical without approximations. Prediction costs one kernel evaluation per support vector. SVMs also output scores rather than probabilities; Platt scaling, fitting a logistic curve to the scores on held-out data, converts them into calibrated probabilities. [9] For more than two classes, SVMs combine several binary classifiers, one for each pair of classes (scikit-learn’s default) or one for each class against the rest.

9. Support Vector Regression

The margin idea also works for numbers. Support vector regression (SVR), introduced by Harris Drucker and colleagues in 1997, fits a function surrounded by a tube of half-width ε. [10] Errors smaller than ε cost nothing; larger errors cost in proportion to how far they stick out. Points inside the tube have no influence; only those on or outside it become support vectors.

With ε = 0.2, C = 10 and γ = 10, the SVR uses 7 of the 15 training points as support vectors and reaches a test error of 0.062, below the cubic polynomial of Part 1 (0.075) and close to the noise floor of 0.0625. Narrowing the tube to ε = 0.1 uses 9 support vectors for a similar test error (0.061); widening it to 0.5 leaves only 4 and the fit becomes too crude (0.18).

10. Kernels Beyond the SVM

Any method that can be written in terms of inner products can be “kernelised”. Kernel ridge regression is the ridge regression of Part 2 with a kernel. Kernel PCA, introduced by Bernhard Schölkopf, Alexander Smola and Klaus-Robert Müller in 1998, finds nonlinear directions of variation in data. [11] Kernels have been designed for strings, graphs, molecules and images, letting the same algorithms compare DNA sequences or chemical structures directly. Choosing a kernel is a way of building in knowledge about what makes two examples similar: an inductive bias, in the language of Part 1.

11. Gaussian Processes: Kernels with Uncertainty

A Gaussian process (GP) treats the unknown function itself as random, with the kernel describing how strongly the values at two points are correlated. Conditioning on the training data gives, for any new x, not just a prediction but a full normal distribution: a mean and an uncertainty. Gaussian processes have long been used in geostatistics, where the method is known as kriging, and Carl Rasmussen and Christopher Williams’ 2006 book made them a standard machine-learning tool. [12]

Fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood of the training data, the GP chooses a length-scale of about 0.25 and a noise level of about 0.18, close to the true 0.25. Its test error is 0.059, the best of any method in the series so far, and its 95% band contains 56 of the 60 test points (93%). Most strikingly, the band balloons in the gap between x = 0.13 and 0.37 where there are no training points: the model is honest about what it does not know. Gaussian processes are the engine of Bayesian optimisation, used to tune hyperparameters and design experiments with few trials.

In code: SVMs, SVR and Gaussian processes in scikit-learn

12. When to Use Kernel Methods

  • Good fit: small to medium data sets (hundreds to tens of thousands of examples), many features relative to examples, well-defined similarity measures, and problems where a smooth boundary is expected. A linear SVM remains excellent for text classification.
  • Gaussian processes when uncertainty matters and data are scarce: calibration curves, surrogate models of expensive simulations, Bayesian optimisation.
  • Poor fit: very large data sets (training cost), raw images, audio or text where deep networks learn better features, and tables with mixed and categorical features, where tree ensembles (Part 4) usually win.

A broad 2014 comparison of 179 classifiers on 121 data sets found random forests and SVMs with Gaussian kernels among the most accurate families overall. [13]

13. Open Questions

Open questions: are neural networks secretly kernel machines?

Two lines of research connect kernels to deep learning. In 2007 Ali Rahimi and Benjamin Recht showed that kernels can be approximated by a layer of random features followed by a linear model, which looks very like a neural network whose first layer is never trained. [14] In 2018 Arthur Jacot, Franck Gabriel and Clément Hongler proved that in the limit of infinitely wide layers, a neural network trained by gradient descent behaves exactly like a kernel method with a particular kernel, the neural tangent kernel. [15] Yet real networks of finite width seem to learn their features in ways no fixed kernel can, which may be the source of their power. How much of deep learning kernel theory can explain is one of the central questions of modern machine-learning theory.

14. Worked Example: Kernels on the Running Data

  1. Classification. RBF SVM with C = 1, γ = 1: test accuracy 90% on the two clouds, 95% on the moons and 98% on the circles, using 36, 40 and 43 support vectors. The polynomial kernel of degree 3 does almost as well (85%, 93%, 98%); the linear kernel fails on the circles (53%).
  2. Overfitting check. With γ = 100, training accuracy rises to 99–100% and test accuracy drops (75% on the clouds, 83% on the moons), with almost every point a support vector: a warning sign worth watching for.
  3. Regression. SVR (ε = 0.2, C = 10, γ = 10): test error 0.062. Gaussian process with hyperparameters fitted by maximum likelihood on the training data: 0.059. Both beat every earlier method on the running example (best so far: the cubic, 0.075), and the GP also says how sure it is.
  4. Caveat. The SVM and SVR settings here were picked by hand for illustration; in practice choose C, γ and ε by cross-validation on the training data only (Parts 1 and 10). The GP’s hyperparameters were fitted to the training data alone, which is legitimate.

16. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “deep learning made SVMs obsolete”

The myth: since neural networks took over image recognition in 2012, support vector machines are a museum piece.

The reality: deep learning did replace SVMs where raw, high-dimensional data and huge labelled sets are available, such as images, speech and language. But on smaller, structured problems, kernel methods remain strong and are often easier to train: the 2014 comparison of 179 classifiers ranked Gaussian-kernel SVMs among the best families. [13] Their ideas live on everywhere: the hinge loss in modern classifiers, margins in theories of generalisation, kernels in Gaussian processes and Bayesian optimisation, and the neural tangent kernel in the theory of deep learning itself. [15]

Why it matters: fashions in machine learning change faster than the problems. The right tool depends on the size and shape of the data, not on what is newest.

17. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • An SVM chooses the boundary with the widest margin; only the support vectors determine it.
  • The soft margin trades margin width against violations through C; it is an L2-regularised hinge loss.
  • The kernel trick replaces inner products by kernel functions, giving nonlinear boundaries without computing new features.
  • The RBF kernel’s γ controls the reach of each support vector; tune C and γ together by cross-validation, after scaling features.
  • SVR ignores errors inside an ε-tube; Gaussian processes give predictions with honest uncertainty.
  • Kernel methods excel on small and medium data; their cost grows quickly with the number of examples.
