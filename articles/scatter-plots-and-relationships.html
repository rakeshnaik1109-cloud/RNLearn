Scatter Plots and Relationships: Correlation, Trend Lines and Hidden Groups

A perfect curve with zero correlation, a treatment that looks harmful until you split the data, and a cluster hidden among 40,000 points. How to read the plots that relate one variable to another.

1. Two Variables at Once

Does more study time go with higher exam scores? Do heavier cars use more fuel? Does a drug’s effect grow with its dose? Questions about how two quantities relate are answered first by a scatter plot: one dot per case, placed by its value on each variable. It is perhaps the most useful plot in science, and also the one most often over-interpreted.

This part covers reading scatter plots and the correlation coefficient, adding trend lines, the trap of lurking variables, what to do when there are too many points, bubble charts that add a third variable, and scatter-plot matrices for many variables.

3. The Scatter Plot

By convention, the explanatory variable (the one we think may influence the other, or that we control) goes on the horizontal axis, and the response on the vertical axis. Each dot is one case: one student, one car, one patient.

How to read a scatter plot
  1. Direction: does y tend to rise or fall as x increases?
  2. Form: is the pattern straight, curved, or made of clusters?
  3. Strength: how closely do the points follow the form?
  4. Exceptions: outliers, subgroups, or a spread that changes along the axis.
  5. Explanation: what else could link the two variables?
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
hours = rng.uniform(0, 10, 80)                               # synthetic: hours of study
score = 45 + 4.5 * hours + rng.normal(0, 7, 80)              # exam score

r = np.corrcoef(hours, score)[0, 1]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4.5))
ax.scatter(hours, score, s=25, alpha=0.8, color="#0072B2", edgecolor="white", linewidth=0.5)
ax.set_xlabel("Hours of study per week")                     # explanatory variable on x
ax.set_ylabel("Exam score")                                  # response variable on y
ax.set_title(f"Study time and exam score (r = {r:.2f})")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. Reading Correlation

The correlation coefficient r, given its modern form by Karl Pearson in 1896 after Francis Galton’s work, measures how closely points follow a straight line, from −1 to +1. [1] It has no units, does not change if you swap the axes or rescale them, and is easy to misjudge by eye.

Remember three facts. First, r measures only linear association: a perfect curve can have r near zero, as Anscombe’s quartet showed (Part 1). [2] Second, a single outlier can drag r a long way. Third, r² is the fraction of the variance in y accounted for by a straight line in x, so r = 0.5 accounts for only a quarter.

5. Trend Lines, Straight and Smooth

A trend line summarises the relationship. The most common is the least-squares straight line, which minimises the sum of squared vertical distances from the points. When the relationship may be curved, a smoother lets the data choose the shape. The best known is LOWESS (locally weighted scatter-plot smoothing), published by William Cleveland in 1979: around each point it fits a straight line to the nearby points, giving closer points more weight, and joins the results. [3]

# pip install numpy matplotlib statsmodels
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.nonparametric.smoothers_lowess import lowess

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
dose = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, 120))
effect = 10 * dose / (2 + dose) + rng.normal(0, 0.9, dose.size)   # saturating curve (synthetic)

slope, intercept = np.polyfit(dose, effect, 1)                   # straight line (least squares)
smooth = lowess(effect, dose, frac=0.4)                         # LOWESS: local weighted fits

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
ax.scatter(dose, effect, s=15, color="gray", alpha=0.7)
ax.plot(dose, intercept + slope * dose, color="#D55E00", label="straight line")
ax.plot(smooth[:, 0], smooth[:, 1], color="#0072B2", lw=2.5, label="LOWESS (frac = 0.4)")
ax.set_xlabel("Dose")
ax.set_ylabel("Effect")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Seaborn’s regplot and lmplot add a fitted line with a shaded confidence band in one call; with lowess=True they draw a smoother instead.

6. Lurking Variables and Simpson’s Paradox

A scatter plot shows association, not cause. Two variables can move together because one causes the other, because the cause runs the other way, by coincidence, or because a third variable drives both. In 1951 Edward Simpson described an extreme case, now called Simpson’s paradox: a relationship in combined data can reverse within every subgroup. [4]

The lesson for reading scatter plots: whenever you see a relationship, ask what grouping variable might be hiding inside it, and colour or facet by it if you can.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
xs, ys, gs = [], [], []
for g, (x0, y0) in enumerate([(2, 9), (5, 6), (8, 3)]):        # three groups (synthetic)
    x = x0 + rng.normal(0, 0.8, 40)
    y = y0 + 0.8 * (x - x0) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, 40)            # positive trend WITHIN each group
    xs.append(x); ys.append(y); gs += [g] * 40
x, y, g = np.concatenate(xs), np.concatenate(ys), np.array(gs)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4), sharey=True)
ax1.scatter(x, y, color="gray", s=15)
b, a = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
ax1.plot([0, 10], [a, a + 10 * b], color="black")
ax1.set_title(f"All together: slope {b:.2f}")
colours = ["#0072B2", "#E69F00", "#009E73"]
for k in range(3):
    m = g == k
    bk, ak = np.polyfit(x[m], y[m], 1)
    ax2.scatter(x[m], y[m], color=colours[k], s=15, label=f"group {k + 1}")
    ax2.plot([x[m].min(), x[m].max()], [ak + bk * x[m].min(), ak + bk * x[m].max()], color=colours[k])
ax2.set_title("By group: every slope is positive")
ax2.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Too Many Points

With thousands of points, markers pile on top of one another, a problem called overplotting. Dense and sparse regions look the same, and small clusters vanish. Remedies include smaller, semi-transparent markers, and hexagonal binning, proposed by Daniel Carr and colleagues in 1987, which counts points in a grid of hexagons and colours each by its count. [5]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 50_000
x = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
y = 0.6 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)
x[:3000] = rng.normal(-1.8, 0.25, 3000)                      # a small second cluster,
y[:3000] = rng.normal(1.5, 0.25, 3000)                       # hidden inside the blob

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 4), sharex=True, sharey=True)
axes[0].scatter(x, y, s=4)
axes[0].set_title("Plain scatter: a solid blob")
axes[1].scatter(x, y, s=2, alpha=0.05)                       # transparency reveals density
axes[1].set_title("Small, transparent points")
hb = axes[2].hexbin(x, y, gridsize=45, cmap="viridis", mincnt=1, bins="log")
fig.colorbar(hb, ax=axes[2], label="points per hexagon (log)")
axes[2].set_title("Hexagonal binning")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Bubble Charts: A Third Variable

A scatter plot can carry a third variable by colour (for categories) or by the size of the marker, making a bubble chart. Bubble charts of countries’ income, life expectancy and population became famous through Hans Rosling’s animated presentations. Size is the least accurate of these encodings (Part 1), and it must be scaled by area, not radius.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
n = 25
income = rng.lognormal(9.3, 0.9, n)                          # synthetic countries
life = 52 + 6 * np.log(income / 1000) + rng.normal(0, 2.5, n)
population = rng.lognormal(16.5, 1.2, n)
region = rng.integers(0, 4, n)
colours = np.array(["#0072B2", "#E69F00", "#009E73", "#CC79A7"])[region]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
# matplotlib's s is the marker AREA, so area is proportional to population (correct)
ax.scatter(income, life, s=population / population.max() * 1500, c=colours,
           alpha=0.6, edgecolor="white")
ax.set_xscale("log")
ax.set_xlabel("Income per person (log scale)")
ax.set_ylabel("Life expectancy (years)")
ax.set_title("Bubble chart: size = population, colour = region")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. Scatter-Plot Matrices

With several variables, a scatter-plot matrix shows every pair in a grid, with each variable’s distribution on the diagonal. Popularised by John Chambers, William Cleveland, Beat Kleiner and Paul Tukey in 1983, [6] it is one of the first plots to make with a new data set. Seaborn’s pairplot draws one in a single line; here it is with Ronald Fisher’s classic iris measurements, which come bundled with scikit-learn. [7]

# pip install seaborn scikit-learn
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns
from sklearn.datasets import load_iris

iris = load_iris(as_frame=True)                    # Fisher's classic iris measurements (bundled, no download)
df = iris.frame.rename(columns=lambda c: c.replace(" (cm)", ""))
df["species"] = iris.target_names[iris.target]
df = df.drop(columns="target")

g = sns.pairplot(df, hue="species", corner=True,   # corner=True: lower triangle only
                 plot_kws=dict(s=15, alpha=0.7), height=1.8)
g.figure.suptitle("Scatter-plot matrix of the iris data", y=1.02)
plt.show()

Read it row by row: each panel shows the variable on its row against the variable on its column. Look for strong pairs, curved relationships and groups that separate, and remember that a pattern seen only in two dimensions may hide structure in others.

10. Common Misreadings

  • Correlation as causation. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because of summer weather, not because one causes the other.
  • Judging strength by slope. A steep line can have weak correlation, and a gentle line a strong one; slope depends on units, r does not.
  • Ignoring the range. A relationship seen over a narrow range may not hold beyond it. Do not extrapolate a trend line past the data.
  • Missing subgroups. Clusters and Simpson’s paradox can make or reverse a trend.
  • Trusting r from a few points or with outliers. One point can create a “strong” correlation.
  • Reading density from an overplotted blob. Without transparency or binning, you cannot see where most of the data are.
  • Ecological fallacy. A correlation between averages of groups (countries, schools) need not hold for individuals within them.

11. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who made the first scatter plot?

The legend: Francis Galton invented the scatter plot to study heredity.

The record: Galton made scatter plots famous, plotting the heights of adult children against the average height of their parents in 1886 and discovering what he called “regression towards mediocrity”: children of very tall parents tend to be tall, but less extreme than their parents. [8] But the historians Michael Friendly and Daniel Denis argue that the first true scatter plot was drawn by the astronomer John Herschel in 1833, who plotted the measured position angles of a double star against the dates of observation and drew a smooth curve through them by hand, to work out its orbit. [9]

A second legend: regression to the mean is a biological law. The record: it is a statistical effect that appears whenever two measurements are imperfectly correlated: students with the highest scores on one test tend to score a little lower on the next, not because they got worse, but because luck played a part in their first result.
