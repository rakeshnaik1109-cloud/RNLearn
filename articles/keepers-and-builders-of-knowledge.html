Keepers and Builders of Knowledge: Science in the Medieval Commonwealth

How medieval thinkers across Islam, India, China, and Latin Christendom built the infrastructure of modern science: from Baghdad’s translation movement and Ibn al-Haytham’s optics to Mādhava’s calculus, Song Dynasty engineering, and the disputations of Paris and Oxford.

1. There Were No "Dark Ages" for Science

A popular story still goes like this: the bright light of Greece and Rome went out when the Roman Empire fell, Europe sank into a thousand years of ignorance (the "Dark Ages"), and only woke up with the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution. In this story the Middle Ages were all superstition, religious fanaticism, belief in a flat Earth and standing still. [4]

Historians of science reject this picture. It was largely invented by Italian writers of the 1300s, such as Petrarch, who wanted to make their own time look like a rebirth, and it was later used by 18th-century writers as a weapon against the Church. The study of nature didn't stop for a thousand years.

Between about 500 and 1500 CE, science became a connected effort spread across much of the world. While western Europe broke up into small kingdoms, lively centres of learning grew in Baghdad, Cairo, Córdoba, Samarkand, Ujjain, Kaifeng, Toledo, Salerno, Paris and Oxford. The medieval period didn't break the chain of discovery; it added important links to it.

The Middle Ages also solved a problem that had held ancient science back: keeping knowledge going. In the ancient world, brilliant individuals such as Archimedes, Eratosthenes and Apollonius made great discoveries, but there were few lasting institutions to train the next generation, and knowledge could be lost when a teacher died or a library was neglected.

The medieval world built those institutions: libraries and translation projects paid for by rulers (such as the House of Wisdom in Baghdad), teaching hospitals (bīmāristān), state observatories, and above all the self-governing university. When Copernicus, Kepler and Galileo changed astronomy and physics in the 1500s and 1600s, they built on methods, star tables, mathematical tools and arguments that had been developed over five centuries of medieval study.

2. Baghdad's House of Wisdom and the Great Translation

In 762 CE the Abbasid caliph al-Manṣūr founded Baghdad on the river Tigris. Within decades it had grown into a city of perhaps half a million people, a crossroads of trade between Africa, Europe and Asia. There, the early Abbasid caliphs and their courts began what the historian Dimitri Gutas calls one of the great intellectual projects in history: translating Greek learning into Arabic. [8]

Under the caliphs Hārūn al-Rashīd (ruled 786–809) and his son al-Ma'mūn (ruled 813–833), the court had a library known as the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Ḥikma). Popular accounts describe it as a kind of research university; Gutas argues it was mainly a palace library, and that the translations were paid for by many patrons: caliphs, officials and wealthy families. Al-Ma'mūn also paid for astronomical observations in Baghdad and Damascus. Missions were sent to the Byzantine Empire to buy Greek manuscripts, and scholars gathered astronomy books from Persia and India.

This was a project of many faiths. The leading translator, Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (808–873 CE), was a Christian doctor. He and his team collected several copies of each Greek or Syriac text, compared them to fix copying mistakes, and produced reliable Arabic versions of the medical works of Galen and Hippocrates and of works by Plato and Aristotle. Thābit ibn Qurra (826–901 CE), from a star-worshipping community in Ḥarrān, translated Apollonius, Archimedes and Euclid, correcting errors in earlier versions.

Indian science arrived too. Around 773 CE, a visiting Indian astronomer brought Sanskrit astronomy books to the court, including Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta. Translated into Arabic by al-Fazārī, they introduced Arabic scholars to Indian place-value numbers, zero and the sine (jyā), all of which you met in Part 4.

The translators weren't just copying. They were working doctors and mathematicians, and they soon found mistakes and gaps in the ancient books. The philosopher al-Kindī (c. 801–873 CE), called "the philosopher of the Arabs", put the attitude well:

"We ought not to be ashamed of appreciating the truth and of acquiring it wherever it comes from, even if it comes from foreign races and nations distant from us. For the seeker of truth there is nothing of higher value than the truth itself."
— Al-Kindī, On First Philosophy (c. 830 CE)

3. Ibn al-Haytham: How Do We See?

Of all the scientists of the medieval Islamic world, few had a bigger lasting effect on physics than Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–1040 CE), known in Latin Europe as Alhazen. He worked in Cairo (a later, colourful story says he pretended to be mad to get out of a job for the caliph and was kept at home) and around 1021 CE finished his seven-volume Book of Optics (Kitāb al-Manāẓir). [1, 3]

Before him, there were two rival ideas about how we see:

  • The eyes send out rays. Greek mathematicians such as Euclid and Ptolemy thought the eye sends out invisible rays that "feel" objects, like a blind person's stick. This worked well for the geometry of perspective, but made little physical sense: if our eyes send out rays, why can't we see in the dark? And how could rays from our eyes reach the stars the instant we look up?
  • Something comes into the eyes. Greek philosophers such as the atomists and Aristotle thought something travels from the object into the eye. But they had no mathematics to explain reflection, the bending of light, or why distant things look smaller.

Ibn al-Haytham insisted on combining physics, mathematics and careful testing. In his writings he described an approach close to what we now call the scientific method. The words below are a paraphrase of passages from the Book of Optics and his Doubts about Ptolemy:

"Our duty is to begin our inquiry by an inspection of the things that exist... to assemble the particulars by induction, and to distinguish what belongs to the senses from what is added by the imagination... We should suspect our premises, avoid following authority blindly, and make argument and demonstration our guide, not the words of any man."
— paraphrased from Ibn al-Haytham, Kitāb al-Manāẓir (c. 1021 CE) and al-Shukūk ʿalā Baṭlamyūs

He showed that light travels in straight lines from bright or lit objects into the eye. To test this he described experiments with a camera obscura: a completely dark room with one tiny hole in the window shutter.

He put several lamps outside at different heights. Each lamp made its own separate spot of light on the opposite wall, upside down: the highest lamp made the lowest spot. When he covered one lamp, its spot disappeared at once while the others stayed exactly as they were. The light from different lamps passed through the same hole without mixing. He concluded that every point of a lit object sends light out in all directions, and that we see because some of that light enters our eyes. His arguments against "rays from the eyes", and his geometry of light rays, shaped European optics and were taken up by Kepler six centuries later.

4. Al-Bīrūnī Measures the Earth from a Hilltop

At the same time, in Central Asia, al-Bīrūnī (973–1048 CE) was doing remarkable work in astronomy, measuring the Earth and studying other cultures. Born in Khwārizm (in today's Uzbekistan), he spent much of his life at the court of Sultan Maḥmūd of Ghazni, in today's Afghanistan. [11]

In the 1020s, during Maḥmūd's campaigns in northern India, al-Bīrūnī was at the fort of Nandana, in the Salt Range of Punjab (in today's Pakistan). The fort sat on a steep hill overlooking the flat plain of the Indus, which stretched away to the horizon.

Two centuries earlier, the caliph al-Ma'mūn had sent teams of surveyors into the flat desert of Sinjar in Iraq to measure the Earth. They walked north and south, measuring the distance with rods, until the height of the Pole Star in the sky changed by one degree. It worked, but it was slow, hard work for large teams, and small errors added up over long distances.

At Nandana, al-Bīrūnī found a clever method that needed just one person and a hill:

  1. First he measured the height of the hill above the plain, using a sighting instrument and a measured line on the ground (he got about 650 cubits, a few hundred metres).
  2. Then he climbed to the top and set his instrument exactly level.
  3. Looking out to where the plain met the sky, he measured how far the horizon was below level. Because the Earth curves away, the horizon dips slightly below the level line. He found the dip was 34 minutes of arc, a little over half a degree.

Now picture three points: the centre of the Earth ($O$), the hilltop ($P$) and the point on the horizon where his line of sight just touches the ground ($T$). The line of sight touches the Earth's curve at $T$, so it meets the radius there at a right angle. That gives a right-angled triangle, and simple trigonometry relates the Earth's radius $R$, the hill's height $h$ and the dip angle $\alpha$:

$$\cos \alpha = \frac{R}{R + h} \quad\text{which rearranges to}\quad R = \frac{h \cos \alpha}{1 - \cos \alpha}$$

Putting in his numbers gives a radius usually converted to about 6,340 km (the exact figure depends on how long his cubit was). The modern average radius is 6,371 km. Being within about 1% owes something to luck: a dip of half a degree is very hard to measure, and the air bends light near the ground by about as much as his likely errors. The real achievement is the method: one person, one hill and some geometry.

Al-Bīrūnī was just as remarkable as a student of other cultures. For his great book on India, he learned Sanskrit, read Indian philosophy and astronomy in the original, and described Indian beliefs and customs fairly and carefully, comparing them with Greek and Islamic ideas, in a way that was very unusual for his time.

5. Ibn Sīnā and the Canon of Medicine

In medicine and philosophy, the towering figure was al-Bīrūnī's contemporary (they wrote letters to each other) Ibn Sīnā (c. 980–1037 CE), known in Europe as Avicenna. [4]

Born near Bukhara, Ibn Sīnā was a prodigy who, by his own account, had mastered religion, law, geometry, astronomy and medicine by the age of eighteen. His great medical book, the Canon of Medicine (al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb, about 1025 CE), runs to about a million words in five books:

  1. General principles: the body, causes of disease, symptoms and healthy living.
  2. Medicines: about 760 plant, mineral and animal remedies.
  3. Diseases of each part of the body, from head to toe.
  4. Diseases of the whole body: fevers, fractures, poisons and skin conditions.
  5. Recipes for compound medicines and ointments.

The Canon was admired not only for its huge range but for its order. Ibn Sīnā combined the practical experience of Greek and Arabic doctors such as Galen and al-Rāzī with Aristotle's way of organising knowledge. He gave influential descriptions of diabetes, of tuberculosis as catching, of diseases spreading through water and soil, and of the difference between facial paralysis caused in the brain and in the nerves of the face.

He also set out rules for testing medicines that sound surprisingly modern. Among them: test the medicine in its pure form; test it on a simple case of the disease, without complications; test it on opposite kinds of case; note how long it takes to work; and check that it has the same effect again and again in many patients, so you are not fooled by chance.

Alongside the ideas came new institutions: the bīmāristān, or hospital. Building on earlier Persian and Byzantine Christian hospitals, great hospitals such as the ʿAḍudī Hospital in Baghdad (founded 982 CE) and the Manṣūrī Hospital in Cairo (1284 CE) were publicly funded and open to all. They had separate wards for men and women and for surgery, eye disease, fevers and mental illness, along with pharmacies, libraries and lecture rooms, and doctors had to pass examinations.

6. Kerala: Infinite Sums Long Before Calculus

Meanwhile, on the south-west coast of India, a line of astronomer-mathematicians known as the Kerala school made a remarkable leap: they found ways to write important quantities as infinite sums, an idea at the heart of later calculus. [6]

The school's founder was Mādhava of Sangamagrāma (c. 1340–1425 CE). He and his successors, including Parameśvara, Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī (author of the Tantrasaṅgraha) and Jyeṣṭhadeva (author of the Yuktibhāṣā, written in Malayalam), had a practical problem: to calculate planetary positions accurately, they needed very accurate sine tables.

By cutting a curved arc into smaller and smaller pieces and adding them up, Mādhava found exact formulas that express angles, sines and cosines as sums that go on forever. The most famous gives π:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{9} - \dots$$

Take one, subtract a third, add a fifth, subtract a seventh, and keep going forever: the total is exactly a quarter of π. In Western textbooks this is usually called the "Leibniz series" (Leibniz, 1673) or the "Gregory series" (James Gregory, 1671). But later Kerala books credit it, with a proof, to Mādhava, more than 250 years earlier (his own writings on it are lost).

Mādhava also found the infinite sums for the sine and cosine:

$$\sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - \dots \qquad \cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \dots$$

(Here 3! means 3 × 2 × 1, and so on.) There was a catch: the sum for π creeps towards the answer painfully slowly; you need thousands of terms for just three correct decimal places. So Mādhava added a clever correction for the part left over when you stop (antya-saṁskāra). Stop after a certain number of terms, add a simple correction fraction, and the answer jumps much closer to the truth. Later Kerala writers record a value of π from Mādhava correct to eleven decimal places: 3.14159265359. In the Yuktibhāṣā, Jyeṣṭhadeva gave full proofs of these results, using sums that amount to a basic rule of calculus (five centuries earlier, Ibn al-Haytham had used similar sums for a different problem).

7. Song China: Shen Kuo and Su Song

In China, the Song dynasty (960–1279 CE) was a time of booming trade, growing cities and new technology. China had produced the three inventions that Francis Bacon, not knowing where they came from, later said had "changed the whole face and state of things throughout the world": the magnetic compass, gunpowder and printing. [7]

The great all-round scholar of the time was Shen Kuo (1031–1095 CE): a senior official, engineer, diplomat and head of the state astronomy office. In his book Dream Pool Essays (1088 CE) he wrote about an extraordinary range of subjects:

  • The compass: he gave the earliest clear description of a magnetic needle compass, and noticed that the needle doesn't point exactly south but slightly to the east. The difference between magnetic north–south and true north–south is called magnetic declination, and he noticed it long before European navigators did.
  • How landscapes form: seeing fossil sea shells in cliffs of the Taihang Mountains, far from the sea, he reasoned that the land had been built up over very long times by mud and silt. Finding fossil bamboo in the dry north, where bamboo doesn't grow, he concluded that the climate there had once been very different.
  • Oil: he named petroleum shiyou, "rock oil", made ink from its soot and predicted: "This substance will surely have a great vogue in the world."

Shen Kuo's contemporary Su Song (1020–1101 CE) built the greatest machine of medieval China: a water-powered astronomical clock tower at Kaifeng, finished in 1092 CE. More than 12 metres tall, it had a rotating bronze instrument for watching the sky on top, a turning star globe inside, and five levels of little mechanical figures that rang bells and struck gongs to mark the time.

Its secret was the way it turned a steady flow of water into regular "ticks". Water poured into buckets on the rim of a big water wheel, about 3.4 metres across. The wheel was held still by a balance lever. When the top bucket filled enough to outweigh a counterweight, the lever tipped, let the wheel move forward by exactly one bucket, and then locked it again. This kind of stop-and-go device is called an escapement, and it is what makes a clock tick. Su Song's version built on earlier Chinese water clocks of the 8th and 10th centuries, and came long before mechanical clocks appeared in Europe in the 1300s.

8. Into Latin: Toledo and the Translators

By the 1000s western Europe was recovering. Trade picked up, better ploughs and crop rotation produced more food, and populations grew. European scholars became aware that their Muslim and Byzantine neighbours had a huge library of philosophy, astronomy and medicine that nobody could read in Latin. [4, 5]

The meeting happened where Christian and Muslim lands touched: in Spain and in southern Italy and Sicily.

The most important centre was Toledo in Spain, taken from Muslim rule by King Alfonso VI of Castile in 1085. Toledo still had large libraries of Arabic books. With support from Archbishop Raymond (in office 1126–1152), scholars from all over Europe came there to translate them into Latin.

The star of this work was Gerard of Cremona (c. 1114–1187 CE). He came from northern Italy looking for Ptolemy's Almagest, was so amazed by what he found that he stayed for the rest of his life, learned Arabic and translated more than eighty major works into Latin, including:

  • Euclid's Elements.
  • Ptolemy's Almagest, the great textbook of medieval astronomy.
  • Al-Khwārizmī's Algebra.
  • Ibn Sīnā's Canon of Medicine.
  • Works by the philosopher al-Fārābī.
  • The surgical work of al-Zahrāwī (Abulcasis), with drawings of more than 200 surgical instruments.

In southern Italy, Constantine the African translated Arabic medical books at the monastery of Monte Cassino, and in the next century William of Moerbeke translated Aristotle directly from Greek. Within about a hundred years (1150–1250 CE), much of the science and philosophy of the ancient world, corrected and greatly expanded by four centuries of work in the Islamic world, poured into western Europe.

9. The First Universities: Learning by Arguing

This flood of difficult new books raised a practical question: where would they be taught and studied? Cathedral and monastery schools, set up mainly to train clergy, weren't equipped for advanced maths, physics and medicine. [4]

The medieval answer was a new kind of institution: the university. The first grew up at Bologna (traditionally dated 1088), Paris (about 1150) and Oxford (about 1167). A university was a universitas, a self-governing guild of teachers and students. Charters from kings and popes gave it its own courts, the right to go on strike, and the right to set its own courses and award degrees that allowed graduates to teach anywhere.

Students first studied the seven liberal arts:

  • Three arts of language: grammar, rhetoric (persuasive speaking) and logic.
  • Four mathematical arts: arithmetic, geometry, music (the mathematics of harmony) and astronomy.

After these and Aristotle's natural philosophy, they could go on to the higher faculties of medicine, law or theology.

At the heart of university teaching was the disputation, a formal debate. A teacher put forward a question, such as "Is motion possible in a vacuum?" or "Does the Earth stand still at the centre of the world?" A student or opponent then made the strongest possible case against the teacher's view. Finally the teacher gave a reasoned answer, replying to every objection one by one.

This trained people to take ideas apart and test them. You couldn't just quote an authority; every claim had to survive public challenge. Far from being a prison of fixed beliefs, the medieval university trained generations of Europeans in careful, critical reasoning about nature.

10. Faith and Reason: Thomas Aquinas and Roger Bacon

The arrival of Aristotle's writings in the 1200s caused a crisis for Christian thinkers. Aristotle described a universe that had always existed and always would, ran by fixed natural rules, and seemed to leave no room for a soul that survives death. To many churchmen, Aristotle looked like a dangerous unbeliever. [4, 5]

The Dominican friar Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), teaching at Paris, worked out a reconciliation. In his huge Summa Theologiae he argued that faith and reason can't truly contradict each other, because both come from the same God. Human reason, working from observation of nature, can discover real truths about the world. Aristotle became known simply as "the Philosopher", and his physics became the basis of university education.

Meanwhile the English Franciscan friar Roger Bacon (c. 1220–1292), nicknamed the "Wonderful Doctor", pushed further. He was impatient with scholars who argued about words without ever looking at nature. In his Opus Majus (1267), written for Pope Clement IV, he named four main causes of error: trusting weak authorities, habit, popular opinion, and pretending to know what you don't.

Real knowledge of nature, Bacon said, rests on two things: mathematics and experiment:

"Without experiment, nothing can be known adequately. A theoretical argument proves a point, but it does not make the mind certain, nor does it remove doubt so that the mind may rest in the intuition of truth, unless the mind finds it by the way of experience."
— Roger Bacon, Opus Majus, Part VI (1267)

Drawing on Ibn al-Haytham's Optics, Bacon experimented with lenses, curved mirrors and the eye. He described how lenses could help weak eyes (spectacles were invented in northern Italy around 1286) and imagined something like a telescope: "We can so shape transparent bodies... that rays will be broken and bent in whatever direction we desire... Thus from an incredible distance we might read the smallest letters."

11. Putting Numbers on Motion: Oxford and Oresme

In the 1300s, natural philosophy took a big step towards modern physics: describing motion with numbers. At Merton College, Oxford, between about 1325 and 1350, a group of scholars including Thomas Bradwardine, William Heytesbury and Richard Swineshead, now called the "Merton Calculators", began to analyse motion mathematically. [10, 5]

Aristotle had described motion in words. The Mertonians made it precise. They clearly separated two ideas:

  • Speed at an instant: how fast something is going at one particular moment (like a speedometer reading).
  • Steady acceleration: speeding up by the same amount in each equal stretch of time.

They then proved what is called the mean speed rule: something that speeds up steadily from rest covers the same distance as something that travels for the same time at a constant speed equal to half its final speed (its average speed).

The Merton proofs were in words and arithmetic. A generation later in Paris, the philosopher (and later bishop) Nicole Oresme (c. 1320–1382) found a way to see it. He drew one of the first graphs of motion.

As Figure 6 shows, Oresme drew time along the bottom and speed going upwards. For something speeding up steadily, the speed line rises in a straight slope, making a triangle. Then came his key insight: the distance travelled equals the area under the speed line. Draw a flat line at the average speed (half the top speed): the bit of the triangle above it is exactly the same size as the gap below it, so you can move one into the other and turn the triangle into a rectangle of the same area. Distance = time × average speed. When Galileo published his study of falling objects in 1638, he used a very similar diagram (whether he knew Oresme's work is debated).

Oresme was bold about the heavens too. In 1377 he asked whether the Earth might spin daily while the heavens stay still. He showed that what we see in the sky would look exactly the same either way, and answered objections (why doesn't an arrow shot straight up land behind us?) by pointing out that motion is relative: "If a man in a boat moving smoothly down a river cannot see the banks, it appears to him that the boat is stationary and the trees are moving." Having shown that no argument could settle it, he concluded, out of respect for scripture and common opinion, that the Earth stands still.

12. Fixing Ptolemy: The Marāgha Astronomers

While Europeans were drawing graphs of motion, astronomers in the Islamic world were tackling a deep problem in Ptolemy's astronomy. [2]

The rule since Aristotle was that heavenly bodies must move at a steady speed in perfect circles. But to make his predictions match the sky, Ptolemy had used a trick called the equant: his planets moved at a steady rate not around the centre of their circle, nor around the Earth, but as seen from a separate, empty point in space. To medieval astronomers this made no physical sense. How could a planet "know" to keep steady time relative to an empty point?

In 1259 CE the Mongol ruler Hülegü Khan, a grandson of Genghis Khan, founded a great observatory at Marāgha in north-western Iran and put the scholar Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (1201–1274 CE) in charge. It had large instruments for measuring star positions, a workshop making brass astrolabes, and, according to later reports, a library of 400,000 books.

To get rid of the equant while keeping only steady circular motions, al-Ṭūsī invented a beautiful device now called the Ṭūsī couple. As Figure 7 shows, a small circle rolls around the inside of a circle twice its size. A point on the small circle doesn't trace a loop; it moves back and forth in a straight line. Two steady circular motions combine to make a straight-line motion.

A century later in Damascus, Ibn al-Shāṭir (1304–1375 CE), timekeeper at the Great Mosque, used such devices to build models of the Moon and planets with no equant at all. His Moon model also fixed a famous flaw in Ptolemy's, which predicted that the Moon should look almost twice as big at some times as at others (it doesn't).

When Copernicus published his Sun-centred astronomy in 1543, his models of the Moon and Mercury were mathematically the same as Ibn al-Shāṭir's, and he used the Ṭūsī couple. How these ideas reached him is still debated. The historian Willy Hartner noticed that Copernicus's diagram of the Ṭūsī couple even labels its points with letters matching those in al-Ṭūsī's diagram. Tools that Copernicus used in launching the Scientific Revolution had been made in the observatories of the Islamic world.

13. How Did They Know? Ibn al-Haytham's Dark Room

How did they know? Ibn al-Haytham's lamp experiment

How could someone in the 11th century, with no electricity or cameras, show that we don't see by sending rays out of our eyes? [1]

In the Book of Optics, Ibn al-Haytham describes his set-up clearly enough to repeat it. In simple form:

  1. A dark room: a room with one window onto a courtyard, made completely dark.
  2. A small hole: a small, neat round hole in the window shutter.
  3. Several lamps: outside, several candles or lamps placed side by side at different positions, facing the hole.
  4. A white screen: inside, opposite the hole, a flat white board.

What he saw: each lamp made its own bright spot on the screen. The highest lamp made the lowest spot; the lowest lamp made the highest. Each lamp, its spot and the hole lay on one straight line.

Then the key test: an assistant held a board in front of one lamp. That lamp's spot vanished at once, while all the others stayed bright and unchanged. If seeing depended on rays coming out of the viewer's eyes, covering one lamp outside couldn't make just one spot disappear inside. So light must be something real that travels from each lamp, in straight lines, into the room and into our eyes. It was a beautifully controlled experiment.

14. Legend and Record: The Burning of the Library of Alexandria

Legend and Record: was there one great fire?

Few events in the history of knowledge are as famous, or as misunderstood, as the destruction of the Great Library of Alexandria. The popular picture is a single great fire in which hundreds of thousands of scrolls burned in an afternoon, throwing the world into darkness. [9]

Depending on who tells the story, one of three villains gets the blame:

  • Story A: Julius Caesar burned it in 48 BCE during his civil war.
  • Story B: Christians led by Bishop Theophilus burned it in 391 CE when they destroyed the temple of Serapis.
  • Story C: The Arab general ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ burned it in 642 CE on the caliph's orders, supposedly saying: "If these books agree with the Book of God, they are not needed; if they disagree, destroy them."

What the evidence shows: Historians such as Luciano Canfora, Mostafa El-Abbadi and Bernard Lewis have shown that the single great fire is a myth:

  1. Caesar (48 BCE): his troops accidentally burned scrolls stored in warehouses near the docks, but the Museum and Library survived; the geographer Strabo worked at the Museum decades later.
  2. Roman wars (3rd century CE): the royal quarter of the city, where the Library stood, was badly damaged when the emperors Aurelian (272 CE) and Diocletian (297 CE) retook the city.
  3. The Serapeum (391 CE): Theophilus destroyed the temple of Serapis, which may have held a smaller "daughter library", but writers of the time describe the destruction of statues and a temple, not a great library of science.
  4. The Arab story (642 CE): the tale of the caliph's order first appears in the 13th century, more than 500 years after the Arab conquest. Writers who actually lived through the conquest, such as the Egyptian bishop John of Nikiû, describe it in detail and never mention a library, because by then the Great Library had long since disappeared.

The Great Library didn't die in one dramatic fire. It faded slowly, through neglect, lack of money, damp, insects and civil wars under Roman rule. Ancient texts survived not because one building kept them safe, but because scholars kept copying, translating and spreading them across the Mediterranean world.

15. Philosophy Corner: Did a Church Ban Help Science?

Philosophy Corner: the Paris condemnations of 1277

In 1277 the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, following a request from Pope John XXI, condemned 219 statements being taught at the University of Paris. Teachers were forbidden, on pain of being thrown out of the Church, to teach that Aristotle could not be wrong, or that philosophy could prove things limiting what God can do. [4, 5]

At first sight this looks like religion attacking free thought. But look at some of the banned statements, which were ideas of Aristotle that we now know are wrong:

  • "That God cannot create more than one world." (Aristotle said more than one world was impossible.)
  • "That God cannot move the whole heavens in a straight line, because that would leave a vacuum behind." (Aristotle said empty space can't exist.)

The French physicist and historian Pierre Duhem argued that the condemnations had a surprising effect: they loosened Aristotle's grip on physics. Later historians think Duhem went too far, but agree that the ban encouraged people to think about what God could have made.

Once it was official that God could make a vacuum, or other worlds, or infinite space, teachers could no longer say "this is impossible because Aristotle said so". So thinkers such as Jean Buridan, Nicole Oresme and Thomas Bradwardine began asking "what if?" questions: if there were a vacuum, how would things fall in it? If the Earth did spin, what would we feel? Imagining worlds that worked by different rules helped loosen the hold of Aristotle's physics and prepared the ground for the Scientific Revolution.
