Linear Models: Gradient Descent, Regularisation and Logistic Regression

The oldest models in machine learning are still among the most useful, and the building block of every neural network. Here is how they learn by gradient descent, how regularisation tames them, and how a line becomes a classifier.

1. Why Start with Straight Lines?

Linear models are the oldest tools in machine learning and still among the most used. They are fast, need little data, and their parameters can be read and explained. They are the baseline every fancier model must beat, and they are the building block of neural networks: a single artificial neuron is a linear model followed by a simple function (Part 6). Understanding them well, including how they are trained, how they are kept from overfitting and how they classify, makes everything later easier. [1, 2]

3. Linear Regression as a Learning Machine

A linear regression model predicts a number by adding up the features, each multiplied by a weight, plus a constant bias (intercept):

$$\hat{y} = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_dx_d + b = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b.$$

Each weight says how much the prediction changes when that feature increases by one unit, with the others held fixed. Learning means finding the weights that minimise the mean squared error on the training data. For this particular model and loss there is an exact answer, the method of least squares published by Legendre in 1805 and covered statistically in Making Sense of Data Part 7. [3] Here we look at linear regression through machine-learning eyes: as a model to be trained, regularised and validated.

The maths: the normal equation

Stack the n training examples as rows of a matrix X (with a column of ones for the bias) and the targets in a vector y. The mean squared error is ‖y − Xw‖²/n, and setting its gradient to zero gives the normal equation:

$$X^\top X\,\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbf{w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}.$$

Solving it costs roughly d³ operations for d features, fine for hundreds of features but not for millions, and it fails when features are perfectly correlated (X⊤X cannot be inverted). Both problems motivate the next two sections.

4. Gradient Descent from Scratch

Imagine the loss as a landscape over the space of parameters: every choice of weights is a point, and its height is the loss. Training means finding the lowest point. Gradient descent does it like a hiker in fog: feel which way the ground slopes most steeply downhill, take a step that way, and repeat. The slope is the gradient, the vector of partial derivatives of the loss with respect to each parameter. Augustin-Louis Cauchy described the method in 1847. [4]

$$\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\,\text{Loss}(\mathbf{w}).$$

The learning rate η sets the step size. It is the most important hyperparameter in all of deep learning, and Figure 3 shows why.

With the raw feature, which runs from 0 to 10, the loss surface is a long, narrow valley: about 190 times steeper across than along. Any learning rate above about 0.024 makes the steep direction overshoot and diverge, yet at that rate progress along the valley floor takes hundreds of steps. After standardising the feature (subtracting its mean and dividing by its standard deviation) the valley becomes round, and a learning rate of 0.5 reaches the minimum in a single step.

Watch out: scale your features

Features on very different scales (metres and millimetres, income and age) make gradient descent slow or unstable, and make penalties such as ridge and lasso unfair to some features. Standardise them, fitting the scaler on the training data only (Part 1, Section 11).

In code: gradient descent for linear regression in NumPy 
import numpy as np

def gradient_descent(X, y, lr=0.1, steps=500):
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])     # add a bias column
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = 2 / len(y) * X.T @ (X @ w - y)     # gradient of the MSE
        w -= lr * grad
    return w

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, 30); y = 2 * x + 5 + rng.normal(0, 2, 30)
z = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()                      # standardise
print(gradient_descent(z, y))                     # [intercept, slope on z]

5. Stochastic and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent

Computing the exact gradient means passing over every training example for every step: expensive when there are millions. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) instead estimates the gradient from one example, or a small random mini-batch, at a time. Each step is noisy but cheap, and over many steps the noise averages out. One full pass through the training data is called an epoch.

The idea goes back to Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro’s stochastic approximation of 1951, which showed that such noisy updates converge if the step size shrinks appropriately, and to Bernard Widrow and Ted Hoff’s least-mean-squares rule of 1960, used to train an adaptive electronic neuron. [5, 6] Today almost every neural network is trained with a mini-batch variant of SGD, refined with momentum and adaptive learning rates (Part 8). The noise is not only tolerated: it may help models find solutions that generalise better.

6. Curves from Lines: Basis Functions

“Linear” refers to the parameters, not the inputs. A model is linear if the prediction is a weighted sum, but the things being weighted can be any functions of the inputs: x², √x, sin x, log x, or products such as x1x2. These basis functions (or engineered features) let a linear model fit curves while keeping all the advantages of linear fitting.

The polynomials of Part 1 were exactly this: the features 1, x, x², …, x9 with ordinary least squares. Other popular choices are splines (smooth curves joined at knots) and Fourier features (sines and cosines). Interaction terms such as x1x2 let the effect of one feature depend on another, like the interactions of ANOVA (Making Sense of Data Part 6). The catch, as Part 1 showed, is that many flexible features invite overfitting. That is what regularisation is for.

Check yourself: linear or not?

ŷ = w0 + w1ex: linear (a weighted sum of fixed functions). ŷ = w0ew1x: not linear, because the parameter w1 sits inside the exponential.

7. Regularisation: Ridge, Lasso and Elastic Net

Regularisation fights overfitting by adding a penalty for large weights to the loss. The model must now balance fitting the data against keeping its weights small, which favours smoother, simpler functions. The strength of the penalty, λ, is a hyperparameter chosen by cross-validation.

  • Ridge regression (L2 penalty) adds λ times the sum of squared weights. Andrey Tikhonov introduced the idea in 1943 for unstable “inverse problems”, and Arthur Hoerl and Robert Kennard proposed it for regression in 1970, to cope with highly correlated predictors. [7, 8] It shrinks all weights smoothly towards zero.
  • The lasso (L1 penalty) adds λ times the sum of absolute weights. Robert Tibshirani proposed it in 1996. [9] It shrinks weights and sets some of them exactly to zero, so it also selects features.
  • Elastic net combines both, keeping the lasso’s sparsity while handling groups of correlated features more gracefully. [10]

On the running example (with standardised features), the nearly unpenalised degree-9 fit has a test error of about 14. Ridge brings it down to 0.12 at λ ≈ 0.06; the lasso does better still, 0.088 at λ ≈ 0.006, using only 4 of the 9 terms. Neither quite matches the plain cubic of Part 1 (0.075), a reminder that choosing the right features can beat regularising the wrong ones.

The maths: the penalised losses

$$\text{Ridge: } \frac{1}{n}\lVert \mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2, \qquad \text{Lasso: } \frac{1}{2n}\lVert \mathbf{y}-X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1.$$

Ridge still has an exact solution, w = (X⊤X + nλI)−1X⊤y, and the added nλI makes the matrix invertible even when features are collinear. The lasso has no closed form; it is usually solved by coordinate descent, updating one weight at a time with a “soft-thresholding” step that snaps small weights to zero, which computes whole regularisation paths quickly. [11] The bias is not penalised, and features must be standardised so that the penalty treats them equally. In Bayesian terms, ridge corresponds to a Gaussian prior on the weights and the lasso to a Laplace prior.

8. Why the Lasso Gives Zeros

Why should absolute values produce exact zeros when squares do not? A penalty is equivalent to a constraint: keep the weights inside a region while minimising the loss. For L2 the region is a circle (a sphere in more dimensions); for L1 it is a diamond, with corners sitting on the axes. The solution is where the smallest contour of the loss touches the region, and contours tend to hit the diamond at a corner, where one or more weights are zero.

9. Classifying with a Line: Logistic Regression

To classify, a linear model computes a score z = w⊤x + b and decides by its sign. Rosenblatt’s perceptron of 1958 did exactly that, adjusting its weights whenever it misclassified an example. [12] But a bare sign says nothing about confidence. Logistic regression passes the score through the S-shaped sigmoid function, turning it into a probability:

$$P(y=1\mid\mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b)}}.$$

The decision boundary, where the probability is 0.5, is where the score is zero: a straight line in two dimensions, a flat plane in more. David Cox set out the regression analysis of binary outcomes in 1958, [13] and logistic regression remains a workhorse of medicine, social science and industry, and one of the strongest baselines for any classification task. Its statistical side, with odds ratios, is covered in Making Sense of Data Part 7.

The running example: classification

From this part on, the series uses three small two-dimensional classification data sets of 200 points each, split into 140 training and 60 test points: two clouds that a straight line can separate, two moons that interlock, and circles, one class inside the other. Because they are two-dimensional, every model’s decision boundary can be drawn and compared across parts.

With the raw features, logistic regression scores 90% on the test points of the two clouds and also 90% on the moons, but only 55% on the circles: no straight line can separate a ring from its centre. Adding polynomial features of degree 3 lifts the moons to 95% and the circles to 98%, while the model stays linear in its parameters.

10. Cross-Entropy and Softmax

Logistic regression is trained by minimising the cross-entropy or log loss: the average of −log of the probability the model gave to the true class. A confident correct prediction costs almost nothing; a confident wrong one costs a great deal. Minimising it is the same as maximising the likelihood of the data, and unlike squared error it gives a well-behaved (convex) loss surface for this model.

For more than two classes, softmax regression (multinomial logistic regression) computes one linear score per class and turns the scores into probabilities that are positive and sum to one. The name “softmax” was coined by John Bridle in 1990. [14] The same softmax sits at the output of almost every neural-network classifier (Parts 6 and 7). Linear, logistic and Poisson regression are all members of the family of generalised linear models introduced by John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn in 1972. [15]

The maths: log loss, its gradient and softmax

With pi = σ(w⊤xi + b),

$$\text{Loss} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big], \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (p_i - y_i)\,\mathbf{x}_i.$$

The gradient has the same form as for linear regression, with the prediction error pi − yi. For K classes with scores zk,

$$\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}.$$

In code: regularised logistic regression with scikit-learn 
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=200, noise=0.2, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0)

for degree in [1, 3]:
    clf = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree), StandardScaler(),
                        LogisticRegression(C=10, max_iter=5000))   # C = 1/lambda
    print(degree, cross_val_score(clf, X_tr, y_tr, cv=5).mean())

11. Reading the Coefficients

A great strength of linear models is that they can be read. In linear regression a weight is the change in prediction per unit change of its feature, others held fixed; in logistic regression, ew is the odds ratio per unit. But reading them correctly takes care:

  • Units matter. A weight of 0.001 per metre is the same as 1 per kilometre. Compare standardised coefficients to judge relative importance.
  • Correlated features share credit unpredictably: two nearly identical features can get large weights of opposite sign. Ridge stabilises them; the lasso tends to pick one arbitrarily.
  • “Held fixed” may be impossible. You cannot raise a house’s floor area while holding its number of rooms fixed in real life.
  • Association, not causation. A weight describes the pattern in the data, not what would happen if you intervened (Making Sense of Data Part 7).

12. When Linear Models Shine, and When They Fail

Linear models are the right first choice when data are limited, when there are many features relative to examples (text, genomics), when interpretability matters, or when predictions must be fast. With good features and regularisation they are surprisingly hard to beat on many tabular problems.

They struggle when the relationship is strongly nonlinear or full of interactions that nobody thought to engineer: images, sound, language, or the interlocking moons of Figure 6. One remedy is to engineer features by hand, as with polynomials. The rest of the series explores the alternatives that learn nonlinear shapes themselves: neighbours and trees (Part 3), ensembles (Part 4), kernels (Part 5) and neural networks (Parts 6–9), which in a sense learn their own basis functions.

13. Open Questions

Open questions: when is fitting the noise harmless?

Part 1 warned that a model fitting its training data exactly will overfit. Yet when a linear model has many more features than examples, gradient descent started from zero converges to the solution with the smallest weights among the infinitely many that fit the data exactly: a hidden form of ridge-like regularisation, called implicit regularisation. In 2020 Peter Bartlett and colleagues showed precisely when such a model can interpolate noisy data and still predict nearly optimally, a phenomenon they called benign overfitting. [16] Linear models have become the laboratory in which researchers try to understand why huge neural networks generalise.

14. Worked Example: Regularising the Running Example

  1. Features. Expand x into the nine powers u, u², …, u9 (with u = 2x − 1), and standardise each using the training points only.
  2. Unpenalised fit. Test error about 14: severe overfitting, as in Part 1.
  3. Ridge path. Test error 0.38 at λ = 0.001, 0.21 at 0.01, 0.14 at 0.1 and 0.35 at 1, with a best of 0.12 near λ = 0.06. All nine coefficients stay non-zero.
  4. Lasso path. Test error 0.21 at λ = 0.001 (5 terms), 0.090 at 0.01 (5 terms), 0.20 at 0.1 (3 terms), and at λ = 1 every coefficient is zero and the model predicts a constant (0.63). Best: 0.088 near λ = 0.006 with 4 terms.
  5. In practice you would choose λ by cross-validation on the training data (Part 1), and only then report the test error once. The numbers above use the test set to show the whole path, which is fine for teaching but not for reporting.
In code: ridge and lasso paths with scikit-learn 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso, LassoCV

# x_train, y_train, x_test: the running example of Part 1
def powers(x, d=9):
    u = 2 * x - 1
    return np.column_stack([u**k for k in range(1, d + 1)])

scaler = StandardScaler().fit(powers(x_train))
A, B = scaler.transform(powers(x_train)), scaler.transform(powers(x_test))

ridge = Ridge(alpha=15 * 0.056).fit(A, y_train)   # sklearn alpha = n * lambda
lasso = Lasso(alpha=0.0056, max_iter=100000).fit(A, y_train)
print((lasso.coef_ != 0).sum())                    # 4 non-zero terms
best = LassoCV(cv=5).fit(A, y_train).alpha_        # choose lambda by CV

16. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “linear models can only draw straight lines”

The myth: linear models are too simple for real problems, because the world is not linear.

The reality: a linear model is linear in its parameters. With the right features (polynomials, splines, interactions, or features learned by another model) it can represent very complicated curves and boundaries, as the circles of Figure 6 show. Kernel methods (Part 5) push this idea to infinitely many features, and the last layer of most neural networks is a linear or logistic regression on features the network has learned.

Why it matters: before reaching for a complex model, it is often worth asking whether a simple model with better features would do, and be easier to trust.

17. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • A linear model predicts with a weighted sum of features; it is linear in its weights, not necessarily in its inputs.
  • Gradient descent steps downhill on the loss; the learning rate must be neither too small nor too large, and standardised features make it far easier.
  • Stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent trade exact gradients for cheap, noisy steps, and power deep learning.
  • Ridge shrinks weights; the lasso shrinks and selects; elastic net does both. Choose λ by cross-validation.
  • Logistic regression turns a linear score into a probability with the sigmoid and is trained with cross-entropy; softmax extends it to many classes.
  • Linear models are fast, interpretable, strong baselines, but need engineered features for nonlinear problems.
