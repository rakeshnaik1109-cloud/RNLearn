Where We Are Now: Reasoning Models, Agents and the AI Boom

In four years AI went from a novelty to a tool used by hundreds of millions of people every week. Models learned to see, hear, reason and act, while the old problems of reliability, cost and trust remained.

1. The Present, as of November 2026

A note on dates

This part describes a field that changes month by month. It was written in November 2026, and focuses on developments that are well documented. New models appear so often that specific names and rankings go out of date quickly; the broad trends described here are what matter.

In the four years since ChatGPT arrived, AI has moved from a curiosity to something hundreds of millions of people use every week. OpenAI said in October 2025 that 800 million people used ChatGPT each week, and it is only one of several popular assistants. AI writes software, drafts documents, tutors students, transcribes meetings, generates images and video, and helps scientists. [1, 2]

Four big shifts stand out, and this part takes them in turn:

  • Models that see, hear and speak, not just read and write text.
  • Models that reason, working through a problem at length before answering.
  • Agents: AI that doesn't just answer but acts, using tools to carry out long, multi-step tasks.
  • Enormous investment in chips, data centres and electricity, with real questions about cost and limits.

Alongside the progress, old problems persist: models still make things up, still fail in surprising ways, and still raise hard questions about copyright, jobs and trust. This part tries to give an honest picture of both.

3. Bigger, Faster, and Able to See and Hear

In March 2023 OpenAI released GPT-4. It was much more capable than the model behind the original ChatGPT, and it could read images: show it a photo of the inside of your fridge and it could suggest recipes. OpenAI reported strong results on many professional and academic exams (section 14 looks at one of those claims closely). [3]

Rivals followed quickly. Google DeepMind's Gemini (December 2023) was designed from the start to handle text, images, audio and video together. Anthropic's Claude models, xAI's Grok and others joined a race in which the lead changed hands every few months. In May 2024 OpenAI's GPT-4o could hold a spoken conversation with almost no delay, and look through a phone's camera while doing so. Models that handle several kinds of data are called multimodal. [4]

A second trend was open-weight models. In July 2023 Meta released Llama 2, whose weights anyone could download and run on their own computers. The French company Mistral, China's DeepSeek and Alibaba (with its Qwen models) and many others released capable open models. These let researchers, companies and governments build on AI without depending on a few providers, but they also make it impossible to recall a model, or stop it being misused, once it has been released. [5]

Models also became much cheaper to use. Stanford's 2025 AI Index found that the price of running a model as good as the original ChatGPT model fell more than 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024. Capabilities once limited to the largest systems spread to small models that can run on a laptop or phone. [1]

4. Models That Think Before Answering

Part 6 described chain-of-thought prompting: asking a model to show its working improves its answers. In September 2024 OpenAI released o1, a model trained to do this by itself, at length. Before answering a hard question it produces a long internal chain of reasoning, trying approaches, checking them and backtracking from mistakes, sometimes for minutes. On competition maths and difficult coding problems, it did far better than earlier models. [6]

The key was reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as maths problems with a known answer, or code that either passes its tests or doesn't. The model is rewarded when its reasoning reaches a correct answer, and over many problems it learns better ways of reasoning. In January 2025 the Chinese company DeepSeek published a detailed account of how it trained a similar model, R1, including the striking finding that behaviours like re-checking its own work emerged during this training without being specifically taught. [7]

These models introduced a new way to get better answers: spend more computing power while answering, not just while training. Thinking longer helps, and so does making several attempts and comparing them. A simple version, called self-consistency, takes the most common answer from many independent attempts: right answers tend to agree with each other, while mistakes scatter. [8]

By 2025 most leading systems combined both modes. GPT-5 (August 2025), for example, decides for itself whether to answer quickly or to reason at length, depending on how hard a question seems.

5. The DeepSeek Moment

Until 2025 many assumed that only a handful of American companies, spending billions of dollars on chips, could build frontier AI. DeepSeek challenged that. Its V3 model (December 2024) and R1 reasoning model (January 2025) matched leading Western systems on many tests, were released with open weights, and came with detailed technical reports. DeepSeek said the final training run of V3 cost about $5.6 million in computing time, a small fraction of what rivals were thought to spend, although that figure excluded earlier research and experiments. [9, 7]

The reaction was dramatic. On 27 January 2025, shares in the chip maker Nvidia fell by about 17 per cent, wiping nearly $600 billion off its value in a single day, the largest one-day loss for any company in US stock-market history. Investors wondered whether the huge spending on chips was really necessary. It was also a reminder that US export controls on advanced chips had not stopped Chinese labs from competing. [10]

The panic was short-lived: cheaper AI tended to mean more use of AI, not less demand for chips. But the episode showed that clever engineering could partly substitute for raw computing power, and that open models could be close to the frontier.

6. Mathematics and Science

The International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO) is the world's hardest maths competition for school students. In July 2024 Google DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 scored at silver-medal level, though the problems had to be translated into a formal mathematical language first and some took days. A year later, in July 2025, an advanced version of Google DeepMind's Gemini solved five of the six problems, working in ordinary language within the competition's time limit, a score of 35 out of 42 that the IMO's organisers confirmed was at gold-medal level. OpenAI reported the same score for one of its experimental models, graded by former medallists it recruited. [11, 12]

Science benefited too. In May 2024 AlphaFold 3 extended protein-structure prediction (Part 5) to how proteins interact with DNA, RNA and potential drug molecules. AI weather models such as Google DeepMind's GraphCast, trained on decades of past weather records, began producing ten-day forecasts that matched or beat traditional physics-based forecasts on many measures, using a tiny fraction of the computing power, and national weather services started using such models alongside their own. Researchers also began using language models to suggest hypotheses, search the scientific literature and write analysis code. [13, 14]

7. Agents: From Chatting to Doing

A chatbot answers questions. An agent carries out tasks. Given a goal, it plans steps, uses tools (searching the web, running code, reading and editing files, operating a web browser), looks at the results, and decides what to do next, repeating this loop until the job is done or it gets stuck. The basic recipe, interleaving reasoning with actions, was described by researchers in 2022. [15]

In October 2024 Anthropic released a model that could use a computer as a person does, by looking at screenshots and controlling the mouse and keyboard; OpenAI and Google followed with similar tools. In November 2024 Anthropic published the Model Context Protocol, an open standard for connecting AI models to tools and data, which other companies soon adopted. [16, 17]

The biggest impact by far has been in software. Coding agents from several companies can read a large codebase, make changes across many files, run tests and fix their own errors. In February 2025 the AI researcher Andrej Karpathy coined the phrase "vibe coding" for building software by describing what you want and letting AI write the code; it became Collins Dictionary's word of the year for 2025. Through 2026, much of the competition between leading labs has centred on how long and complex a task their agents can handle reliably.

Agents also bring new risks. An agent that reads web pages or emails can be tricked by hidden instructions planted in them, an attack called prompt injection. And the more an agent can do, from spending money to deleting files, the more a single mistake can cost. Most systems therefore ask a person to approve important actions. [18]

8. How Fast Is It Improving?

Measuring AI progress has become surprisingly hard. Standard tests (benchmarks) keep getting "saturated": models score close to 100 per cent, and the test stops telling us anything. Some test questions also leak into training data. Researchers have responded with harder tests, such as Humanity's Last Exam (2025), a set of very difficult questions written by experts across many fields, and ARC-AGI-2 (2025), visual puzzles that are easy for people but hard for AI. [19, 20]

One of the most informative measures comes from METR, a non-profit research group. Rather than counting right answers, it asks: how long a task, measured by how long it takes a skilled human, can an AI agent complete? In March 2025 it reported that this "time horizon", for tasks the agent completes half the time, had doubled about every seven months since 2019. Leading models at the time could reliably do some software tasks that took people about an hour. In January 2026 METR's updated measurements suggested that since 2023 the doubling had been faster, roughly every four months, though it cautioned that estimates for the longest tasks are uncertain. [21, 22]

Exponential trends are powerful, and treacherous to extrapolate. The tasks in such studies are mostly self-contained software tasks with clear success criteria, which suit AI better than messy real-world work. Trends can slow as easy gains run out, or speed up as AI helps build better AI. Section 13 looks at how the measurement works.

9. Pictures, Video and Voice

Image generators (Part 6) became photorealistic, and could follow detailed instructions and render text correctly. Video followed. In February 2024 OpenAI previewed Sora, which produced minute-long videos from text descriptions; in May 2025 Google's Veo 3 generated video with matching sound and speech. AI voices became nearly indistinguishable from human ones, and AI music generators could produce complete songs. [23]

These tools opened new creative possibilities, and new dangers. Deepfake voices were used to impersonate company executives and family members in scams. Fake images and videos circulated during elections and disasters. Non-consensual fake intimate images became a serious form of abuse, prompting new laws in several countries. Companies began adding invisible watermarks and "content credentials" to AI-generated media, but these can be removed, and detecting fakes reliably remains an unsolved problem.

10. AI in Everyday Life and Work

Hundreds of millions of people now use AI assistants for writing, learning, searching, coding and advice. What does it actually do to productivity? The evidence so far is mixed, and depends heavily on the task and the person. [1]

  • A study of over 5,000 customer-support agents found that an AI assistant raised the number of problems resolved per hour by 14 per cent on average, and by 34 per cent for the least experienced workers. [24]
  • In an experiment with management consultants, those using GPT-4 completed tasks within the AI's abilities faster and with higher-quality results. But on a task chosen to be just outside its abilities, consultants using AI were less likely to get the right answer than those working without it, because they trusted its confident but wrong output. The researchers called this a "jagged technological frontier". [25]
  • In a 2025 trial by METR, experienced open-source programmers working on their own projects took on average 19 per cent longer when allowed to use AI tools, even though they believed the tools had made them about 20 per cent faster. [26]

The pattern that emerges: AI tends to help most with well-defined tasks, and with people who are less experienced in a field. It can hurt when people trust it outside its competence, and the gains people feel are not always the gains they get. In education, teachers face both opportunity, with patient AI tutors for every student, and difficulty, as essays and homework can be written by AI. Effects on jobs and wages are still emerging, and are a central topic of Part 8.

11. The Cost: Chips, Power and Money

Training and running AI at this scale requires vast amounts of specialised computing. Demand for Nvidia's AI chips made it, in October 2025, the first company ever valued at $5 trillion. In January 2025 OpenAI, SoftBank and Oracle announced Stargate, a plan to invest up to $500 billion in US data centres; Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta each planned to spend tens of billions of dollars a year on AI infrastructure. [27]

All that computing needs electricity. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5 per cent of the world's total, and projected that this would roughly double to about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, with AI the main driver. That is still a small share of global electricity, but growth is concentrated in particular regions, straining local power grids and water supplies, and some new demand is being met by gas-fired power. Technology companies have also signed deals for nuclear and renewable power. [28]

Whether all this investment will pay off is hotly debated. Optimists point to rapidly growing revenues and usage. Sceptics point out that spending has run far ahead of profits, and draw comparisons with the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, when the internet proved transformative even though many companies went bust.

12. What Still Goes Wrong

Hallucination. Models still state false things confidently, though less often than before. A 2025 OpenAI paper argued that a key cause lies in how models are trained and tested: most scoring gives no credit for saying "I don't know", so, like a student guessing on a multiple-choice exam, a model does better on average by guessing confidently. [29]

Sycophancy. Models trained on human approval can learn to tell people what they want to hear. In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back an update to GPT-4o after users found it excessively flattering, even agreeing with harmful ideas; the company said it had relied too much on short-term thumbs-up feedback. [30]

Jaggedness. A model that can solve olympiad maths may still make basic errors in counting, spatial reasoning or common sense, and it often doesn't know which is which. This makes it hard to know when to trust it.

Copyright and consent. Most models were trained on text, images and code from the internet without their creators' permission. Lawsuits followed, including The New York Times against OpenAI and Microsoft (filed December 2023). In 2025 a US judge ruled in a case brought by authors against Anthropic that training on lawfully acquired books could be fair use, but that the company's use of pirated copies was not protected; Anthropic agreed to pay $1.5 billion to settle the authors' claims. Courts in different countries are reaching different conclusions, and the law remains unsettled. [31, 32]

13. How Did They Know? Measuring Progress by Task Length

How did they know? METR's time-horizon method

Benchmark scores are hard to compare over time: a test that is too hard tells you nothing about weak models, and one that is too easy tells you nothing about strong ones. METR wanted a single scale that could compare models from 2019 to today. [21]

  1. Collect varied tasks: about 170 tasks in software engineering, cybersecurity and machine learning, from ones taking seconds to ones taking many hours.
  2. Time real experts: skilled professionals attempted the tasks, and METR recorded how long each took them. This gives each task a "length" in human time.
  3. Test the AI agents: each model, set up as an agent with tools, attempted every task several times.
  4. Fit a curve for each model: models succeed on short tasks and fail on long ones. The task length at which a model succeeds half the time is its 50% time horizon.
  5. Plot horizons against release dates: on a logarithmic scale the points from 2019 to 2025 fell close to a straight line, meaning steady exponential growth, doubling about every seven months.

METR was careful about the limits. Horizons at 80 per cent success are several times shorter than at 50 per cent. Its tasks are well defined, self-contained and automatically checkable, unlike much real work, which involves vague goals, other people and long-term context. And its own trial of experienced programmers (section 10) showed that impressive scores do not automatically translate into faster work. A single trend line is useful evidence, not a law of nature.

14. Legend and Record: Did GPT-4 Score in the Top 10% on the Bar Exam?

Legend and Record: a headline number, re-examined

The legend: when GPT-4 was released in March 2023, OpenAI's technical report said it passed a simulated US bar exam (the test lawyers must pass to practise) with a score around the top 10 per cent of test takers. The claim was repeated everywhere as proof that AI could now do lawyers' work. [3, 33, 34]

The record: GPT-4 really did pass, a remarkable result. But in 2024 Eric Martínez, then a researcher at MIT, re-examined the "top 10 per cent" figure. The percentile had been estimated against people taking a February exam, a group that includes many repeat takers who had failed before, and who tend to score lower. Compared with everyone taking the July exam, he estimated GPT-4 would rank below the 69th percentile overall, and around the 48th percentile on the written essay sections; compared only with first-time takers, its standing was lower still.

Why it matters: GPT-4 was genuinely impressive, and later models have done better. But headline comparisons with humans depend on details: which test, which group of humans, which parts, and how the answers were graded. And passing an exam is not the same as doing the job: lawyers also interview clients, judge what matters and take responsibility. The same caution applies to every "beats humans" headline.

15. Philosophy Corner: Is This Already AGI?

Philosophy Corner: what would count as general intelligence?

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) usually means AI that can do most intellectual tasks as well as a capable human, across many fields, rather than being expert in just one. The leading labs say that building it is their goal. But there is no agreed definition, and so no agreed answer to whether we are close. [35, 20]

Some say it is nearly here: today's models write, code, reason, pass professional exams and hold conversations on almost any subject, in dozens of languages. By the standards of the Dartmouth proposal of 1955 (Part 2), much of the original dream has been met. Some argue that if you had shown today's systems to anyone in 2015, they would have called them AGI.

Some say it is still far off: models are jagged, unreliable and easily confused by tasks that are simple for people. They learn far less efficiently than a child, who learns language from a tiny fraction of the text a model sees. They struggle to keep learning after training, to plan over long periods, and to act in the physical world. Tests like ARC-AGI were designed to show this gap.

Perhaps the question is badly posed: a Google DeepMind group proposed treating generality and performance as separate scales, with "levels" of AGI, rather than a single finish line. Others argue that what matters is not a label but specific capabilities, such as whether AI can do a particular job, carry out scientific research, or improve itself, because those are what will change the world. Turing, who replaced "Can machines think?" with a practical test (Part 2), might have agreed.
