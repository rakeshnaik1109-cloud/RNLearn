Network Architectures: Convolutional, Recurrent and Transformer Networks, and Autoencoders

The same neurons can be wired in very different ways. Arrange them to match the structure of images, sequences or graphs, and a network learns far more from far less.

You need: Part 6 (neurons, backpropagation, vanishing gradients) and Part 8 (normalisation, skip connections).

1. Why Architecture Matters

The networks of Parts 6–8 were fully connected: every unit in one layer is linked to every unit in the next. In principle, such a network can approximate any function (Part 6). In practice, the most successful networks are arranged to match the structure of their data. Images have nearby pixels that belong together and objects that can appear anywhere; sentences unfold in order, and a word’s meaning depends on others far away. An architecture builds such assumptions, an inductive bias, into the network, so that it needs fewer weights and less data to learn.

This part tours the main families: convolutional networks for grids such as images, recurrent networks and transformers for sequences, and autoencoders for learning compact representations. [1, 2]

3. The Limits of Full Connection

A colour photograph of 224 × 224 pixels has 150,528 numbers. Connecting each to just 1,000 hidden units takes over 150 million weights in the first layer alone. Worse, such a layer has no notion that neighbouring pixels are related, or that a cat in the top-left corner is the same as a cat in the bottom-right: it would have to learn every object separately at every position, from examples at every position. Sequences pose similar problems: a fully connected network needs a fixed input length, and has no built-in sense of order.

4. Convolution

In 1962 David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel found that neurons in the cat’s visual cortex respond to simple features, such as an edge at a particular angle, within a small patch of the visual field, their receptive field. [3] Kunihiko Fukushima’s neocognitron of 1980 copied this layered arrangement of local feature detectors, [4] and in 1989 Yann LeCun and colleagues trained such a network by backpropagation to read handwritten zip codes. [5]

The key operation is convolution. A small grid of weights, a filter or kernel, slides over the image; at each position, it multiplies the pixels beneath it by its weights and adds them up. The result is a feature map showing where the filter’s pattern occurs. Because the same filter is used at every position (weight sharing), a pattern learned in one place is recognised everywhere, and the number of weights depends on the filter size, not the image size.

A convolutional layer applies many filters at once, say 64, each producing its own feature map, and the next layer’s filters combine all 64 maps. Early layers end up detecting edges and colours; deeper layers combine them into textures, parts and whole objects. Hand-designed filters like those in Figure 3 were the basis of classical image processing; a convolutional network learns its filters from data.

5. Pooling, Depth and Receptive Fields

Each 3 × 3 filter sees only a tiny patch. Stacking layers widens the view: a unit in the second layer sees 5 × 5 input pixels, in the third 7 × 7. Pooling layers, which keep only the maximum or average of each small block, and convolutions with a stride of 2, which skip every other position, halve the size of the feature maps, so the receptive field grows much faster and computation shrinks.

With width-3 filters and stride 1, three layers see 7 inputs and six layers 13. With stride 2, three layers already see 15 inputs and six layers 127. LeCun’s LeNet-5 of 1998 alternated convolution and pooling to read cheques; [6] in 2012, AlexNet, a much deeper version trained on two graphics processors, won the ImageNet challenge with a top-5 error of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the next entry, and set off the deep-learning boom. [7] Later networks grew deeper with small 3 × 3 filters, [8] and residual networks with skip connections (Part 8) reached 152 layers. [9]

The maths: receptive field size

For layers l = 1, …, L with filter widths kl and strides sl, the receptive field r of a unit in the last layer is

$$r = 1 + \sum_{l=1}^{L} (k_l - 1)\prod_{i=1}^{l-1} s_i .$$

With k = 3 and s = 2 throughout, r = 2L+1 − 1: it doubles with every layer.

In code: a small convolutional network in PyTorch 
import torch
from torch import nn

cnn = nn.Sequential(                       # input: 3 × 32 × 32 colour image
    nn.Conv2d(3, 32, kernel_size=3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(32), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),                       # → 32 × 16 × 16
    nn.Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(64), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),                       # → 64 × 8 × 8
    nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1), nn.Flatten(), # → 64 numbers
    nn.Linear(64, 10))                     # → scores for 10 classes
print(sum(p.numel() for p in cnn.parameters()))   # 20,234 parameters

6. Recurrent Networks

For sequences, such as text, speech or sensor readings, a recurrent neural network (RNN) reads one element at a time and carries a hidden state from each step to the next: ht = tanh(W ht−1 + U xt + b). The same weights are used at every step, the time-wise equivalent of weight sharing, so the network handles sequences of any length. Jeffrey Elman showed in 1990 how such networks discover structure, such as word boundaries, in streams of input. [10] They are trained by unrolling the loop over time and applying backpropagation, “backpropagation through time”.

Unrolled, a recurrent network is a very deep network, and it suffers the vanishing gradients of Part 6: the influence of early inputs shrinks exponentially with every step. Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber’s long short-term memory (LSTM) of 1997 adds a memory cell that is updated by addition and controlled by gates, small sigmoid units that decide what to forget, what to write and what to read. [11] Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues’ gated recurrent unit (GRU) of 2014 is a simpler variant. [12]

With a recurrent weight of 1, the simple RNN’s sensitivity falls to about 3 × 10−4 after 20 steps and about 10−8 after 50. Raising the weight does not help: the tanh saturates and its slope collapses. An LSTM cell with a forget gate of 0.99 keeps 61% of its sensitivity after 50 steps. LSTMs powered speech recognition and machine translation for much of the 2010s; in 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le translated whole sentences with one LSTM that read the source and another that wrote the translation. [13]

7. Attention

Sutskever’s translator had to squeeze the meaning of a whole sentence into one fixed-length vector before writing a word. In 2015 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio let the writer look back at all the source words instead, weighting each by its relevance to the word being written: attention. [14]

Attention is a soft lookup. Each word offers a key (what it contains) and a value (what it passes on); the word doing the looking presents a query (what it needs). Scores are dot products of the query with every key, a softmax (Part 7) turns them into weights that sum to 1, and the output is the weighted average of the values.

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)\,V$$

Dividing by √d, where d is the length of the vectors, keeps the scores from growing with d and pushing the softmax into saturation. Unlike a recurrent network, attention connects any two words directly, however far apart, and all the scores can be computed at once in parallel.

8. Transformers

In 2017 Ashish Vaswani and colleagues at Google showed that attention alone, without any recurrence, gave better translations and trained much faster: “Attention is all you need”. [15] Their transformer stacks identical blocks, each with two parts:

  • Multi-head self-attention: every word attends to every other word in the same sequence, with several attention “heads” in parallel, each using its own learned maps from word vectors to queries, keys and values, so that different heads can track different relationships.
  • A feed-forward network applied to each word separately, usually with a GELU or SwiGLU activation (Part 6).

Each part is wrapped in a skip connection and layer normalisation (Part 8). Because attention treats its inputs as an unordered set, the word order is supplied by adding a positional encoding to each word’s vector.

Transformers now dominate language processing: BERT, from Jacob Devlin and colleagues, learns by filling in masked words, [16] and the GPT models learn by predicting the next word, reaching 175 billion parameters with GPT-3 in 2020. [17] In 2021 Alexey Dosovitskiy and colleagues cut images into 16 × 16 patches and fed them to a transformer like words; this vision transformer matched the best convolutional networks, but only when pre-trained on very large data sets, a sign of how much the convolutional inductive bias is worth when data are scarce. [18] The price of attention is that every word attends to every other, so the cost grows with the square of the sequence length.

The maths: sinusoidal positional encodings

For position p and dimensions 2i and 2i + 1 of a d-dimensional code:

$$PE_{p,\,2i} = \sin\!\big(p / 10000^{2i/d}\big), \qquad PE_{p,\,2i+1} = \cos\!\big(p / 10000^{2i/d}\big).$$

Each pair of dimensions rotates at its own speed as p increases, so moving k positions is the same rotation wherever it happens, which makes relative positions easy for attention to use. Many recent models instead learn the codes, or rotate queries and keys directly.

9. Autoencoders and Embeddings

An autoencoder is trained to reproduce its input, after forcing it through a narrow middle layer, the bottleneck. To succeed, the encoder must summarise each input in a few numbers that the decoder can expand again, and those numbers become a compact embedding of the data. A linear autoencoder learns the same subspace as principal component analysis; in 2006 Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov showed that deep nonlinear autoencoders compress images far better. [19]

The best straight line leaves a reconstruction error of 0.094; after 2,000 steps the nonlinear autoencoder’s is about 0.004, because its one number records where each point lies along the curve. Embeddings appear throughout deep learning. Word embeddings place words with similar meanings close together; Tomas Mikolov and colleagues found that such vectors even capture analogies, with king − man + woman landing near queen. [20] Every transformer begins by turning tokens into embeddings, and the hidden layers of a trained network are embeddings that can be reused for other tasks.

10. Generative and Graph Networks

  • Variational autoencoders, by Diederik Kingma and Max Welling, make the bottleneck a probability distribution, so that new data can be generated by sampling codes. [21]
  • Generative adversarial networks, by Ian Goodfellow and colleagues, train a generator to fool a discriminator that tries to tell its output from real data. [22]
  • Diffusion models, developed by Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel among others, learn to remove noise step by step, and generate images by starting from pure noise. [23] They power most of today’s image generators.
  • Graph neural networks, such as the graph convolutional network of Thomas Kipf and Max Welling, pass messages between connected nodes, for molecules, social networks or road maps. [24]

In practice, start from a published architecture that works on similar data, and, where possible, from its pre-trained weights: fine-tuning a network trained on millions of examples usually beats training from scratch on thousands.

12. Open Questions

Open questions: does architecture still matter?

The vision transformer suggests that with enough data, a general architecture can learn what a specialised one has built in. [18] Jared Kaplan and colleagues found that the loss of language models falls smoothly, as a power law, with the number of parameters, the amount of data and the computation used, and depends only weakly on details such as depth versus width. [25] Does that mean architecture matters less and less as scale grows? Or does the quadratic cost of attention, the hunger for data, and the difficulty of reasoning over long contexts mean new architectures are still needed? Researchers are actively exploring alternatives, from recurrent models with efficient training to mixtures of experts that activate only part of a network for each input, and the answer is not yet clear.

13. Worked Example: Counting Weights

  1. Fully connected: a 224 × 224 colour image has 150,528 inputs. One layer of 64 hidden units needs 150,528 × 64 = 9.6 million weights.
  2. Convolutional: 64 filters of size 3 × 3 over 3 colour channels need 3 × 3 × 3 × 64 = 1,728 weights, about 5,600 times fewer, and produce 64 full-size feature maps instead of 64 numbers.
  3. Going deeper: a second layer of 64 filters reading 64 maps needs 3 × 3 × 64 × 64 = 36,864 weights, still independent of the image size.
  4. Attention: a transformer layer with vectors of length 512 has four 512 × 512 matrices (queries, keys, values, output), about 1 million weights, whatever the sequence length. But its attention scores form an n × n table: 1 million entries for a sequence of 1,000 tokens, 100 million for 10,000.
  5. Recurrent: an LSTM with 512 hidden units and 512 inputs has 4 gates, each with (512 + 512) × 512 weights plus biases: about 2.1 million, applied again at every step.

15. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “convolutional networks see shapes, as we do”

The myth: because convolutional networks were inspired by the visual cortex and classify objects as well as people do, they recognise objects by their shape, as humans do.

The reality: in 2019 Robert Geirhos and colleagues showed people and networks images in which shape and texture conflicted, such as the outline of a cat filled with the texture of elephant skin. People overwhelmingly went by shape; standard ImageNet-trained networks mostly went by texture, answering “elephant”. Training on images whose textures had been randomised made the networks rely more on shape and become more robust. [26]

Why it matters: a network’s architecture makes some solutions easy to learn, but the data decide which one it actually learns. Similar accuracy does not mean similar reasoning.

16. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Architectures build assumptions about the data into a network, so it learns from fewer weights and examples.
  • Convolutional networks slide shared filters over grids; stacking and downsampling layers widens their receptive field.
  • Recurrent networks carry a state through sequences; LSTMs and GRUs use gates to keep memory and gradients alive.
  • Attention computes a weighted average of values, with weights from a softmax of query–key dot products.
  • Transformers stack multi-head self-attention and feed-forward layers with skip connections, normalisation and positional encodings; their cost grows with the square of the sequence length.
  • Autoencoders learn compact embeddings through a bottleneck; generative models such as VAEs, GANs and diffusion models create new data.
  • Choose the architecture whose built-in assumptions match your data, and start from pre-trained weights when you can.
