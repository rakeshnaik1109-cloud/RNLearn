How Big Is the Earth? From Eratosthenes’ Shadows to Satellites

More than two thousand years ago, using shadows, angles and careful reasoning, people measured the size of a planet they could never see whole. Here is how they did it, and how you can do it too.

1. Measuring the Unmeasurable

How do you measure something you can never see all at once, and could never walk around? That was the problem facing anyone who wanted to know the size of the Earth. Yet more than two thousand years ago, using nothing but shadows, angles and careful reasoning, people worked out the answer remarkably well. [1, 2]

This is the first part of a series about how humanity measured the universe: the Earth, the Moon, the Sun, the speed of light, the distances to the stars, the age of everything, and the size of atoms. Each part tells the story of the measurements, explains the reasoning in plain language, and includes:

  • interactive figures that let you repeat the measurements yourself;
  • a "How Close Did They Get?" table comparing old results with modern values;
  • a "Try It Yourself" experiment you can do at home or at school.

The story of the Earth's size runs from ancient Egypt to the satellites overhead, through a mountain in Punjab, a carriage wheel in France and a chain of church towers in the Netherlands. (Eratosthenes' measurement also appears briefly in Part 3 of How We Came to Know; here we go much deeper.)

3. First, Is the Earth Round?

You can only measure the size of a ball if you know it is a ball. By the 4th century BC, Greek thinkers were convinced the Earth was round. Around 350 BC Aristotle gave the evidence in his book On the Heavens: [3]

  • Eclipses: during an eclipse of the Moon, the edge of the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved. Only a sphere casts a round shadow from every direction.
  • Changing stars: travelling even a short way north or south changes which stars you can see. Some stars visible in Egypt cannot be seen from Greece. This only makes sense if the Earth's surface is curved, and not very big.

Sailors added a third clue: a ship sailing away disappears hull first, its mast last, as if going over a hill. Aristotle even reported that "mathematicians" had estimated the circumference at 400,000 stadia (a stadion was a Greek unit of length, roughly 150 to 200 metres). That would be about 60,000 to 80,000 km, too big, but the right order of size. Nobody knows how it was calculated.

The idea that people before Columbus thought the Earth was flat is a modern myth (section 14). Educated people throughout the ancient and medieval world knew it was round. The real question was: how big?

4. Eratosthenes and the Shadows

The most famous answer came from Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–194 BC), chief librarian of the great Library of Alexandria in Egypt, a scholar so wide-ranging that rivals nicknamed him "Beta": second best at everything. His own book on the subject is lost. Our best account comes from a later Greek writer, Cleomedes, in a short astronomy textbook. [2, 4]

The method was beautifully simple:

  1. At the town of Syene (modern Aswan), far to the south, the noon Sun on the longest day of the year was directly overhead. Upright objects cast no shadow. (Later writers say the Sun shone straight down a deep well there.)
  2. At the same moment in Alexandria, to the north, a vertical pointer did cast a short shadow. Using a bowl-shaped sundial, Eratosthenes found the Sun's rays were tilted by one-fiftieth of a full circle, about 7.2 degrees.
  3. He assumed the Sun is so far away that its rays reach the whole Earth parallel. Then simple geometry shows that the angle of the shadow at Alexandria equals the angle between the two cities measured at the centre of the Earth.
  4. So the distance from Syene to Alexandria is one-fiftieth of the way round the Earth. He took that distance as 5,000 stadia, giving a circumference of 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. (Later writers give 252,000, perhaps rounded to make each degree a neat 700 stadia.)

How accurate was he? It depends on the length of his stadion, which we don't know for sure: scholars have argued for values between about 157 and 185 metres, giving between about 39,000 and 46,000 km. The true figure through the poles is about 40,008 km. He also had some luck. Syene is not exactly on the Tropic of Cancer, not exactly due south of Alexandria, and the true distance between them is about 800 km, which may not match his 5,000 stadia exactly. Some of these errors partly cancelled out. [1, 5]

But the achievement was the method, not the decimal places. Eratosthenes showed that the size of the whole Earth could be found from one angle and one distance, measured without leaving Egypt.

5. Stars and Deserts: Posidonius and the Caliph

About 150 years later, the philosopher Posidonius (c. 135–51 BC) tried a different marker: a star instead of the Sun. The bright star Canopus, he said, just grazed the horizon at the island of Rhodes, but rose to about one-forty-eighth of a circle (7.5 degrees) above the horizon at Alexandria. With the distance between them taken as 5,000 stadia, the circumference came out at 240,000 stadia. The distance across the sea was very uncertain, and so was the result; other writers report a much smaller value of 180,000 stadia attributed to him. [2, 6]

The next great survey was carried out in the 9th century by astronomers working for the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in Baghdad, who founded a famous centre of learning (Part 5 of How We Came to Know). According to later accounts, teams went to a flat plain in northern Mesopotamia. Starting from one spot, one group walked north and another south, measuring the ground with ropes and rods, until the height of the Pole Star had changed by exactly one degree. They found that one degree of the Earth's curve measured about 56⅔ Arabic miles. Since the exact length of their mile is uncertain, so is the exact result, but it implies a circumference of very roughly 40,000 km. [6]

6. Al-Biruni's Mountain

Around the 1020s, the great Persian scholar Abu Rayhan al-Biruni (973–c. 1050) found a way to measure the Earth without travelling at all, from a single mountaintop. He described it in a book on finding the positions of cities. [7, 6]

While travelling in India, at the fort of Nandana near the plains of the Punjab (in present-day Pakistan), he used a mountain overlooking a flat plain:

  1. Measure the mountain's height. He sighted the summit from two points on the plain a known distance apart, and used the angles to work out the height by trigonometry: about 652 cubits, or very roughly 300 metres.
  2. Climb the mountain and measure the dip. From the top, the horizon appears slightly below the true level, because the Earth's surface curves away. He measured this "dip" as 34 minutes of arc, a little over half a degree.
  3. Solve the triangle. The line of sight to the horizon just touches the Earth, so it makes a right angle with the Earth's radius at that point. This right-angled triangle links the mountain's height, the dip angle and the radius.

Al-Biruni's result, a radius of about 3,930 Arabic miles, is usually estimated to be within one or two per cent of the true value, depending on how long his mile was. That close agreement is partly lucky. As Figure 3 shows, an error of one arcminute in the dip, a tiny fraction of a degree, changes the answer by several per cent, and the bending of light by the air near the ground makes the horizon appear higher than it really is. But the method was brilliant, and al-Biruni himself was careful to note its practical difficulties.

7. Counting Wheel Turns

In Europe, interest revived in the 16th century, partly because of long ocean voyages. In 1525 the French physician Jean Fernel measured the Sun's height at noon in Paris, then travelled north until it was exactly one degree lower, which took him to near Amiens. To measure the distance, he counted the turns of his carriage wheel along the road, then corrected for bends and hills. [8]

His result, published in 1528, was equivalent to about 110.6 km per degree, astonishingly close to the true value of about 111 km. Since the road was not straight and the counting was crude, much of that accuracy was luck. To do better, measurers needed a method that didn't depend on rough roads.

8. Snell's Chain of Triangles

The answer was triangulation, first described by the Dutch mathematician Gemma Frisius in 1533. Measuring long distances directly over the ground is slow and error-prone. Measuring angles is much easier and more accurate: you can sight from one tall building to another miles away. [9, 10]

The idea:

  1. Measure one fairly short line on flat ground, the baseline, as accurately as possible, using rods laid end to end.
  2. From each end of the baseline, measure the angles to a distant landmark, such as a church tower. You now know one side and two angles of a triangle, so you can calculate the other sides (using the rule of sines).
  3. Use one of those new sides as the base of the next triangle, and so on, building a chain of triangles across the country.

In 1615–17 Willebrord Snell (Snellius), professor at Leiden and better known for the law of refraction of light, used a chain of 33 triangles between the towns of Alkmaar and Bergen op Zoom, mostly sighting between church towers. He called his book Eratosthenes Batavus, "the Dutch Eratosthenes". His value for one degree was about 107 km, about 3.5 per cent too small, mainly because the angle-measuring instruments of his day were limited. But the method was right, and it became the basis of all accurate surveying for the next 350 years. [10]

In 1635 the English mathematician Richard Norwood took a more direct route: he walked from London to York, measuring the distance with a chain and the Sun's height at each end, and got about 111.9 km per degree, much better. [11]

9. Picard's Telescope Sights

The breakthrough in accuracy came from a new instrument. In 1669–70 the French astronomer Jean Picard, working for the newly founded Paris Academy of Sciences, measured a chain of 13 triangles between Paris and Amiens, close to Fernel's route. His secret was to fit his angle-measuring instruments with telescopes containing fine crosshairs, which let him point at distant targets far more precisely than with open sights. He measured two baselines with wooden rods and checked one against the other. [12]

His result, 57,060 toises (an old French unit) per degree, equals about 111.2 km, within about 0.1 per cent of the modern value. It was so trusted that Isaac Newton used it in the 1680s when testing whether the force that holds the Moon in orbit is the same as the gravity that makes apples fall (Part 6 of this series). The answer, which depended on knowing the Earth's size accurately, was yes. [13]

10. Is the Earth a Perfect Sphere? And Today's Answer

Picard's success raised a new question. Was the Earth really a perfect sphere? Newton predicted from his theory of gravity that a spinning Earth should bulge at the equator and be flattened at the poles. Measurements by the Cassini family in France seemed to show the opposite. Settling the argument took expeditions to the Arctic and to the Andes in the 1730s, and is the story of Part 2. [14]

Today the Earth is measured from space. Satellites, GPS receivers and laser ranging give its size and shape to within centimetres. The standard model used by GPS, called WGS 84, gives an equatorial radius of 6,378.137 km and a polar radius of 6,356.752 km. The average radius is about 6,371 km. The circumference is about 40,075 km around the equator and about 40,008 km around the poles. [15]

That last number is no coincidence: when the metre was defined in the 1790s, it was intended to be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, so the full circle through the poles comes to about 40 million metres. How that definition was made is also part of Part 2.

12. Try It Yourself: Measure the Earth with a Friend

Try it yourself: Eratosthenes' experiment

You can repeat Eratosthenes' measurement with a friend who lives a few hundred kilometres north or south of you. Schools around the world do this every year, often at the March or September equinox.

You need: a straight stick or pencil about 1 metre long (or a smaller one), a spirit level or plumb line (a weight on a string) to make it vertical, a tape measure, a sunny day, and a friend somewhere else doing the same.

  1. Find local noon. This is when the Sun is highest and shadows are shortest. It is usually not 12:00 on the clock; look it up for your town ("solar noon" calculators are online), or measure the shadow every few minutes and use the shortest.
  2. Set up the stick vertically on flat, level ground. Measure its height above the ground, h.
  3. At local noon, measure the shadow length, s.
  4. Work out the Sun's angle from vertical: angle = arctan(s ÷ h). A calculator or phone can do this (use the "tan⁻¹" button, in degrees).
  5. Compare with your friend. Subtract the two angles. Find the north–south distance between your two places (a map or online tool will tell you; if one place is east of the other, use only the north–south part).
  6. Calculate: circumference = 360 ÷ (angle difference) × (north–south distance).

Worked example: in one place a 1.00 m stick casts a 0.700 m shadow (angle 35.0°); 555 km further north, the same stick casts a 0.810 m shadow (angle 39.0°). The difference is 4.0°, so the circumference is 360 ÷ 4.0 × 555 = about 50,000 km. That is 25 per cent too big, which shows how much a few millimetres of shadow matter. Repeat the measurements several times and average them. Getting within 10 per cent of 40,000 km is a very good result.

No friend nearby? At the March or September equinox, the noon Sun is overhead at the equator. Your shadow angle then equals your latitude, so you can do the calculation using the distance from you to the equator.

13. How Did They Know? Measuring Angles Precisely Enough

How did they know? The problem of small angles

Every method in this part comes down to measuring an angle accurately. Why was that so hard, and how did measurers overcome it? [10, 12]

  1. Make the instrument big. On a circle 2 metres across, one degree of arc is about 17 mm long and one arcminute less than 0.3 mm. Astronomers built huge quadrants and sundials so that small angles became visible distances.
  2. Use the right scale. Eratosthenes's bowl-shaped sundial (called a scaphe) had lines drawn inside it; the shadow's position could be read directly as a fraction of a circle.
  3. Sight better. Open sights, like those on a rifle, can only be pointed to within a few arcminutes. Telescopes with crosshairs, used by Picard from the 1660s, improved pointing about tenfold.
  4. Repeat and average. Surveyors measured each angle many times, at different times of day, and averaged the results to reduce random errors.
  5. Check the triangles. The three angles of a flat triangle must add up to 180 degrees. Any difference showed the size of the errors, which could then be shared out.
  6. Use a second baseline. Measuring a second baseline at the far end of a chain, and comparing it with the length calculated through the triangles, showed how much error had built up.

These habits, measuring the same thing in more than one way, estimating your own errors and checking for consistency, are at the heart of good science in every field.

14. Legend and Record: Did Columbus Prove the Earth Was Round?

Legend and Record: the myth of the medieval flat Earth

The legend: before 1492, people believed the Earth was flat and feared sailing off the edge. Columbus, braver and wiser, set out to prove it was round, against the opposition of ignorant churchmen. [16]

The record: educated Europeans had known the Earth was round since ancient Greek times; medieval scholars such as Bede and Thomas Aquinas took it for granted, and textbooks taught it in universities. The real argument with Columbus was about size. His critics, using values close to Eratosthenes', correctly said that Asia was far too far to sail to westwards with the ships and supplies of the day. Columbus used a much smaller figure for the Earth, and a much larger one for the width of Asia, which made Japan seem only a few thousand miles west of Spain. He was wrong, and would have died at sea if an unknown continent had not been in the way.

Where the myth came from: the historian Jeffrey Burton Russell traced the flat-Earth story largely to the 19th century, especially to Washington Irving's romanticised biography of Columbus (1828) and to later writers who wanted to portray the Middle Ages as an age of superstition opposed to science.

Why it matters: the past was not as ignorant as legend suggests. Eratosthenes' measurement had been known for 1,700 years, and on the question of the Earth's size, Columbus's critics were right.

15. Philosophy Corner: Measuring What You Cannot See

Philosophy Corner: every measurement needs a model

Eratosthenes never saw the whole Earth. He measured a shadow and a distance, and reasoned his way to the rest. Is that really "measuring" the Earth? [1]

Figure 2 shows the problem: the same two shadows fit a round Earth with a distant Sun, or a flat Earth with a nearby Sun. The shadows alone don't decide between them. Eratosthenes' result depends on a model: assumptions that the Earth is a sphere, that the Sun is far away, that the two cities lie on the same north–south line.

This is true of almost all measurement, not just ancient ones. A thermometer measures how much a liquid expands, and relies on a model linking expansion to temperature. A GPS receiver measures time delays, and relies on models of satellite orbits and even of relativity. Indirect measurement is not second best; it is how nearly all of science works.

What makes such measurements trustworthy is independent agreement. When shadows, star positions, mountain dips and triangulation, each relying on different assumptions, all give about the same answer, the model behind them becomes very hard to doubt. That is exactly what happened to the round Earth, long before anyone saw it from space. This idea, that agreement between independent methods builds confidence, will return again and again in this series.
