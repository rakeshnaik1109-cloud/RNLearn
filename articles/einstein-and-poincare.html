Einstein and Poincaré: Space, Time and the Role of Convention

A French mathematician argued that geometry and simultaneity are conventions we choose; a Swiss patent clerk showed that time itself depends on motion. Between them they dissolved Newton’s absolute space and time.

1. Space, Time and Convention

Newton’s absolute space and time (Part 6) had been questioned by Leibniz and Mach (Part 9), but they remained the stage on which all of physics played. Around 1900, a clash between Newton’s mechanics and the theory of light forced physicists to rethink what space and time are. Two men led the way. Henri Poincaré, the greatest French mathematician of his age, argued that some of the most basic “facts” of physics, such as the geometry of space and the simultaneity of distant events, are conventions we choose. Albert Einstein, a young clerk in the Swiss patent office, rebuilt physics on two principles and showed that time itself depends on motion. [1, 2]

Their stories ask how much of what we call knowledge of nature is discovered, and how much is a framework we bring to it: Kant’s question (Part 7) in a new form.

3. Henri Poincaré: The Last Universalist

Henri Poincaré was born in Nancy in 1854. Trained as a mining engineer, he became a professor at the Sorbonne and made fundamental contributions to almost every branch of mathematics and mathematical physics. In 1889 he won a prize offered by King Oscar II of Sweden for work on the stability of the Solar System; while correcting an error in his entry, he discovered that the motion of three bodies under gravity can be so sensitive to initial conditions that it is effectively unpredictable, the first glimpse of what is now called chaos. [3] He also wrote best-selling books on the philosophy of science, beginning with Science and Hypothesis (1902), and as a member of the Bureau des Longitudes worked on the practical problem of synchronising clocks across continents by telegraph. [1]

4. The Crisis of the Ether

James Clerk Maxwell’s equations of 1865 showed that light is an electromagnetic wave travelling at a fixed speed, c. A speed relative to what? Physicists assumed a medium, the luminiferous ether, filling space, through which the Earth must be moving. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley built an interferometer sensitive enough to detect the Earth’s motion through the ether by comparing the speed of light in perpendicular directions. They found nothing. [4] George FitzGerald and Hendrik Lorentz proposed that objects moving through the ether contract along their direction of motion by just enough to hide the effect, and Lorentz developed transformations for space and time coordinates that kept Maxwell’s equations unchanged. In 1898 Poincaré pointed out that we have no direct intuition of the simultaneity of distant events: we define it by a convention, for example by exchanging light signals and assuming that light takes the same time each way. [5] By 1904 he was speaking of a “principle of relativity”: no experiment could reveal absolute motion.

5. Albert Einstein: The Patent Clerk

Albert Einstein was born in Ulm in 1879 and grew up in Munich. He chafed at the rote learning of his German school, left it at fifteen, and finished school in Aarau, Switzerland, before studying physics at the Federal Polytechnic in Zurich. Unable to find an academic post, in 1902 he became a technical expert at the patent office in Bern, examining inventions, some of them for synchronising electric clocks. With friends in a reading group they called the “Olympia Academy” he read Hume, Mach and Poincaré’s Science and Hypothesis, which, one of them recalled, held them breathless for weeks. [2, 1]

In 1905, his “miraculous year”, Einstein published papers that explained the photoelectric effect with quanta of light (for which he later received the Nobel Prize), showed how Brownian motion could reveal molecules (Part 9), introduced special relativity, and derived the equivalence of mass and energy, E = mc².

6. Special Relativity

Einstein’s paper “On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies” began from two postulates: the laws of physics are the same for all observers moving uniformly relative to each other (Galileo’s principle of relativity, Part 5, extended to light), and the speed of light in empty space is the same for all such observers. [6] He declared the ether superfluous. The consequences follow with elementary algebra:

  • Simultaneity is relative: events simultaneous for one observer are not for another moving relative to her.
  • Moving clocks run slow by the factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) (time dilation).
  • Moving objects are shortened along their motion by the same factor.
  • Nothing can be accelerated to the speed of light, and mass and energy are equivalent.

Lorentz’s equations reappeared, but now as a consequence of the nature of space and time rather than a mechanical effect of the ether. In the same months, Poincaré independently completed the mathematics, named the Lorentz transformations and showed that they form a group; but he kept the ether as a reference, even if undetectable. Historians still debate how much credit each deserves; Einstein did not cite Poincaré, and the two met only once, at a conference in 1911. [7, 1]

7. The Relativity of Simultaneity and Spacetime

Einstein illustrated the relativity of simultaneity with a train. Lightning strikes both ends; an observer midway on the platform sees both flashes at once. An observer midway on the moving train rushes towards one flash and away from the other, sees them at different times, and, knowing that light moves at the same speed for her, concludes they were not simultaneous. Neither observer is wrong; “now” for distant events depends on one’s state of motion. Galison has argued that Einstein’s concrete thinking about clocks and light signals echoed the practical problems of clock synchronisation he saw at the patent office, and that Poincaré met them at the Bureau des Longitudes. [1]

In 1908 Einstein’s former teacher Hermann Minkowski recast relativity geometrically: “Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality.” [8] In four-dimensional spacetime, different observers simply slice it differently into space and time.

8. Testing Relativity

Special relativity is among the best-tested theories in physics. Muons created by cosmic rays high in the atmosphere, which would decay long before reaching the ground at their speed, arrive in large numbers because their clocks run slow. Particle accelerators routinely boost particles to γ of thousands. In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew atomic clocks around the world on commercial airliners and found time differences matching the combined predictions of special and general relativity. [9] Every satellite navigation system now builds relativity into its clocks. [10]

9. Poincaré’s Conventionalism

Poincaré’s philosophy, set out in Science and Hypothesis, holds that some principles of science are neither empirical facts nor Kant’s synthetic a priori truths, but conventions: free choices guided by convenience and simplicity. His most famous example is geometry. Imagine a disc-shaped world, hot at the centre and cold at the rim, in which all objects shrink as they cool. Its inhabitants, using rulers that shrink with them, would find that their shortest paths form triangles with angles adding to less than 180°: a non-Euclidean geometry. But they could equally keep Euclid’s geometry and postulate a universal force that shrinks everything. No experiment can decide between geometry-plus-physics combinations; we choose the simplest. Poincaré was sure that Euclidean geometry would always remain the most convenient. [11]

10. General Relativity: Gravity as Geometry

Special relativity left out gravity. Einstein’s key insight came in 1907: a person falling freely feels no weight, so gravity and acceleration are locally indistinguishable (the equivalence principle). After eight years of struggle, learning the mathematics of curved spaces from his friend Marcel Grossmann, he completed general relativity in November 1915. [12] Matter and energy curve spacetime; free bodies follow the straightest possible paths in it, which we perceive as motion under gravity. The theory explained a long-standing anomaly, the extra 43 seconds of arc per century in the advance of Mercury’s perihelion, and predicted that starlight passing the Sun should bend by 1.75 seconds of arc, twice the value naive Newtonian reasoning gives.

Here Einstein, in effect, answered Poincaré. He chose a non-Euclidean geometry for physical space because, combined with the simplest physical laws, it gave the best account of the world. In his 1921 lecture “Geometry and Experience” he argued that once we specify how rigid rods and clocks behave, geometry becomes an empirical science, though he granted that Poincaré was right “sub specie aeterni” (in principle). (Part 2 quoted the same lecture.)

11. 1919 and After

On 29 May 1919, expeditions led by Arthur Eddington and Andrew Crommelin photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun from the island of Príncipe and from Sobral in Brazil. In November the Astronomer Royal, Frank Dyson, announced that the deflection agreed with Einstein rather than with Newton, and Einstein became world-famous overnight. [13] The measurements were difficult, and some historians have questioned how the data were selected, but later eclipses and radio measurements confirmed the prediction with high precision. General relativity went on to predict black holes, the expanding universe, gravitational lensing and gravitational waves, detected directly by LIGO in 2015 from two black holes merging over a billion light years away. [14] Poincaré died in 1912 and did not see any of this; Einstein died in 1955, still seeking a unified theory.

12. Two Ways of Doing Physics

Poincaré was cautious and mathematical: he saw the principle of relativity as an empirical generalisation that might one day fail, and preferred to keep the ether as a convenient framework. Einstein was bold and physical: he elevated the principle to a postulate, threw away the unobservable ether in Mach’s spirit (Part 9), and followed the consequences wherever they led, even when they overturned intuition about time. Both styles matter in science. Einstein himself said that “the supreme task of the physicist is to arrive at those universal elementary laws from which the cosmos can be built up by pure deduction”, but that there is no logical path to these laws, “only intuition, resting on sympathetic understanding of experience”. [2]

13. What Changed

Relativity did not make everything relative. It replaced absolute space and absolute time with an absolute spacetime and an invariant speed of light; the laws of physics are the same for everyone. What became relative were quantities, such as simultaneity, length and duration, that had seemed to be observer-independent facts. It also showed that a theory as successful as Newton’s could be superseded: Newtonian mechanics survives as an excellent approximation at low speeds and weak gravity, but not as the final truth, a lesson Popper and Kuhn would draw on (Part 12).

14. Try It Yourself: Relativity Without a Rocket

Try it yourself: think like Einstein and Poincaré
  1. Time dilation: at what speed does a moving clock tick at half the rate of one at rest? (Solve γ = 2.)
  2. Synchronising clocks: how would you synchronise two clocks at opposite ends of a long field using only a torch and a mirror? What must you assume about the light’s speed each way?
  3. Curved space: draw a large triangle on a balloon, measure its angles, and compare with a triangle on a saddle-shaped crisp. Which adds to more, which to less, than 180°?
  4. GPS: roughly how far does light travel in 38 microseconds? Why would uncorrected satellite clocks make your map useless within a day?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: “Einstein failed mathematics”

The legend: Einstein was a poor student who failed mathematics at school.

The record: Einstein excelled at mathematics, teaching himself geometry and calculus by about fifteen. When shown a newspaper claim in 1935 that he had failed mathematics, he replied that he had mastered differential and integral calculus before he was fifteen. [2] He did fail the general part of the entrance examination for the Zurich Polytechnic at sixteen, two years younger than usual, while scoring highly in mathematics and physics, and he disliked rote learning. The myth may have been fed by his Swiss school in Aarau, where 6 was the top grade, the reverse of some other systems.

The grain of truth: Einstein clashed with authoritarian teachers and did not shine in subjects that bored him. But the comforting story that a genius can fail mathematics is not his.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: found or chosen?

Poincaré’s conventionalism says that some parts of physics are chosen rather than discovered, and that experiment tests only whole packages of geometry plus physics. The philosopher Pierre Duhem made a related point: no hypothesis is ever tested alone, since predictions always depend on auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction can be blamed on any of them. W. V. O. Quine later radicalised this into the thesis that our whole web of beliefs faces experience together (Part 12).

Einstein accepted the logic but drew the opposite practical conclusion: choose the package that makes the laws of nature simplest, and treat its geometry as real. General relativity’s predictions, from black holes to gravitational waves, suggest that curved spacetime is more than a convenient fiction.

Questions to think about: Is the one-way speed of light a fact or a convention? If two combinations of geometry and physics always predict the same observations, does it make sense to ask which is true?
