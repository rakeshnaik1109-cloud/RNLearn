A teaspoon of oil on a pond, specks jiggling under a microscope, and drops of oil hovering between charged plates: how the size of molecules, the number of atoms and the charge of an electron were measured.

1. How Big Is an Atom?

This series began with Eratosthenes measuring the Earth with a shadow and a stick, and has climbed the ladder of distances to the edge of the observable universe. This final part goes the other way, down to sizes far too small to see. How big is a molecule? How many atoms are in a spoonful of water? How much electric charge does a single electron carry?

For most of the nineteenth century, atoms were a useful hypothesis that many respected scientists refused to believe in. The questions were settled between 1890 and 1913 by a remarkable set of measurements, several of which you can repeat at home, starting with a teaspoon of oil. [1]

3. Atoms as an Idea

Greek philosophers such as Democritus argued that matter must be made of tiny indivisible pieces, but they had no way to test it. In the early nineteenth century chemistry gave the idea substance. John Dalton proposed in 1808 that each element consists of identical atoms of a particular weight, which explained why elements combine in fixed proportions. In 1811 Amedeo Avogadro suggested that equal volumes of different gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. The number of molecules in a standard amount of a substance (a mole), now called Avogadro’s number, became central to chemistry, but nobody knew its value.

Chemists could work out the relative weights of atoms without knowing how big or how many they were. Some leading scientists, such as Ernst Mach and Wilhelm Ostwald, argued that atoms were a convenient fiction: if they could never be observed, science should do without them. To answer them, someone had to measure an atom.

4. Franklin’s Teaspoon of Oil

Benjamin Franklin had read in Pliny that sailors calmed waves by pouring oil on the sea, and he carried a little oil in the hollow top of his cane to try it. One windy day in 1773, at a pond on Clapham Common near London, he poured out “not more than a Tea Spoonful” of oil. It spread rapidly across the water, “making all that Quarter of the Pond, perhaps half an Acre, as smooth as a Looking Glass”. [2]

Franklin was interested in calming waves, not in molecules, and he did not take the next step. But his numbers contain a measurement. A teaspoon, about 2 millilitres, spread over half an acre, about 2,000 square metres, makes a film only about one millionth of a millimetre thick: a nanometre. Nothing can be thinner than a single layer of molecules, so oil molecules must be no larger than this.

5. Rayleigh and Pockels: One Molecule Thick

More than a century later, in 1890, Lord Rayleigh did the calculation carefully. He measured how much olive oil was needed to stop the lively dancing of fragments of camphor on water, which oil films suppress, and found that the effect began when the film was about one or two millionths of a millimetre thick. [3]

At the same time, Agnes Pockels, a self-taught scientist working in her kitchen in Brunswick, Germany, had built a simple trough with a sliding barrier to measure the surface tension of water as films on it were compressed. Since women were then barred from German universities, she sent her results to Rayleigh, who forwarded them to Nature in 1891. [4] Her method, developed further by Irving Langmuir, showed that the films are a single layer of molecules standing on end. The thickness of such a film is the length of one molecule.

6. Loschmidt Counts the Molecules in a Gas

A different route came from the physics of gases. By the 1860s, James Clerk Maxwell and Rudolf Clausius had explained gas pressure and viscosity as the effects of countless molecules colliding. The theory related the viscosity of a gas to the average distance a molecule travels between collisions, which in turn depends on the size of the molecules. In 1865 Josef Loschmidt, in Vienna, combined this with the density of liquefied gases, in which the molecules are packed together, to estimate that a molecule of air is about a millionth of a millimetre across. [5] His estimate was too large by a factor of about three, but it was the first sound measurement of the size of a molecule, and it implied that a cubic centimetre of gas contains billions of billions of molecules.

7. Brownian Motion and Einstein

In 1827 the Scottish botanist Robert Brown watched tiny particles released from pollen grains suspended in water under his microscope. They jiggled restlessly and never stopped. He found the same motion in particles of dust, glass and even fragments of the Sphinx, so it had nothing to do with life. For decades nobody could explain it convincingly.

In 1905, the same year as his theory of relativity, Albert Einstein published a theory of this motion. [6] A small particle in a liquid is bombarded on all sides by molecules. At any instant, slightly more hit it from one side than the other, and it is nudged about at random. Einstein showed that the particle’s average squared distance from its starting point grows in proportion to time, at a rate that depends on the temperature, the viscosity of the liquid, the size of the particle, and Avogadro’s number. Measuring the jiggling of visible particles would count invisible molecules. Marian Smoluchowski reached similar results independently in 1906.

8. Perrin Makes Atoms Real

Jean Perrin, in Paris, set out to test Einstein’s theory. He made tiny spheres of gamboge, a yellow resin, and painstakingly separated them by centrifuging until he had particles all of nearly the same size, less than a thousandth of a millimetre across. Watching them through a microscope, he recorded their positions every 30 seconds and drew their zigzag paths. He also counted how the particles thinned out with height in a still liquid, like a miniature atmosphere. [7]

Both methods gave Avogadro’s number close to 7 × 1023. In his book Les Atomes (1913), Perrin listed thirteen different ways of finding it, from the blue of the sky to radioactivity and the spectrum of glowing bodies, which all gave values between about 6 and 7.5 × 1023. [8] Such agreement among unrelated phenomena was hard to dismiss. Ostwald accepted the reality of atoms in 1909, and Perrin received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1926. The modern value is 6.022 × 1023: 18 grams of water, about a tablespoon, contains that many molecules.

9. Millikan Weighs the Electron’s Charge

In 1897 J. J. Thomson had shown that cathode rays consist of tiny charged particles, electrons, and measured the ratio of their charge to their mass, but not the charge itself. At the University of Chicago from 1909, Robert Millikan, with his graduate student Harvey Fletcher, measured it directly. They sprayed a fine mist of oil drops between two horizontal metal plates. A drop picks up a few electrons by friction or from X-rays. With the plates connected to a battery of several thousand volts, the electric force on a charged drop can balance its weight, and the drop hovers. [9]

The charges Millikan measured were always whole multiples of one value, which he published in 1913 as 4.774 × 10−10 electrostatic units, or 1.592 × 10−19 coulombs. Charge comes in indivisible units: the charge of one electron. Combined with the Faraday constant, the charge needed to deposit a mole of atoms by electrolysis, it gave Avogadro’s number with far better precision than before. His value was about 0.6% too low, mainly because he used an inaccurate value for the viscosity of air. Millikan received the Nobel Prize in 1923.

10. Seeing Atoms

After 1912, when Max von Laue showed that crystals diffract X-rays, William Henry and Lawrence Bragg used X-rays to measure the spacing of atoms in crystals directly: about 0.28 nanometres between neighbouring atoms in table salt. X-ray crystallography went on to reveal the structures of thousands of substances, from minerals to DNA.

Seeing individual atoms took until 1981, when Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM in Zurich built the scanning tunnelling microscope. A needle with a tip one atom wide moves across a surface a nanometre away, and a tiny electric current that leaks across the gap changes sharply with distance, mapping the surface atom by atom. They shared the 1986 Nobel Prize. In 1989 Don Eigler and Erhard Schweizer used the same kind of instrument to push 35 xenon atoms across a nickel surface to spell out “IBM”. [10] The atoms that Mach doubted could now be seen and moved one at a time.

11. Counting Atoms to Define the Kilogram

From 1889 the kilogram was defined as the mass of a single cylinder of platinum and iridium, the International Prototype Kilogram, kept in a vault at Sèvres near Paris. Comparisons with its official copies over a century showed differences of up to about 50 micrograms; nobody could tell whether the prototype or the copies were changing, because by definition the prototype always weighed exactly one kilogram.

On 20 May 2019 the International System of Units was redefined in terms of fixed constants of nature. The kilogram is now defined by fixing the value of Planck’s constant, and at the same time Avogadro’s number was fixed at exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 and the electron’s charge at exactly 1.602176634 × 10−19 coulombs. [11] One of the experiments that made this possible counted atoms: an international team made nearly perfect spheres of silicon-28, measured their diameters and the spacing of their atoms with lasers and X-rays, and so counted the atoms in each sphere. [12] Franklin’s pond and Perrin’s jiggling grains had become the foundation of the world’s system of measurement.

12. From Atoms to Nuclei, and the Whole Ladder

Atoms themselves turned out to be mostly empty. In 1911 Ernest Rutherford, interpreting experiments by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden in which alpha particles occasionally bounced back from thin gold foil, concluded that nearly all of an atom’s mass is in a tiny nucleus, about 100,000 times smaller than the atom. [13] If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be a pea at the centre. Particle accelerators today probe distances a thousand times smaller still.

Looking back over the twelve parts of this series, the measured universe now stretches from the size of a proton, about 10−15 metres, to the observable universe, about 1027 metres across: more than forty factors of ten. Every step was reached by a chain of reasoning from something closer at hand: a shadow in Egypt, a pendulum, a transit of Venus, a pulsating star, a teaspoon of oil. None of these numbers was handed down; each was argued for, checked and corrected.

14. Try It Yourself: An Oil Film

Try it yourself: measure a molecule in the kitchen

You need a large shallow tray or basin, water, a fine powder (talc, chalk dust or ground black pepper), a dropper, and a little cooking oil. Adult supervision is advised for any solvent.

  1. Fill the tray with water and let it settle. Sprinkle a very light, even layer of powder over the surface.
  2. Count how many drops from your dropper fill 1 mL, to find the volume of one drop (often about 0.02 mL).
  3. A whole drop of oil makes a patch far too large for a tray, so dilute it. If you can obtain oleic acid dissolved in alcohol (a common school solution, about 1 part in 200), use that; the alcohol dissolves into the water and leaves only the oil.
  4. Let one drop fall onto the centre of the water. The film pushes the powder outwards into a roughly circular patch. Measure its diameter quickly, in several directions, and average.
  5. The thickness of the film is the volume of oil (drop volume ÷ dilution) divided by the area of the patch. You should get a few nanometres: close to the length of an oleic acid molecule, about 2 nm.

15. How Did They Know? Weighing a Drop You Cannot Weigh

How did they know? Stokes’s law and the falling drop

To calculate a drop’s charge from the balancing voltage, Millikan needed its mass, but a drop a thousandth of a millimetre across cannot be put on a balance. He found it from how fast the drop fell with the plates switched off. A tiny drop in air quickly reaches a steady speed at which air resistance balances its weight. George Stokes had shown in 1851 that the resistance on a small sphere is 6πηav, where η is the viscosity of air, a the radius and v the speed. Setting this equal to the weight, (4/3)πa³ρg, gives the radius, and so the mass, from the falling speed alone. [9]

Millikan timed each drop with a stopwatch as it fell between lines on a telescope eyepiece, then switched on the field and timed it rising, over and over, sometimes for hours, as the drop gained or lost electrons. Each change in rising speed corresponded to a change of a whole number of charges. He also corrected Stokes’s law for drops so small that they are comparable to the distance between air molecules.

The weak point was the viscosity of air. Millikan used a value that later proved slightly too low, and since his charge depends on the viscosity to the power 3/2, his result was 0.6% too low. The error came to light in the 1930s, when X-ray methods gave a slightly higher charge, and new measurements of the viscosity of air resolved the discrepancy.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did Millikan cherry-pick his drops?

The legend: Millikan measured every drop and reported them all, as his 1913 paper said: the results were “not a selected group of drops but … all of the drops experimented upon during 60 consecutive days”.

The record: his notebooks, studied by the historian Gerald Holton in the 1970s, show that he excluded many drops from the published set, judging some measurements unreliable for reasons such as convection or uncertain timing. [14] The Viennese physicist Felix Ehrenhaft was meanwhile claiming to see “subelectrons” with smaller charges, and Millikan was keen to avoid giving him ammunition.

The grain of truth on both sides: the physicist and historian David Goodstein argued that the excluded drops would not have changed Millikan’s value, and that experienced experimenters routinely reject runs with known problems. [15] But the sentence in the paper was misleading. Richard Feynman later used the story to make another point: after Millikan, each new measurement of the charge crept up only slightly towards the true value, as if researchers had hesitated to publish results too far from his. [16]

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: when did atoms become real?

No one in 1909 had seen an atom, yet Perrin’s work persuaded almost everyone that atoms exist. His argument was not a single experiment but a convergence: thirteen methods, based on quite different phenomena and assumptions, gave the same number. If atoms were a fiction, this agreement would be an astonishing coincidence. Philosophers have called this kind of reasoning an “argument from coincidence”, and it is a central argument for scientific realism, the view that our best theories describe things that really exist, even if they are unobservable.

Anti-realists reply that theories have often been successful and later abandoned, so success alone does not prove truth. The same question hangs over dark matter (Part 11) today. This series has shown again and again that the most convincing measurements are those confirmed by independent methods: the Earth’s size by shadows and satellites, the Sun’s distance by Venus and radar, the age of the Earth by many radioactive clocks. Is that enough to say that we know?
