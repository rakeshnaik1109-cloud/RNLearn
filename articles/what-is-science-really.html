1. Can You Just Collect Facts? Francis Bacon

Over the last eleven parts we have followed people from tally marks and Moon calendars to Euclid's proofs, Islamic optics, Newton's laws, Lavoisier's oxygen, Darwin's tree of life, Maxwell's fields, Einstein's spacetime, quantum physics and Cantor's infinities. Again and again, careful study replaced guesswork with explanation. Now, at the end, we face a deeper question: what actually is science? [7]

What makes scientific knowledge different from religion, myth, politics or art? Is there one "scientific method", a recipe that reliably produces truth? Or is science a human activity that changes over time, always struggling with bias, mistakes and the opinions of the day?

One of the first people to try to set out the rules of modern science was the English statesman Francis Bacon (1561–1626). In his book Novum Organum ("The New Tool", 1620), he wanted to replace arguing from first principles with induction: building general rules up from many observations. First, though, he warned about four kinds of mental trap, which he called idols of the mind:

  • Idols of the tribe: mistakes everyone tends to make, such as seeing patterns that aren't there, and noticing evidence that agrees with us while ignoring evidence that doesn't.
  • Idols of the cave: personal biases from our own upbringing, education and character.
  • Idols of the marketplace: confusion caused by loose, vague use of words.
  • Idols of the theatre: big philosophical or religious systems accepted without question, like plays we mistake for reality.

To escape these traps, Bacon suggested that the investigator should clear their mind of theories, observe nature without expectations, collect huge tables of facts, and let general laws emerge from them on their own.

The trouble is that observation doesn't work like that. As Charles Darwin later put it: "How odd it is that anyone should not see that all observation must be for or against some view, if it is to be of any service!" Send someone into a field and just say "Observe!", and they won't know where to start. Count the blades of grass? Watch the clouds? Weigh a worm? Without a question or an idea to guide you, observation is just noise.

2. Why Should the Future Be Like the Past? David Hume

About a century later, the Scottish philosopher David Hume (1711–1776) raised a problem that still troubles philosophers: the problem of induction. [6, 7]

Hume split everything we can know into two kinds (often called "Hume's fork"):

  1. Truths of reasoning: maths and logic, such as 2 + 2 = 4 or "all bachelors are unmarried". Denying them leads to a contradiction, so they are certain. But they don't tell us anything about what actually exists in the world.
  2. Matters of fact: claims about the world, such as "the Sun will rise tomorrow" or "bread nourishes us". We learn these from experience, and denying them is never a contradiction: we can perfectly well imagine the Sun not rising tomorrow.

So what justifies our confidence about matters of fact? We expect the future to be like the past: the Sun has risen every morning so far, so we're sure it will rise tomorrow. But why is that a good argument? It isn't a logical certainty, since we can imagine it failing. Can we justify it by experience instead?

Hume showed that this goes round in a circle:

We say that reasoning from past experience is reliable because it has worked in the past. But that is itself reasoning from past experience. We are using induction to prove that induction works.

Induction depends on an assumption we can't prove: that nature stays the same, so the future will resemble the past. Take the old European belief that "all swans are white". For centuries, every swan Europeans saw was white: millions of confirming sightings. Then in 1697 the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh reached Western Australia and saw black swans. One black swan was enough to overturn the belief, however many white ones had been seen.

Hume concluded that our expectations about the world rest not on logic but on habit. The philosopher Immanuel Kant said Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", and for two centuries induction remained what C. D. Broad called "the glory of science and the scandal of philosophy": a tool science can't do without, which no one could justify by logic.

3. Only Testable Statements Make Sense? The Vienna Circle

In the 1920s a group of mathematicians, scientists and philosophers in Vienna, including Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath and Philipp Frank, formed the Vienna Circle and started a movement called logical positivism. [8, 7]

Worried by the rise of extreme nationalism and fed up with what they saw as the empty, high-sounding language of some philosophers (Carnap singled out Martin Heidegger), they wanted to clear nonsense out of philosophy. Their main tool was the verification principle:

A statement has meaning only if it is either true by definition (like statements of logic and maths) or can be checked by observation.

By this test, much of traditional philosophy and religion wasn't false; it was meaningless. Asking "What is the ultimate nature of Being?" was no more sensible than asking "What does Tuesday taste of?"

But the idea ran into a fatal problem with itself. Is the verification principle true by definition? No. Can it be checked by observation? No. So by its own test it was meaningless.

Worse, it seemed to rule out science itself. A law of nature, such as Newton's laws of motion, is meant to hold everywhere, always: an endless number of cases. No one can ever check an endless number of cases. So, strictly applied, the principle would make the laws of physics meaningless too.

4. Science Is What Could Be Proved Wrong: Karl Popper

In 1934 a young Viennese schoolteacher, Karl Popper (1902–1994), published a reply: The Logic of Scientific Discovery. Popper turned the problem of induction upside down. [1]

He agreed with Hume: no number of white swans can prove that all swans are white. Reasoning "if my theory is true, I'll see this; I saw it; so my theory is true" is a logical mistake (it is called affirming the consequent). Lots of other theories might predict the same thing.

But Popper noticed an important imbalance. While no amount of agreeing evidence can prove a general theory true, a single clear failure can prove it false. "If my theory is true, I'll see this; I didn't see it; so my theory is false" is perfectly good logic (logicians call it modus tollens).

From this, Popper drew his famous answer to the question of what makes something science (the "demarcation problem"): not that it can be proved true, but that it could be proved false. He called this falsifiability.

A scientific theory sticks its neck out. It says clearly what should not happen, so that we know in advance what observation would sink it. Popper's examples of pseudoscience were astrology, Freudian psychoanalysis and crude Marxism: their supporters could explain any outcome at all. A Freudian, he said, could explain both a man pushing a child into the water (repression) and a man jumping in to save one (sublimation). A theory that fits every possible event tells us nothing.

Einstein's general relativity was the opposite. It predicted a definite amount of bending of starlight passing the Sun: 1.75 seconds of arc, twice the figure Newton's physics gave. A clear failure at the 1919 eclipse would have counted heavily against it, and Einstein himself wrote that if another of its predictions (a shift in the colour of light from heavy stars) were not found, the theory would have to go. For Popper, science isn't a store of proven truths. It is bold guesses, tested as hard as we can. A theory is never proved; it is only corroborated, which means it has survived our best attempts to knock it down so far.

5. How Science Really Changes: Thomas Kuhn

Popper painted the scientist as a hero, ready to throw away a favourite theory at the first contrary result. In 1962 Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996), a physicist who had become a historian of science, challenged that picture in a short book, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. [2]

Kuhn looked at what scientists had actually done in history, rather than what philosophers said they should do. His central idea was the paradigm: a shared package of theory, instruments, standard examples and assumptions that a whole field takes for granted and works within, often for generations. Ptolemy's astronomy, Newton's mechanics and Lavoisier's chemistry were all paradigms.

Kuhn described science as moving through a repeating cycle:

  1. Before a paradigm: rival schools argue about basics, with no agreement.
  2. Normal science: one paradigm wins, and most research becomes puzzle-solving within it. Scientists don't question the paradigm; they use it to fill in details and measure things more precisely. If an experiment disagrees with the theory, they usually blame the experiment (a loose wire, an impure chemical), not the paradigm.
  3. Anomalies: over time, results pile up that refuse to fit (such as the odd motion of Mercury's orbit, or the radiation problem that led to quantum theory). At first they are put aside as puzzles for later.
  4. Crisis: the anomalies become too many to ignore. Confidence falls, patches multiply, and scientists start arguing about fundamentals again.
  5. Revolution (a "paradigm shift"): a new paradigm appears, often championed by younger scientists or outsiders, and there is a struggle for the field's loyalty. In the end the old paradigm is replaced.

Kuhn quoted the physicist Max Planck's bitter remark: "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it."

6. Can Rival Paradigms Even Be Compared?

Kuhn's most controversial claim was that rival paradigms are incommensurable, which means they have no common measure. You can't simply line them up side by side and score them, like two cars on a test track. [2]

Why not? Because a paradigm shift doesn't just add new facts; it changes how scientists see the world, like the famous drawing that flips between a duck and a rabbit. People working in different paradigms, Kuhn said, in a sense see different things:

  • Looking at a swinging stone on a string, a follower of Aristotle saw a heavy object held back by the string as it tried to reach its natural place at the centre of the Earth.
  • Looking at the same thing, Galileo saw a pendulum: something that, apart from small disturbances, would repeat the same swing forever, with regular properties he could measure.

Kuhn also argued that key words change meaning. In Newton's physics, mass is simply the fixed amount of matter in a body. In Einstein's physics, mass is tied to energy ($E = mc^2$). The words are the same, but the ideas behind them differ, so there is no completely neutral language in which to judge between the two theories.

Critics accused Kuhn of making science irrational, as if a paradigm shift were a religious conversion or a fashion. In later years Kuhn explained that he didn't think science was irrational. Choosing between theories, he said, isn't done by a formula, but it is guided by values scientists share: accuracy, consistency, broad scope, simplicity and the ability to lead to new discoveries.

7. The Core and the Shield: Imre Lakatos

The Hungarian-born philosopher Imre Lakatos (1922–1974) tried to combine the best of Popper and Kuhn: to keep science rational while being faithful to its history. He called his view the methodology of research programmes. [3]

Lakatos pointed out that scientists don't drop a successful theory the moment something goes wrong. In the early 1800s the planet Uranus wasn't moving quite as Newton's gravity predicted. Astronomers didn't declare Newton wrong. Instead, Urbain Le Verrier and John Couch Adams suggested that an unseen planet was pulling on Uranus, worked out where it should be, and in 1846 Neptune was found almost exactly there.

Lakatos pictured a research programme as having two parts:

  1. The hard core: the central ideas that the programme refuses to give up, such as Newton's laws of motion and gravity. Scientists agree not to blame the core when things go wrong.
  2. The protective belt: all the supporting assumptions around the core: about instruments, measurements, unseen objects and so on. When a problem turns up, scientists adjust the belt and leave the core alone.

So how do we tell good science from bad? Lakatos looked at what the adjustments achieve:

  • Progressive programmes: adjustments lead to new, surprising predictions that turn out to be right, as when the idea of an unseen planet led to the discovery of Neptune.
  • Degenerating programmes: adjustments are just excuses, made up after the fact to patch over problems, predicting nothing new. Late attempts to save the "ether" (a supposed medium for light) by giving it new special properties after each failed experiment are an example. (The popular image of medieval astronomers adding "epicycles upon epicycles" to Ptolemy's system is itself largely a myth.)

A programme isn't abandoned because of one failure, but when it keeps degenerating and a rival progressive programme comes along that can explain everything the old one did and predict new things too.

8. "Anything Goes"? Paul Feyerabend

In 1975 the Austrian philosopher Paul Feyerabend (1924–1994), a deliberate provocateur, published Against Method. It was meant to be one half of a debate book with his friend Lakatos, a plan cut short by Lakatos's sudden death in 1974. In it Feyerabend defended what he called epistemological anarchism: the view that there are no fixed rules for how to gain knowledge. [4]

He looked closely at great breakthroughs, especially Galileo's defence of the Sun-centred system after 1610, and argued that Galileo succeeded by breaking the rules philosophers had laid down:

  • When Galileo turned his telescope to the sky, no one could explain how a telescope worked; on Earth it often gave blurry, distorted images; and the accepted physics of the day said a moving Earth would throw everything off its surface.
  • Galileo won people over partly by brilliant writing and persuasion, by patching problems with guesses, and by the beauty of his picture. If he had followed Popper's rule and dropped the theory at the first contrary evidence, the Sun-centred system might never have taken hold.

Feyerabend concluded that there is no single, timeless scientific method. Any rule you try to impose, whether verification, falsification or simplicity, has been broken by great scientists, and sometimes has to be broken for science to advance. He summed this up in a famous and much-misunderstood slogan:

The only principle that does not inhibit progress is: anything goes.

He didn't mean that every claim is as good as any other. He meant that scientists need freedom to use whatever methods, tools and ideas work in a given situation. Science, for him, was less a rulebook than a creative craft.

9. No Idea Is Tested Alone: Duhem and Quine

In 1951 the American philosopher W. V. O. Quine (1908–2000) published one of the most influential philosophy papers of the century, Two Dogmas of Empiricism. In it he developed an idea that had first been put forward by the French physicist Pierre Duhem in 1906, now called the Duhem–Quine thesis. [5]

The idea is simple but powerful: a theory is never tested on its own.

To test a main idea, you always rely on a bundle of other assumptions too: that your telescope's lenses are good, that you've allowed correctly for the air bending the light, that your clocks are accurate, that no stray magnetic field is upsetting the equipment, and so on. The prediction comes from the main idea and all of these together. So if the prediction fails, logic only tells you that something in the bundle is wrong. It doesn't tell you what.

Was the main idea wrong? Or was a clock slightly off, or did a lens warm up and change shape? A scientist can always protect the main idea by blaming one of the supporting assumptions, which is exactly what Lakatos's "protective belt" does.

Quine pictured all our knowledge, from physics and chemistry to geography and even maths and logic, as one connected web of belief that touches experience only at its edges:

The totality of our so-called knowledge or beliefs... is a man-made fabric which impinges on experience only along the edges... A conflict with experience at the periphery occasions readjustments in the interior of the field... there is much latitude of choice as to what statements should be reevaluated in the light of any single contrary experience.

Beliefs near the edge, such as "the needle pointed to 4.2", are easy to change. Beliefs deep in the middle, such as the conservation of energy or basic arithmetic, we protect strongly, because changing them would disturb everything else. But in Quine's view nothing is completely safe: some philosophers have even suggested changing the rules of logic to fit quantum physics.

10. Science as Updating the Odds: Bayes

Many philosophers today describe scientific reasoning in terms of probability, using a rule worked out by the 18th-century English minister Thomas Bayes. On this view, called Bayesianism, learning from evidence means gradually updating how confident you are. [8]

A Bayesian doesn't treat a theory as simply true or false. You give it a level of confidence between 0 (certainly false) and 1 (certainly true): your prior. When new evidence comes in, you update it to a posterior. Bayes' rule says how:

$$P(H|E) = \frac{P(E|H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E|H)\,P(H) + P(E|\text{not }H)\,P(\text{not }H)}$$

In words:

  • $P(H)$ is how confident you were in the theory before the evidence.
  • $P(E|H)$ is how likely the evidence would be if the theory is true.
  • $P(E|\text{not }H)$ is how likely the evidence would be anyway, if the theory is false (the "false alarm" rate).
  • $P(H|E)$ is your new confidence after seeing the evidence.

A quick example: suppose you're 20% confident in a theory. It predicts a result that would happen 90% of the time if the theory is right, but only 5% of the time if it's wrong. You see the result. Bayes' rule says your confidence should jump to about 82%. If instead the result would have happened 50% of the time anyway, your confidence rises only to about 31%.

This helps with some old puzzles:

  1. Why surprising predictions matter most: if a result was expected anyway, seeing it hardly changes your confidence. But if a theory predicts something that would be very unlikely otherwise, such as starlight bending by just the right amount near the Sun in 1919, seeing it should boost your confidence a lot.
  2. The raven puzzle: the philosopher Carl Hempel pointed out that "all ravens are black" means the same, logically, as "everything that isn't black isn't a raven". So does seeing a green apple support "all ravens are black"? Bayesianism says yes, but by an amount so tiny it's practically zero, because there are so many non-black things in the world. Seeing a black raven gives a real boost.

11. Is Science Telling Us What's Really There?

When physicists talk about quarks, curved spacetime, electron waves or dark matter, are these real things out there, or just useful mathematical tools for predicting what our instruments will show? [8, 9]

This is the debate between scientific realism (science describes what is really there, including things we can't see) and anti-realism (science just needs to predict observations correctly).

For realism, the "no miracles" argument: put forward by the philosopher Hilary Putnam in 1975:

Realism is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle.

Modern science makes astonishingly accurate predictions. Quantum electrodynamics predicts the magnetism of the electron in agreement with measurements to better than one part in a billion. GPS satellites have to correct for both of Einstein's effects on time, about 7 millionths of a second a day from their speed and about 45 millionths from weaker gravity up there, or your phone's position would quickly drift off. If curved spacetime weren't real, it would be a miracle that GPS works.

Against realism, the "pessimistic induction": put forward by Larry Laudan in 1981. Laudan pointed out that the history of science is full of theories that were successful and widely accepted, but whose central objects turned out not to exist:

  • The crystal spheres that carried the planets in ancient and medieval astronomy.
  • Phlogiston, the supposed substance of fire in 18th-century chemistry.
  • Caloric, the supposed fluid of heat in Lavoisier's chemistry.
  • The ether, the supposed medium that carried light waves.
  • The "vital force" that was supposed to make living things alive.

If so many successful theories were wrong about what exists, why should we think today's quarks, strings, dark energy and wavefunctions will do any better?

One popular answer is structural realism, put forward by John Worrall. What survives when theories change is not the picture of what things are made of, but the mathematical structure of how they relate. Fresnel's equations for how light reflects and bends survived intact when Maxwell replaced the ether with electromagnetic fields, and survived again when quantum theory described light as photons. On this view, science gets the mathematical structure of reality right, even as its pictures change.

12. When Results Don't Repeat: The Replication Crisis

In the 21st century, science has faced a very practical problem: the replication crisis. [10]

In the 2010s, large projects tried to repeat well-known published studies, including the Reproducibility Project: Psychology, led by Brian Nosek, which repeated 100 psychology studies, and similar efforts in cancer biology. Many results didn't hold up: in the psychology project, fewer than half of the repeated studies found the same effect.

Researchers who study how science works quickly pointed to three main causes:

  1. Studies too small: with only a few participants, a study can't reliably detect a small real effect, and the results it does find are more likely to be flukes.
  2. "P-hacking": analysing data in many different ways (trying different subgroups, dropping awkward data points, choosing the hypothesis after seeing the results) until something passes the usual test of "statistical significance" (a p-value below 0.05). Try enough things and something will pass by chance.
  3. Publication bias: journals prefer exciting positive results and rarely publish "we found nothing". If twenty labs test a drug that doesn't work, the nineteen that find nothing may never publish, while the one that gets a lucky positive result does.

But discovering the problem isn't proof that science is broken. It shows that science can correct itself: scientists found these weaknesses by doing more science. The response has been the open science movement:

  • Pre-registration: researchers publicly record their hypothesis, sample size and analysis plan before collecting any data, so they can't change them afterwards.
  • Registered reports: journals agree to publish a study based on its plan, before the results are known, whether the results turn out positive or negative.
  • Open data and code: researchers share their raw data and analysis code online, so anyone can check and repeat the work.

Science isn't an oracle that hands out truth. It is a demanding, sometimes messy human craft that can correct its own mistakes. Its strength comes not from individual scientists being always right, but from openness that lets others check their work.

13. How Did They Know? Eddington's Eclipse Measurements

How did they know? Telling the data apart from the assumptions

Did Arthur Eddington's famous eclipse expedition of 1919 simply "prove" Einstein right and Newton wrong, as newspapers around the world reported? [8]

Historians of science such as John Earman and Clark Glymour have shown that it is a perfect example of the Duhem–Quine thesis at work. Eddington went to the island of Príncipe, off West Africa, while a second British team, Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson, went to Sobral in northern Brazil.

Conditions were difficult: tropical heat affected the instruments, clouds covered the sky, and equipment shifted. Einstein predicted a bending of 1.75 seconds of arc; Newton's physics gave about half that. The results:

  • Sobral, main telescope: sixteen photographs, giving an average of 0.93 seconds of arc, close to the Newtonian value.
  • Sobral, smaller 4-inch telescope: seven clear photographs, giving 1.98 ± 0.12 seconds of arc, supporting Einstein.
  • Príncipe: mostly clouded out; two usable photographs, giving 1.61 ± 0.30 seconds of arc.

Why did Eddington and the Astronomer Royal, Frank Dyson, set aside the main Sobral results that pointed towards Newton? Not through fraud, but by appealing to a supporting assumption: they argued that the Sun had heated the mirror feeding that telescope, blurring the images and spoiling the measurement. Later checks, including a re-measurement of the Sobral photographs in 1979, have supported their judgement, and many later measurements, especially with radio telescopes, have confirmed Einstein's value precisely.

So Eddington wasn't testing relativity against "raw" data. He was weighing a whole web of beliefs: about mirrors heating up, photographic plates shrinking, the air shimmering, and gravity bending light. Data never speaks entirely for itself; it always speaks through our assumptions.

14. Legend and Record: Do Scientists Really Follow Popper?

Legend and Record: the scientist who drops a theory at the first failure

Popper's ideas are probably the most popular philosophy of science among working scientists. Ask a physicist or biologist what makes their field rigorous, and they will often say: "We try to prove our hypotheses wrong." Yet historians have pointed out that if scientists had always dropped a theory at the first contrary evidence, many of history's most successful theories would have been abandoned at birth. [3, 7]

Copernicus (1543): his moving Earth seemed to contradict everyday experience: we don't feel the Earth moving, dropped stones fall straight down, and no one could detect the tiny shifts in star positions (parallax) that a moving Earth should produce.

Newton (1687): his theory of gravity got the slow turning of the Moon's orbit wrong by a factor of two, a problem that lasted more than sixty years until Alexis Clairaut improved the calculation in 1749. Later, from 1859, it failed to explain a small extra turning of Mercury's orbit. No one abandoned Newton because of these problems.

Darwin (1859): Lord Kelvin's calculations from physics gave the Earth and Sun ages of only tens of millions of years, which seemed far too short for slow evolution (the discovery of radioactivity later removed the problem). Fleeming Jenkin argued that if offspring were a blend of their parents, new variations would be diluted away. Darwin didn't give up; he held on to his theory.

This is Lakatos's point: one problem is usually treated as a reason to work harder, not as a death sentence, and history shows that this kind of persistence is often what lets a good theory mature.

15. Philosophy Corner: Useful Tool or True Picture?

Philosophy Corner: what can we know about what we can't see?

At the end of this twelve-part journey, we come back to the deepest question: does science describe the unseen world as it really is, or does it just give us mathematical tools for predicting what we observe? [9, 8]

The useful-tool view (constructive empiricism): set out by Bas van Fraassen in The Scientific Image (1980). On this view, science aims for theories that are empirically adequate: everything they say about things we can observe (dials, pointers, spectra) is correct. Whether the unobservable things in our theories, like electrons, really exist, we can stay neutral about.

Structural realism: defended by John Worrall, James Ladyman, Steven French and others. It takes a middle path: we shouldn't be confident about what the "stuff" of the world ultimately is, but we can be confident about the mathematical relationships science discovers. When Maxwell's theory replaced Fresnel's, the ether was thrown out, but the mathematical relationships between how light reflects, bends and is polarised survived unchanged.

Seen this way, science is the slow, careful building of mathematical bridges across human experience. We may never know what reality is ultimately made of, but science has given us an increasingly reliable map of how it is put together.
