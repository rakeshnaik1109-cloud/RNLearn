Combinational Circuits: Karnaugh Maps, Multiplexers and Binary Adders

Gates become useful machines when they are combined: circuits that simplify themselves, route data and do arithmetic. The tricks behind them, such as two’s complement, are why one adder can do all of a computer’s sums.

We simplify logic with Karnaugh maps; route data with multiplexers and decoders; add binary numbers with half and full adders; represent negative numbers in two’s complement and detect overflow; and make addition fast with carry lookahead.

1. Building with Gates

Part 1 showed that any truth table can be built from gates. Real designs need more than that: they need circuits that are small, fast and do useful jobs such as choosing between signals and doing arithmetic. Circuits whose outputs depend only on their current inputs, with no memory, are called combinational. They are the arithmetic and routing heart of every processor; memory and timing come in Part 3. [1, 2]

3. Simplifying Logic with Karnaugh Maps

Algebraic simplification works but is error-prone. In 1953 Maurice Karnaugh, at Bell Labs, refined a chart method published by Edward Veitch the year before into a picture of the truth table in which simplifications can be seen. [3, 4] The Karnaugh map arranges the outputs in a grid whose rows and columns follow Gray code order (00, 01, 11, 10), so any two neighbouring cells, including those that wrap around the edges, differ in exactly one variable. A pair of neighbouring 1s therefore does not depend on that variable, and the variable drops out.

The rule: cover all the 1s with as few loops as possible, each loop as large as possible, with sizes that are powers of two (1, 2, 4, 8). A loop of 2ᵏ cells gives a term with k fewer variables. Inputs that can never occur, such as the codes 10–15 in a decimal digit, are don’t-cares, which may be counted as 1 or 0, whichever helps.

The starting example has eight 1s. Written out, it needs eight terms of four variables; the map reveals it is just B̅D̅ + BD: two terms of two variables. For larger problems, Willard Van Orman Quine and Edward McCluskey developed a tabular method that a computer can follow, which this figure uses behind the scenes. [5, 6] Modern chip-design software simplifies circuits with millions of gates using heuristic programs descended from these ideas, such as Espresso. [7]

4. Choosing and Routing: Multiplexers and Decoders

A multiplexer (mux) is a data selector: n select lines choose which of 2ⁿ inputs is passed to the output, like a rotary switch. A decoder does the reverse job: it turns an n-bit number into a signal on exactly one of 2ⁿ output lines. Inside a processor, multiplexers choose which register feeds the arithmetic unit and which result is written back; decoders select a row of memory from an address and turn instruction codes into control signals.

A useful trick: a multiplexer can compute any function of its select inputs if you wire the truth-table outputs to its data inputs. That is exactly how the lookup tables inside programmable chips (FPGAs) implement arbitrary logic.

5. Adding Binary Numbers

In 1937 George Stibitz, a Bell Labs mathematician, built a circuit on his kitchen table from relays, batteries and torch bulbs that added two binary digits; it became known as the Model K and led to Bell Labs’ relay calculators. [8] Binary addition follows the same column method as decimal: 1 + 1 = 10, so write 0 and carry 1.

  • A half adder adds two bits: sum S = A ⊕ B (XOR), carry C = A·B (AND).
  • A full adder also takes a carry in from the previous column: S = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin, and Cout = A·B + Cin·(A ⊕ B).
  • Chaining n full adders, each passing its carry to the next, gives an n-bit ripple-carry adder.

6. Negative Numbers: Two’s Complement

How should a computer store −5? The near-universal answer is two’s complement: in an n-bit number, the leftmost bit has weight −2n−1 instead of +2n−1. In 4 bits, the patterns 0000–0111 mean 0 to 7 and 1000–1111 mean −8 to −1. To negate a number, invert every bit and add 1.

The beauty of the scheme is that the ordinary adder works unchanged for negative numbers, and subtraction needs no new circuit: A − B = A + (B̅ + 1), so the adder simply receives the inverted B with a carry-in of 1. IBM’s influential System/360 computers of 1964 used two’s complement for binary integers, and almost all processors since have done the same. [9]

With a fixed number of bits, results can fall outside the range. For signed numbers this is overflow, detected when the carry into the leftmost bit differs from the carry out of it: in 4 bits, 7 + 1 gives 1000, which reads as −8.

Watch out: overflow is silent

Hardware sets an overflow flag, but many programming languages ignore it, so a signed integer can wrap from its largest value to its most negative without warning. Overflow bugs have crashed rockets and corrupted games; the first launch of the Ariane 5 rocket in 1996 failed after a 64-bit floating-point value was converted to a 16-bit integer that could not hold it. [10]

7. Making Addition Fast: Carry Lookahead

A ripple-carry adder is slow because each bit must wait for the carry from the bit before: the delay grows in proportion to the width. In 1958 Arnold Weinberger and John Smith showed how to compute carries in advance. [11] For each bit, define generate G = A·B (this column creates a carry by itself) and propagate P = A ⊕ B (this column passes on a carry it receives). Then Ci+1 = Gi + Pi·Ci, which can be expanded and combined over groups of bits in a tree. Peter Kogge and Harold Stone’s 1973 parallel-prefix method computes all carries in a time that grows only with the logarithm of the width. [12]

For a 64-bit adder, the idealised count is 128 gate delays for ripple carry against 14 for a parallel-prefix adder, about nine times faster, paid for with more gates and wires. Processors use such fast adders because addition sits on the critical path of almost everything they do.

8. Comparators and the Arithmetic Logic Unit

Other combinational building blocks follow the same pattern. A comparator decides whether A = B (all bits equal: an AND of XNORs) or A > B. An arithmetic logic unit (ALU) combines an adder, logic operations (AND, OR, XOR) and a multiplexer that chooses which result to output according to an operation code. Together with registers (Part 3), the ALU is the core of the processor built in Part 12.

In code: a full adder and ripple-carry adder in Python 
def full_adder(a, b, cin):
    s = a ^ b ^ cin
    cout = (a & b) | (cin & (a ^ b))
    return s, cout

def add(A, B, n=8, subtract=False):
    """Add or subtract two n-bit numbers bit by bit (two's complement)."""
    carry = 1 if subtract else 0
    result = 0
    for i in range(n):                     # least significant bit first
        a = (A >> i) & 1
        b = ((B >> i) & 1) ^ (1 if subtract else 0)
        s, carry = full_adder(a, b, carry)
        result |= s << i
    return result

print(add(6, 7, 4), add(5, 3, 4, subtract=True))   # 13, 2

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): add 0110 and 0111
Solution

Column by column from the right: 0 + 1 = 1; 1 + 1 = 10 (write 0, carry 1); 1 + 1 + 1 = 11 (write 1, carry 1); 0 + 0 + 1 = 1. Result 1101. As unsigned numbers, 6 + 7 = 13 ✓. As 4-bit signed numbers, 1101 means −3: the true answer 13 is outside −8…7, so this is a signed overflow (carry into the leftmost bit is 1, carry out is 0).

Problem 2 (easy): write −5 in 8-bit two’s complement
Solution

+5 = 00000101. Invert: 11111010. Add 1: 11111011. Check: −128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 2 + 1 = −5 ✓.

Problem 3 (medium): simplify F(A, B, C) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 5, 6, 7)
Solution

On a 3-variable map, the 1s are at 000, 001, 010, 101, 110, 111. Three pairs cover them all: 000–001 gives A̅B̅; 010–110 gives BC̅; 101–111 gives AC. So F = A̅B̅ + BC̅ + AC, three two-literal terms instead of six three-literal terms. (A second, equally small answer exists: A̅C̅ + B̅C + AB.)

Problem 4 (medium): design a 2-bit comparator for A > B
Solution

A = A₁A₀ is larger if its top bit is larger, or if the top bits are equal and its lower bit is larger: GT = A₁·B̅₁ + (A₁ ⊙ B₁)·A₀·B̅₀, where ⊙ is XNOR (equality). Check A = 10, B = 01: A₁B̅₁ = 1·1 = 1, so GT = 1 ✓. The same pattern extends to any width.

Problem 5 (harder): adder timing

A full adder’s carry path takes 2 gate delays of 20 ps. What clock frequency could a 32-bit ripple-carry adder support if addition must finish within one clock cycle, and what about a parallel-prefix adder with 12 gate delays?

Solution

Ripple: 32 × 2 × 20 ps = 1,280 ps, so at most 1/1.28 ns ≈ 0.78 GHz (ignoring everything else in the cycle). Parallel prefix: 12 × 20 ps = 240 ps, allowing up to ≈ 4.2 GHz. This is why fast adders are essential for multi-gigahertz processors.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: how small can a circuit be?

Finding the absolutely smallest circuit for a given function is extremely hard. Even exact two-level (sum-of-products) minimisation becomes intractable for large functions, which is why industrial tools rely on heuristics such as Espresso. [7] For general multi-level circuits, no efficient method is known to find the minimum, and proving that a function needs large circuits is tied to the deepest open problems of computer science, including P versus NP. Practical design therefore balances size, speed and power with clever approximations rather than guaranteed optima.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “computers have separate circuits for adding and subtracting”

The myth: a processor needs one circuit to add, another to subtract, and separate rules for positive and negative numbers.

The reality: thanks to two’s complement, one adder handles positive and negative numbers and both operations: subtraction is addition of the inverted number with a carry-in of 1. Even multiplication is built mostly from adders. Choosing the right number representation saves enormous amounts of hardware.

Why it matters: good representations, not just faster gates, are a recurring source of efficiency in computing.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Combinational circuits have no memory: outputs depend only on current inputs.
  • Karnaugh maps use Gray-code order so that grouping neighbouring 1s eliminates variables; don’t-cares help further.
  • Multiplexers select one of many inputs; decoders activate one of many outputs.
  • Full adders (S = A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin, Cout = AB + Cin(A ⊕ B)) chain into multi-bit adders.
  • Two’s complement lets one adder handle negative numbers and subtraction; watch for overflow.
  • Carry lookahead cuts adder delay from proportional to n to proportional to log n.
