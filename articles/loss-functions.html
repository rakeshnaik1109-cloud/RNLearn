Loss Functions: What Machine Learning Models Minimise

Every model is trained to make one number as small as possible. Choose that number well and the model predicts what you need; choose badly and it chases outliers, learns slowly or gives probabilities you cannot trust.

1. What Does a Network Minimise?

Part 6 showed how a network computes and how backpropagation finds the direction that lowers its error. But “error” was taken for granted. Every model in this series is trained by making one number as small as possible, the loss, and the choice of that number decides what the model learns. Change the loss and the same network, on the same data, will predict a mean instead of a median, a probability instead of a yes-or-no, or will ignore a few strange points instead of chasing them.

This part is about that choice: where the common losses come from, what each one assumes, and how to pick one. [1, 2]

3. Loss, Cost and Risk

A loss function L(ŷ, y) scores a single prediction ŷ against the true value y: zero for a perfect prediction, larger for worse ones. Training minimises the average loss over the training set, sometimes called the cost or the empirical risk, often with a penalty on large weights added (Part 2):

$$J(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i),\, y_i\big) \;+\; \lambda\,\Omega(\theta).$$

What we really want is a low loss on new data, the expected or true risk; the training average is our estimate of it, which is why Part 1 insisted on held-out data. A second distinction matters just as much: the loss used for training is often not the measure we finally report. Accuracy, the F1 score and the area under the ROC curve are what people care about, but they change in jumps as predictions cross a threshold, so their gradient is zero almost everywhere and gradient descent cannot use them. Training therefore minimises a smooth stand-in, a surrogate loss, chosen so that pushing it down also improves the real target.

Running example

As in earlier parts, the regression examples use simple data on the interval 0 to 1, and the classification examples use the series’ 200-point data sets (140 for training, 60 for testing). Figures 3 and 4 add a few deliberate outliers to show how losses react to them.

4. Squared and Absolute Error

The oldest loss in science is the squared error (ŷ − y)². Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805, for fitting the orbits of comets: choose the curve that makes the sum of squared errors smallest. [3] Its average over a data set is the mean squared error (MSE). Its rival is the absolute error |ŷ − y|, whose average is the mean absolute error (MAE).

The difference between them shows up most clearly in the simplest possible model: a single number c that predicts every value. The c that minimises the total squared error is the mean of the data; the c that minimises the total absolute error is the median. A model trained with squared error therefore predicts the average outcome for each input, and one trained with absolute error the middle outcome.

Twelve values between 4.1 and 6.7 have mean and median both 5.5. Add a thirteenth at 15 and the mean jumps to 6.23 while the median moves only to 5.6; put the outlier at 40 and the mean is 8.15, larger than every typical value, while the median stays at 5.6. Squaring makes big errors count enormously, so the squared error bends over backwards to reduce the largest ones.

The maths: why the mean and the median

The derivative of Σ(yi − c)² with respect to c is −2Σ(yi − c), which is zero when c is the mean. The derivative of Σ|yi − c| is (number of values below c) − (number above c), which is zero when c has as many values on each side: the median. Each point’s “pull” on c is proportional to its error under squared loss, but is the same size, ±1, under absolute loss.

5. Huber and Robust Losses

Absolute error resists outliers but has drawbacks of its own: its gradient jumps from −1 to +1 at zero error, which can make optimisation jittery near the solution, and it estimates the median, which is not always what is wanted. In 1964 Peter Huber proposed a compromise: a loss that is squared for errors smaller than a threshold δ and grows only linearly beyond it. [4]

$$L_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac12 r^2 & |r| \le \delta \\ \delta\big(|r| - \tfrac12\delta\big) & |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$

Near the solution it behaves like squared error, smooth and efficient; far away it behaves like absolute error, so no point can pull with more than a fixed force. The Huber loss (called “smooth L1” in some libraries) is widely used in regression with noisy data, in object detectors that predict box coordinates, and in reinforcement learning.

Without outliers all three lines have slopes close to the true value of 2 (1.95 for squared and Huber loss, 1.88 for absolute error). With four outliers among the 34 points, the least-squares slope collapses to 0.75, while Huber (δ = 0.5) gives 1.74 and absolute error 1.81. With six outliers the least-squares line is nearly flat (slope 0.22).

Watch out: a robust loss is not a data cleaner

A robust loss limits how hard each point can pull, but it does not make an outlier disappear. A flexible network trained for long enough can still bend itself to pass through a few wild points, whatever the loss. Look at the data first: an outlier may be a recording error to fix, or the most interesting observation you have.

6. Losses as Likelihoods

Are losses just a matter of taste? No: almost every standard loss can be derived from a probability model of the data. In 1809 Carl Friedrich Gauss justified least squares by assuming that measurement errors follow the normal (bell-shaped) distribution; [5] in 1922 Ronald Fisher set out the general principle of maximum likelihood: choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable. [6]

Maximising the likelihood is the same as minimising its negative logarithm, the negative log-likelihood, and that turns out to be a familiar loss in each case:

  • Normal errors give the squared error.
  • Laplace (double-exponential) errors, which have heavier tails, give the absolute error.
  • A yes-or-no outcome with probability p (a Bernoulli variable) gives the binary cross-entropy.
  • One of K classes (a categorical variable) gives the categorical cross-entropy.
  • Counts (a Poisson variable) give the Poisson loss ŷ − y log ŷ.

Choosing a loss therefore amounts to choosing what you believe about the noise in your data. It also tells you what the trained model predicts: the mean of a normal distribution, the median of a Laplace distribution, a probability for a Bernoulli outcome.

The maths: from the normal density to squared error

If y = f(x) + ε with ε normal, mean 0 and standard deviation σ, the density of one observation is (2πσ²)−½ exp(−(y − f(x))²/2σ²). Its negative logarithm is

$$-\log p(y \mid x) = \frac{(y - f(x))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2),$$

a scaled squared error plus a constant. Summing over independent observations, maximising the likelihood is exactly minimising the sum of squared errors. If the network also predicts σ, the same formula gives a loss that learns how uncertain each prediction is.

7. Cross-Entropy for Classification

A classifier with a sigmoid output (Part 6) predicts a probability p that the label is 1. Its negative log-likelihood is the binary cross-entropy, also called log loss:

$$L(p, y) = -\big[\,y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\,\big].$$

If the true label is 1, the loss is −log p: 0.105 for p = 0.9, 0.693 for p = 0.5, 2.30 for p = 0.1 and 4.6 for p = 0.01, growing without limit as a confident prediction turns out wrong. The name comes from information theory: Claude Shannon’s 1948 entropy measures the average surprise of a source, and the cross-entropy measures the average surprise when outcomes from one distribution are predicted with another. [7] The difference between the two is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, introduced in 1951, which is zero only when the predicted distribution matches the true one. [8]

Why not simply use squared error on the probability? Because of how the gradient flows back through the sigmoid. With cross-entropy, the sigmoid’s slope cancels and the gradient with respect to the logit z is just p − y: the more wrong the prediction, the harder the push. With squared error, the gradient carries an extra factor p(1 − p), the sigmoid’s slope, which is almost zero when the network is confidently wrong.

At z = −5 the network gives the true class a probability of 0.0067. The cross-entropy gradient is 0.993, nearly the maximum; the squared-error gradient is 0.013, about 75 times smaller. The consequences are easy to see in training.

From a neutral start, both losses reach about 95% training accuracy on the two clouds within 50 steps (96% with cross-entropy, 94% with squared error). From weights pointing the wrong way, cross-entropy is back at 94% after 50 steps; squared error is still at 6% after 250 steps and only 10% after 500. In speech-recognition experiments, Pavel Golik, Patrick Doetsch and Hermann Ney found the same at scale: networks trained with squared error from random starting weights did not reach a good solution, whereas cross-entropy did. [9] This is why classifiers are trained with cross-entropy.

The maths: the sigmoid’s slope cancels

With p = σ(z) and dp/dz = p(1 − p):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\big[-y\log p-(1-y)\log(1-p)\big] = \Big(-\frac{y}{p}+\frac{1-y}{1-p}\Big)p(1-p) = p - y,$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(p-y)^2 = 2(p-y)\,p(1-p).$$

The same cancellation happens for a softmax output with categorical cross-entropy, and for a linear output with squared error: each output activation has a “matching” loss whose gradient is simply prediction minus target.

In code: use the numerically stable versions 
import torch
from torch import nn

logits = torch.tensor([-5.0, 0.3, 4.0])     # raw network outputs, no sigmoid
y      = torch.tensor([ 1.0, 0.0, 1.0])

# Binary: sigmoid and cross-entropy fused, stable for large |z|
print(nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()(logits, y))

# Multi-class: softmax and cross-entropy fused; targets are class indices
scores = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.1]])
print(nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)(scores, torch.tensor([0])))

# Robust regression
print(nn.HuberLoss(delta=0.5)(torch.tensor([1.0, 9.0]), torch.tensor([1.2, 2.0])))

Passing raw logits to these fused losses avoids taking the logarithm of a probability that has rounded to exactly 0 or 1.

8. Softmax and Many Classes

For K classes, the network outputs one score per class, the logits z1, …, zK, and the softmax turns them into probabilities that are positive and sum to 1:

$$p_k = \frac{e^{z_k/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}},$$

with temperature T = 1 in ordinary training. John Bridle gave the function its name in 1990, interpreting a network’s outputs as class probabilities trained by maximum likelihood. [10] The categorical cross-entropy is −log ptrue, the negative log of the probability given to the correct class, and its gradient with respect to each logit is again prediction minus target, pk − tk.

Logits of 2, 1 and 0.1 give probabilities 0.659, 0.242 and 0.099, and a loss of 0.417 if the first class is correct. Raising the temperature flattens the probabilities without changing their order; lowering it sharpens them. Temperature is used to tune how adventurous a language model’s sampled text is, and, after training, to correct overconfident probabilities (Section 11).

Cross-entropy with hard 0/1 targets keeps rewarding the network for pushing the correct logit ever higher, which can make it overconfident. Label smoothing, introduced by Christian Szegedy and colleagues for image classification in 2016, replaces the target 1 with 1 − ε and spreads ε evenly over all classes. [11] With ε = 0.1 and three classes, the target becomes 0.933, 0.033 and 0.033. Rafael Müller, Simon Kornblith and Geoffrey Hinton later showed that label smoothing tends to improve calibration, but can make the trained network a worse teacher for distilling knowledge into a smaller one. [12]

9. Margin Losses

With labels written as y = ±1 and a score f(x) whose sign gives the predicted class, the margin m = y·f(x) is positive for a correct prediction and negative for a mistake. The loss we would really like is the 0–1 loss: 1 for a mistake, 0 otherwise. It has no useful gradient, so each method in this series replaces it with a different smooth or convex stand-in, and all of them can be drawn on the same axis.

  • The hinge loss max(0, 1 − m) of the support vector machine (Part 5) is zero for points safely beyond the margin, so only the support vectors matter. [13]
  • The logistic loss log(1 + e−m) is the binary cross-entropy written in terms of the margin: logistic regression and neural networks.
  • The exponential loss e−m is what AdaBoost (Part 4) minimises, as Jerome Friedman, Trevor Hastie and Robert Tibshirani showed in 2000. [14] At m = −2 it is 7.4, against 3 for the hinge, so boosting is especially sensitive to mislabelled points.

Peter Bartlett, Michael Jordan and Jon McAuliffe identified in 2006 the condition under which minimising such a convex surrogate also minimises the misclassification rate, given enough data; the hinge, logistic and exponential losses all satisfy it. [15] So the choice between them is less about getting the boundary right in the limit and more about robustness to label noise, sparsity and whether you need probabilities. Only the logistic loss yields probabilities directly.

10. Imbalanced Data and Focal Loss

When one class is rare (fraud, disease, defects), the average loss is dominated by the common class, and a model can score well by mostly ignoring the rare one. The simplest remedy is a weighted cross-entropy, which multiplies the loss of each rare-class example by a weight, often the inverse of its frequency. Resampling the data has a similar effect.

A subtler problem appears when most examples are not just common but easy. A one-stage object detector evaluates tens of thousands of candidate locations per image, almost all of them obviously background. Each contributes a small loss, but together they swamp the few hard examples that matter. In 2017 Tsung-Yi Lin, Priya Goyal, Ross Girshick, Kaiming He and Piotr Dollár proposed the focal loss, which multiplies the cross-entropy by (1 − pt)γ, where pt is the probability given to the true class. [16] Well-classified examples are down-weighted heavily, poorly classified ones hardly at all; they found γ = 2 worked best.

$$\text{FL}(p_t) = -(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\,\log p_t.$$

11. Probabilities You Can Trust

If a model says “70% chance of rain”, it should rain on about 70% of such days. A model whose probabilities match observed frequencies is calibrated. Calibration depends on the loss used to judge probabilities. Glenn Brier proposed scoring weather forecasts by their squared error in 1950; [17] the Brier score and the log loss are both proper scoring rules: a forecaster minimises the expected score by reporting their true belief. [18] Not every plausible score has this property.

If rain has probability 0.7, both log loss and the Brier score are best when the forecaster reports 0.7. Under absolute error, reporting 1 gives an expected score of 0.30, better than the honest 0.7, which scores 0.42. A model trained with an improper loss learns to exaggerate.

Training with cross-entropy, a proper rule, does not guarantee calibration on new data, though. In 2017 Chuan Guo, Geoff Pleiss, Yu Sun and Kilian Weinberger found that modern deep networks, unlike those of a decade earlier, are systematically overconfident, and that the cross-entropy on validation data can rise while accuracy keeps improving. [19] Their remedy, temperature scaling, divides all logits by a single temperature T fitted on validation data: it leaves every predicted class unchanged and makes the probabilities honest.

12. Beyond Prediction Error

  • Quantile (pinball) loss. To predict the 90th percentile rather than the average, for example a delivery time you can promise, penalise under-prediction by τ = 0.9 and over-prediction by 1 − τ = 0.1. Roger Koenker and Gilbert Bassett introduced this tilted absolute loss in 1978; with τ = 0.5 it is half the absolute error and gives the median. [20] Training several quantiles gives a prediction interval.
  • KL divergence. When the target is a whole distribution rather than one label, as in distilling a large model into a small one, or in the variational autoencoders of Part 9, the loss compares distributions with the Kullback–Leibler divergence. [8]
  • Losses on pairs and triplets. For face recognition or search, what matters is that similar items end up close together. The triplet loss of FaceNet pulls an image towards another image of the same person and pushes it away from a different person by at least a margin. [21]
  • Penalties. The L1 and L2 penalties of Part 2 are added to the loss to prefer simpler models; Part 8 returns to them as weight decay.
  • Several tasks at once. A network that predicts a class and a bounding box minimises a weighted sum of a classification loss and a regression loss; the weights are extra hyperparameters.

Start from the question “what should the model predict for each input: a mean, a median, a quantile, a probability?” and the loss usually follows. Then evaluate with the metric you actually care about, on held-out data.

14. Open Questions

Open questions: does the loss have to match the goal?

The theory of surrogate losses guarantees that, with unlimited data and a flexible enough model, minimising cross-entropy or the hinge loss also minimises the error rate. [15] Real networks have neither unlimited data nor perfect optimisation, and practice is full of puzzles. Losses whose minimisers are identical in theory often give different results; networks trained with a proper loss are nonetheless overconfident on new data; [19] and label smoothing, which deliberately distorts the targets, often improves accuracy. [11, 12] How the choice of loss interacts with the network’s architecture, the optimiser and the amount of data, and how best to train directly for measures such as F1 or ranking quality, remain active research questions.

15. Worked Example: How Much Do Easy Examples Count?

Imagine an image with 10 objects and 10,000 background locations. The detector already handles the background well, giving the right answer probability 0.99, but struggles with the objects, giving them only 0.3.

  1. Cross-entropy, background: each easy example costs −ln 0.99 = 0.01005; all 10,000 together cost 100.5.
  2. Cross-entropy, objects: each costs −ln 0.3 = 1.204; the 10 together cost 12.0. The easy background makes up 89% of the total loss, and of the gradient that trains the network.
  3. Focal loss, γ = 2, background: each loss is multiplied by (1 − 0.99)² = 0.0001, so the 10,000 together cost 0.010.
  4. Focal loss, objects: each is multiplied by (1 − 0.3)² = 0.49, so the 10 together cost 5.90.
  5. Result: the background’s share falls from 89% to 0.17%. Training now concentrates on the objects. Figure 9 lets you vary the number of easy examples and γ.

17. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a lower loss means a better model”

The myth: the training loss measures how good a model is, so a lower loss is always better and the loss is what we should report.

The reality: the loss is a tool for training, not a verdict. A training loss can be pushed towards zero by memorising the data (Part 1). Losses with different scales cannot be compared: a cross-entropy of 0.3 and a squared error of 0.3 mean quite different things. Even on held-out data, the loss and the measure that matters can disagree: Guo and colleagues found validation cross-entropy rising while classification error kept falling, as networks became overconfident rather than less accurate. [19]

Why it matters: train with a loss chosen for good gradients and the right kind of prediction, then judge the model with the measure that reflects the real cost of its mistakes, on data it has not seen.

18. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • A model learns whatever its loss rewards: squared error gives the mean, absolute error the median, the pinball loss a quantile, cross-entropy a probability.
  • Squared error is very sensitive to outliers; the Huber and absolute losses cap the pull of each point.
  • Most standard losses are negative log-likelihoods, so choosing a loss means choosing a noise model.
  • Train classifiers with cross-entropy: with a sigmoid or softmax output its gradient is simply prediction minus target, while squared error stalls when the model is confidently wrong.
  • Hinge, logistic and exponential losses are convex stand-ins for the 0–1 loss used by SVMs, logistic regression and boosting.
  • Focal and weighted losses rebalance training when easy or common examples dominate.
  • Log loss and the Brier score are proper scoring rules, but deep networks still need calibration checks; temperature scaling is a simple fix.
