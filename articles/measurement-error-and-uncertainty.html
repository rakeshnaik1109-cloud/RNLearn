Measurement, Error and Uncertainty: How Sure Are You?

Every measurement is imperfect. Here is how to say exactly how imperfect: error and uncertainty, uncertainty budgets, propagation through formulas, and how to report a result honestly.

1. A Number Is Not Enough

A student times a pendulum and announces that the acceleration due to gravity is 9.792817 m/s². Another reports 9.83 m/s². Who is right? Both numbers are meaningless as they stand, because neither says how well it is known. If the first result is uncertain by ±0.5 m/s², its six decimal places are fiction; if the second is uncertain by ±0.01 m/s², it disagrees with the accepted local value and something is wrong.

Every measurement is imperfect. Scales are slightly miscalibrated, hands shake, thermometers lag, digital displays round, and repeated readings scatter. Metrology, the science of measurement, has developed a careful language for this: error and uncertainty, random and systematic effects, accuracy and precision, Type A and Type B evaluations, and rules for propagating uncertainty through calculations. This part of the series explains that language and how to use it, from the pendulum in a school laboratory to the international comparisons that define the units of measurement. [1, 2]

The guiding principle is simple: a measurement result is a value together with its uncertainty. Without the uncertainty, the value cannot be compared with anything.

3. Error and Uncertainty

The two words are often used interchangeably, but metrologists distinguish them carefully. [3]

  • The error of a measurement is the difference between the measured value and the true value. Since we never know the true value exactly, we never know the error exactly either.
  • The uncertainty is a number that quantifies our doubt about the result: a range within which the true value is believed to lie, with a stated level of confidence.

A measurement can be very close to the true value by luck (small error) and still have a large uncertainty; or it can have a small stated uncertainty and a large error, if something has been overlooked. The goal of uncertainty analysis is an honest, well-founded statement of how much we know.

Measurement rests on agreed standards. In 1875 seventeen nations signed the Metre Convention, creating the International Bureau of Weights and Measures. Since 2019, the units of the International System (SI) have been defined by fixing the values of fundamental constants, such as the speed of light and the Planck constant, so that any well-equipped laboratory can realise them. [4] Every measurement is ultimately linked to these definitions through a chain of calibrations, a property called traceability.

4. Random and Systematic Errors

Errors come in two broad kinds.

  • Random errors vary unpredictably from one reading to the next: electronic noise, small air currents, the exact moment you press a stopwatch. They scatter results in both directions and can be reduced by repeating the measurement and averaging.
  • Systematic errors shift every reading in the same way: a balance that reads 0.3 g too high, a ruler that has stretched, a thermometer that lags behind a rising temperature, reading a scale at an angle (parallax), or always pressing the stopwatch a little late. Averaging cannot remove them. They are found only by calibration, by comparing different methods, and by careful thought.

A third category, blunders, covers outright mistakes: misreading a dial, writing down the wrong number, using the wrong units. They are not part of the uncertainty and should be found and corrected (Part 1 discussed how to treat suspicious values).

The discovery of human systematic error has a famous history. In 1796 Nevil Maskelyne, the Astronomer Royal, dismissed his assistant David Kinnebrook, whose timings of stars crossing the telescope's crosshairs were consistently about 0.8 seconds later than his own. Decades later, Friedrich Bessel investigated and found that every observer has a characteristic delay, which he called the personal equation. [5] Kinnebrook had not been careless; he simply had a different reaction time. Astronomers began measuring and correcting for each observer's personal equation, and eventually replaced human timing with automatic recording.

5. Accuracy, Precision and Trueness

In everyday speech "accurate" and "precise" mean the same thing. In measurement they do not. [3, 6]

  • Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree with each other: small scatter, small random error.
  • Trueness describes how close the average of many measurements is to the true value: small systematic error.
  • Accuracy describes how close a single result is to the true value, and requires both trueness and precision.

Precision itself has two flavours: repeatability (the same person, instrument and conditions, over a short time) and reproducibility (different people, instruments, laboratories or days). Reproducibility is almost always worse, because it includes effects that stay constant within one session. A method that is repeatable but not reproducible is hiding a systematic effect.

6. Type A: Uncertainty from Repeated Measurements

The Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (the GUM, first published in 1993 and now the international standard approach) classifies uncertainty components by how they are evaluated, not by where they come from. [1, 7] A Type A evaluation uses statistics on repeated observations.

If you make n independent readings with sample standard deviation s, the best estimate of the quantity is their mean, and its standard uncertainty is the standard error of the mean:

$$u(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

For five timings of 20 swings of a pendulum, 40.05, 40.12, 39.98, 40.10 and 40.03 s, the mean is 40.056 s, s = 0.056 s, and u = 0.056/√5 = 0.025 s. The standard deviation describes the scatter of single readings and does not shrink with more data; the standard error of the mean does, as 1/√n (Part 2). But averaging only beats random error. As Figure 3 shows, it brings you ever closer to the true value plus any systematic offset.

With very few readings, s itself is uncertain, and the standard error underestimates the true spread of possible means; Part 4 shows how the t-distribution corrects for this.

7. Type B: Uncertainty from Everything Else

A Type B evaluation uses any other information: calibration certificates, manufacturer specifications, the resolution of a display, previous data, published values, or scientific judgement. [1, 8] The skill is turning that information into a standard uncertainty, comparable with a standard deviation. Common cases:

  • A stated range, equally likely anywhere (a rectangular distribution, ±a): u = a/√3. A digital display with resolution 0.01 V rounds by at most ±0.005 V, so u = 0.005/√3 ≈ 0.0029 V. Reading a ruler marked in millimetres to the nearest mark: ±0.5 mm, so u ≈ 0.29 mm.
  • A range where the centre is more likely (a triangular distribution, ±a): u = a/√6.
  • A calibration certificate stating an expanded uncertainty U with coverage factor k (for example "U = 0.6 mm, k = 2"): u = U/k = 0.3 mm.
  • A manufacturer's specification such as "accuracy ±(0.5% of reading + 2 digits)": usually treated as rectangular, u = limit/√3, unless the manufacturer says otherwise.

Type A and Type B uncertainties are treated identically once evaluated. The distinction is only about how the number was obtained. In 1980 an international working group made this the basis of a common approach, eventually written into the GUM. [7]

8. Combining Uncertainties: The Budget

A real measurement has several uncertainty components. If they are independent, their standard uncertainties combine by adding in quadrature, the square root of the sum of squares:

$$u_c = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2 + \dots + u_k^2}.$$

This follows from the fact that variances of independent quantities add (Part 2). It has a striking consequence: the largest component dominates. Components of 3 and 4 units combine to 5, but components of 1 and 10 combine to 10.05: the small one hardly matters.

The combined standard uncertainty uc corresponds roughly to one standard deviation. For reporting, it is usually multiplied by a coverage factor k to give an expanded uncertainty U = k·uc. With k = 2, the interval ±U covers the true value with about 95% confidence, if the distribution is roughly normal. Always state which k you used.

A table listing every component, its source, type, value and contribution is called an uncertainty budget. Its greatest value is practical: it shows where the uncertainty comes from, and therefore where effort to improve the measurement will pay off.

9. Propagation of Uncertainty

Most results are calculated from several measured quantities: density from mass and volume, g from length and period. How do the input uncertainties carry through to the result? For a result q = f(x, y, …) with independent inputs and small uncertainties, the general rule (the "law of propagation of uncertainty") is [1, 9]

$$u_q^2 = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)^2 u_x^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)^2 u_y^2 + \cdots$$

Each input's uncertainty is multiplied by how sensitive the result is to that input (the partial derivative, or sensitivity coefficient). For common formulas this simplifies to easy rules:

Two practical lessons follow. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers can destroy precision, so design experiments to measure differences directly when possible. And quantities raised to high powers deserve the most care: in g = 4π²L/T², the period counts twice as much as the length.

The formula is an approximation that assumes small uncertainties and nearly linear behaviour. When those fail, for example when dividing by a quantity with a large relative uncertainty, the modern alternative is Monte Carlo propagation: simulate thousands of possible values of each input from its distribution, compute the result for each, and look at the spread. A 2008 supplement to the GUM describes the method. [10]

One important caveat: the quadrature rules assume the input errors are independent. If two inputs share a systematic error, for example two lengths measured with the same miscalibrated ruler, their errors are correlated, and the formula must include an extra covariance term. Ignoring it can either overstate or understate the uncertainty.

10. Reporting a Result

  • Round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures (two is common when the first digit is 1 or 2), and round the value to the same decimal place. Not 9.792817 ± 0.03155 m/s², but 9.793 ± 0.032 m/s².
  • State what the uncertainty means: standard uncertainty (k = 1) or expanded uncertainty (k = 2, about 95%).
  • The concise notation puts the uncertainty in the last digits in brackets: 9.793(32) m/s² means 9.793 ± 0.032. Fundamental constants are often written this way.
  • Carry extra digits during calculations, and round only the final result, to avoid accumulating rounding errors.
  • Describe the main components, ideally with an uncertainty budget, so others can judge and reproduce your result.

Two results agree if their difference is small compared with the combined uncertainty of the difference. For results x1 ± u1 and x2 ± u2, a difference larger than about 2√(u1² + u2²) is a warning sign worth investigating.

11. Combining Measurements: The Weighted Mean

When several independent measurements of the same quantity have different uncertainties, the best combined estimate gives more weight to the more precise ones. The weights are the inverse squares of the uncertainties, wi = 1/ui²: [9]

$$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}, \qquad u(\bar{x}_w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum w_i}}.$$

A measurement ten times more precise counts a hundred times more. But a weighted mean is only meaningful if the measurements are consistent. A check is to compute χ² = Σ wi(xi − x̄w)² and compare it with the number of degrees of freedom, n − 1. Their ratio's square root, √(χ²/(n − 1)), is known as the Birge ratio, after Raymond Birge, who pioneered the systematic combination of measurements of the fundamental constants from 1929. [11, 12] A Birge ratio near 1 means the scatter matches the stated uncertainties; much larger than 1 means at least one measurement has an unrecognised error. For four consistent measurements of g of 9.807(4), 9.815(6), 9.801(5) and 9.812(3) m/s², the weighted mean is 9.8092(20) m/s², with a Birge ratio of 1.26.

12. Calibration Curves

Many instruments do not measure the quantity you want directly. A spectrophotometer measures how much light a solution absorbs; a sensor outputs a voltage; you want a concentration, a temperature or a pressure. The link is a calibration curve: measure the instrument's signal for several standards of known value, fit a line (or curve) through them, and then read it backwards to convert the signal of an unknown sample into a value.

The calibration contributes its own uncertainty: from the standards themselves, from the scatter of the points about the fitted line, and from how far the unknown lies from the centre of the calibration range. Results are most reliable in the middle of the range covered by the standards; extrapolating beyond the highest or lowest standard is risky, because the instrument's response may not stay linear. Part 7 covers fitting lines and their uncertainties properly.

13. Limits of Detection and Quantification

Every instrument gives a small, noisy signal even for a sample containing none of the substance (a blank). How small an amount can be detected at all? In 1968 the chemist Lloyd Currie set out a clear statistical framework, and the common practical definitions are: [13, 14]

  • Limit of detection (LOD): the smallest amount whose signal can be reliably distinguished from the blank, often taken as 3 × the standard deviation of the blank signal, divided by the slope of the calibration curve.
  • Limit of quantification (LOQ): the smallest amount that can be measured with acceptable precision, often 10 × the blank standard deviation divided by the slope.

Between the LOD and the LOQ, you can say a substance is present but not how much with confidence. Below the LOD, report "not detected", with the LOD ("< 0.4 mg/L"), never "zero". Definitions vary between fields and regulators, so always state how your limits were calculated.

14. Worked Example: Measuring g with a Pendulum

A pendulum of length L is timed for 20 swings, five times. The model is g = 4π²L/T², with T the period.

  1. Length. L = 0.9950 m from the pivot to the centre of the bob. Components: tape resolution ±0.5 mm (u = 0.29 mm, rectangular); judging the bob's centre ±1 mm (u = 0.58 mm); tape calibration certificate U = 0.6 mm, k = 2 (u = 0.30 mm). Combined: u(L) = √(0.29² + 0.58² + 0.30²) = 0.71 mm, a relative uncertainty of 0.072%.
  2. Time. Five timings of 20 swings: mean 40.056 s, SD 0.056 s, so Type A u = 0.025 s. Reaction time at start and stop: a possible systematic offset of up to ±0.1 s per timing that does not average out, treated as rectangular: u = 0.058 s. Combined u(t) = √(0.025² + 0.058²) = 0.063 s, so T = 2.0028 s with u(T) = 0.0031 s (0.157%).
  3. Propagate. g = 4π² × 0.9950/2.0028² = 9.7928 m/s². Relative uncertainty: √(0.072%² + (2 × 0.157%)²) = 0.32%, so u(g) = 0.032 m/s². A Monte Carlo simulation with 200,000 trials gives the same: 0.0316 m/s².
  4. Report. g = 9.793 ± 0.032 m/s² (k = 1), or 9.79 ± 0.06 m/s² (k = 2, about 95%). The accepted value near sea level at mid-latitudes is about 9.80–9.81 m/s², within the expanded uncertainty.
  5. Learn from the budget. The reaction-time component contributes about 80% of the variance, the timing scatter 15%, and the length only 5%. A better tape measure is pointless; a light gate or timing more swings would help most. (Real pendulums also have small systematic effects, such as the finite size of the bob and the swing amplitude, which a careful budget would include.)
Try it in Python: an uncertainty budget and Monte Carlo propagation 
import numpy as np
t20 = np.array([40.05, 40.12, 39.98, 40.10, 40.03])          # s, 20 swings each
uA = t20.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(t20))                     # 0.025 s (Type A)
uB = 0.1 / np.sqrt(3)                                        # 0.058 s (reaction, Type B)
T, uT = t20.mean() / 20, np.hypot(uA, uB) / 20
L = 0.9950
uL = np.sqrt((0.0005/np.sqrt(3))**2 + (0.001/np.sqrt(3))**2 + 0.0003**2)
g = 4 * np.pi**2 * L / T**2
ug = g * np.sqrt((uL/L)**2 + (2*uT/T)**2)
print(round(g, 4), round(ug, 4))                             # 9.7928 0.0316

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                               # Monte Carlo check
gs = 4*np.pi**2 * rng.normal(L, uL, 200_000) / rng.normal(T, uT, 200_000)**2
print(gs.std())                                              # about 0.0316

15. Try It Yourself: The Thickness of a Sheet of Paper

Try it yourself: beat your ruler's resolution
  1. Try to measure the thickness of one sheet of printer paper with a ruler marked in millimetres. The reading is "less than 1 mm": the resolution (u ≈ 0.29 mm) is larger than the thing you are measuring.
  2. Now measure the thickness of a stack of 200 sheets (a new ream is usually 500). Suppose it reads 21 mm ± 0.5 mm (resolution), u = 0.29 mm.
  3. Divide: one sheet is 21/200 = 0.105 mm, with u = 0.29/200 ≈ 0.0015 mm. The rule "q = a/c" divides the uncertainty too.
  4. Repeat with different stacks, or squeeze the stack differently, and compare. Is the reproducibility as good as the calculation suggests? (Air gaps between sheets are a systematic effect: how could you check for them?)

Draw up a small uncertainty budget: resolution, squeezing, and the count of sheets. You have just used propagation of uncertainty, and a classic trick of experimental physics: measure many identical things together, or many cycles of a repeating event, to divide the uncertainty.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did a simple maths error lose a spacecraft?

The legend: NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999 because an engineer forgot to convert from imperial to metric units.

The record: the spacecraft was lost on 23 September 1999 as it arrived at Mars. The investigation found that ground software supplied by one team produced thruster impulse data in pound-force seconds, while the navigation software of another team expected newton seconds, a factor of 4.45. The resulting small errors in each trajectory correction accumulated over months, and the orbiter passed far too close to the planet. [15]

The grain of truth: it was a units error, but not one person's slip. The investigation blamed the processes that should have caught it: interface checks, verification, and the navigators' unease about trajectory anomalies that was not acted on. It is a perfect example of a systematic error: every correction was wrong in the same direction, so repetition reinforced it instead of averaging it away.

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: are our uncertainties honest?

In 1986 Max Henrion and Baruch Fischhoff studied the history of measurements of physical constants, such as the speed of light. They found that published uncertainties were systematically too small: successive measurements often differed by many times their stated uncertainties, and new values frequently lay outside the error bars of previous ones. [16] Scientists, it seems, tend to overlook sources of systematic error and to be overconfident.

There is also a subtler bias. In his 1974 talk "Cargo Cult Science", Richard Feynman described how measurements of the electron's charge crept slowly from Robert Millikan's slightly low value towards the modern one, rather than jumping. When later experimenters got a value far from Millikan's, Feynman suggested, they looked for something wrong; when they got a value close to it, they did not look so hard. [17] The previous answer acted as an anchor.

These stories suggest that uncertainty is not only a property of instruments but of people. Honest uncertainty analysis means actively hunting for systematic errors, deciding analysis methods before seeing the answer (some experiments now hide the result until the analysis is fixed, a practice called blind analysis), and remembering that the true value does not care what we expect.

Questions to think about: Can we ever be sure that all systematic errors have been found? If every measurement is uncertain, in what sense do we "know" the value of a physical constant?
