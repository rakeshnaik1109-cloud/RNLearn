Ensembles: Bagging, Random Forests and Boosting

A crowd of imperfect guesses can beat almost every individual. Ensembles turn that idea into the most successful methods for tabular data, by averaging many different trees or by building strong models from a sequence of weak ones.

1. The Wisdom of Crowds

In 1907 Francis Galton visited a country fair where 787 people paid to guess the weight of an ox once slaughtered and dressed. Individually the guesses were all over the place, but the middle (median) guess, 1,207 pounds, was within 1% of the true weight of 1,198 pounds. [1] A crowd of imperfect estimates, combined, beat almost every individual.

The same idea powers some of the most successful methods in machine learning. An ensemble combines many models, often of the same simple kind, into one. Random forests and gradient-boosted trees, the subjects of this part, are the methods most likely to win on ordinary tables of data, as Part 3’s Open Questions noted. [2, 3]

3. Why Combining Works, and When It Does Not

Averaging helps for the same reason that the mean of many measurements is more precise than one (Making Sense of Data Part 2): random errors partly cancel. But there is a catch. Errors cancel only if they differ. A hundred copies of the same model make the same mistakes, and averaging them gains nothing. What matters is how correlated the models’ errors are.

For voting, the same insight goes back to the Marquis de Condorcet’s jury theorem of 1785: if each voter is independently more likely to be right than wrong, a majority of many voters is almost certainly right. [4] Independence is the hard part. Ensemble methods are, in essence, clever ways of making models that are individually good but different from one another.

The maths: the variance of an average

If B models each have prediction variance σ² and every pair has error correlation ρ, the variance of their average is

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat f_b\Big) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2.$$

The second term vanishes as B grows; the first does not. Adding models reduces only the uncorrelated part of the error; reducing ρ, by making the models more different, lowers the floor. Averaging does not reduce bias: an ensemble of models that are all wrong in the same way is still wrong.

4. Bagging: Bootstrap Aggregating

How do you train many different models from one data set? In 1996 Leo Breiman proposed bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating. [5] Draw a bootstrap sample: as many points as the training set, chosen at random with replacement, so some appear twice or more and about 37% not at all (the bootstrap of Making Sense of Data Part 4). Train a model on it. Repeat many times, and average the predictions (or take a majority vote).

Bagging helps most with models that are unstable, whose predictions change a lot with small changes to the data. Decision trees, as Part 3 showed, are the classic example: low bias but high variance. Bagging keeps their low bias and averages away much of the variance.

5. Random Forests

Bagged trees grown on similar data still tend to make the same first splits on the strongest features, so their errors stay correlated. A random forest adds a second source of randomness: at each split, a tree may consider only a random subset of the features. This decorrelates the trees, lowering the floor in Figure 3. Tin Kam Ho had grown forests of trees on random feature subsets in 1995, and in 2001 Breiman combined the idea with bagging in the method now known as the random forest. [6, 7] A common default is to consider √d of the d features at each split for classification.

On the particular two moons of this series, a single deep tree, 100 bagged trees and a 100-tree random forest all score 92–93% on the 60 test points: with so few test points, one data set cannot show the difference. Repeating the experiment on 30 freshly generated sets of moons tells the real story. The average test accuracy is 93.1% for a single tree (standard deviation 4.3 points), 95.0% for bagging (3.0) and 94.6% for the random forest (2.9). On the circles, it rises from 94.8% for a tree to 96.9% for the forest. The ensembles are more accurate on average and, just as important, much less variable from one data set to the next.

Watch out: one test set is one draw

Comparing models on a single small test set can mislead in either direction. Repeat the comparison over several splits or data sets, or use cross-validation, before concluding that one method is better (Part 1).

6. Free Gifts of the Forest: Out-of-Bag Error and Feature Importance

Each bootstrap sample leaves out about a third of the training points. For any point, the trees that never saw it form a mini-ensemble that can predict it honestly. Averaging these out-of-bag predictions over all points gives an estimate of test accuracy without a separate validation set or cross-validation, as Breiman noted. [7] In Figure 4, the out-of-bag estimate for the 100-tree forest on the moons is 92%, close to the test accuracy.

Forests also rank features by importance. The quick way adds up how much each feature reduces impurity across all splits; the more reliable way, permutation importance, measures how much accuracy drops when a feature’s values are shuffled. Impurity-based importance is biased towards features with many possible split points, such as continuous variables or identifiers, a problem documented by Carolin Strobl and colleagues. [8] Importance measures association with the prediction, not cause, and correlated features share or steal each other’s importance (Part 10).

7. Boosting: Strength from Weakness

Bagging trains its models independently and in parallel. Boosting trains them in sequence, each one focusing on the mistakes of those before. The idea began as a theoretical question posed by Michael Kearns and Leslie Valiant: can a “weak” learner, only slightly better than guessing, be turned into a strong one? In 1990 Robert Schapire proved that it can. [9]

The practical breakthrough was AdaBoost (adaptive boosting), published by Yoav Freund and Schapire in 1997, work later honoured with the Gödel Prize. [10] AdaBoost gives every training point a weight. Each round fits a weak learner, often a stump (a tree with a single split), to the weighted data; then it raises the weights of the points the stump got wrong and lowers the others. The final prediction is a weighted vote, with more accurate stumps getting more say.

On the circles, a single stump manages 53% on test: no single straight cut can separate a ring. Five rounds reach 82%, twenty 87%, and a hundred 93%. Each stump is barely better than guessing on its own weighted data, yet together they draw a closed boundary.

The maths: AdaBoost’s update

With labels yi = ±1 and weights wi summing to 1, round t fits a weak learner ht with weighted error εt, gives it the vote

$$\alpha_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t},$$

and updates wi ← wi exp(−αt yi ht(xi)), then renormalises. The final classifier is sign(Σt αt ht(x)). AdaBoost turns out to be gradient descent on the exponential loss e−yF(x), the insight that led to gradient boosting.

8. Gradient Boosting

In 2001 Jerome Friedman recast boosting as gradient descent, not on parameters as in Part 2, but on the predictions themselves. [11] For squared error the recipe is simple: start with a constant (the mean); compute the residuals, the errors still left; fit a small tree to the residuals; add a fraction ν of it to the model; repeat. Each new tree corrects what the ensemble so far gets wrong. For other losses, the trees are fitted to the negative gradient of the loss, which for squared error is exactly the residual, so the same machinery handles classification, ranking and robust regression.

The learning rate ν (also called shrinkage) matters a great deal. With ν = 1 and stumps, the test error is best after just 10 rounds (0.117), then rises as later stumps chase noise. With ν = 0.1, progress is slower but steadier: 0.130 after 50 rounds and about 0.12 from 100 rounds on. Small learning rates with many trees, stopped early using a validation set, usually generalise best.

The maths: boosting as gradient descent in function space

For a loss L(y, F), start from F0 = argminc Σ L(yi, c). At round m compute the pseudo-residuals and fit a tree hm to them:

$$r_{im} = -\left.\frac{\partial L(y_i, F)}{\partial F}\right|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)}, \qquad F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu\,h_m(x).$$

For squared error L = ½(y − F)², the pseudo-residual is simply y − F. For log loss in classification, it is y − p, the gap between label and predicted probability.

9. Modern Boosting Libraries

Gradient boosting became the workhorse of applied machine learning through fast, carefully engineered libraries:

  • XGBoost (2016) adds regularisation of the trees, clever handling of missing values, and parallel, cache-aware computation. Its authors reported that 17 of the 29 winning solutions published on the Kaggle competition platform in 2015 used it. [12]
  • LightGBM (2017) speeds up training on large data sets by binning feature values into histograms and growing trees leaf by leaf. [13]
  • CatBoost (2018) handles categorical features natively and uses an ordering scheme that avoids a subtle form of target leakage. [14]

All three, and scikit-learn’s HistGradientBoostingClassifier, share the same core ideas: many shallow trees, a small learning rate, row and column subsampling for extra randomness, and early stopping on validation data.

In code: forests and boosting with scikit-learn 
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import (RandomForestClassifier, AdaBoostClassifier,
                              HistGradientBoostingClassifier)

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=1000, noise=0.3, random_state=0)

forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, oob_score=True, random_state=0).fit(X, y)
print("OOB accuracy:", forest.oob_score_)

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=0)       # stumps by default
gbm = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.1, max_iter=500,
                                     early_stopping=True, random_state=0)
for name, model in [("AdaBoost", ada), ("Gradient boosting", gbm)]:
    print(name, cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5).mean())

11. Stacking and Blending

Bagging and boosting combine many models of one kind. Stacking, proposed by David Wolpert in 1992, combines different kinds: a logistic regression, a forest, a k-NN, a neural network. [15] A second-level meta-model learns how to weigh their predictions. The crucial detail is that the meta-model must be trained on predictions the base models made for data they did not train on, usually out-of-fold predictions from cross-validation; otherwise it learns to trust whichever base model overfits most, a form of leakage (Part 1).

Stacking and its simpler cousin, blending, are favourites of machine-learning competitions, where tiny gains in accuracy decide the winner. Whether those gains are worth their cost outside a competition is another matter, as Section 16 shows.

12. Tuning and Using Ensembles

  • Random forests: more trees is always at least as good (a few hundred usually suffice); tune the number of features per split and the minimum leaf size; trees are usually grown deep.
  • Gradient boosting: set a small learning rate (0.05–0.1), use shallow trees (depth 3–8, or a limit on leaves), subsample rows and columns, and pick the number of rounds by early stopping on validation data. Tune with cross-validation (Part 10).
  • Costs: ensembles are larger and slower to predict than single models, and harder to explain. Tree ensembles still cannot extrapolate beyond the range of the training targets, like the regression trees of Part 3.
  • Probabilities: forest votes and boosted scores are often poorly calibrated; recalibrate if probabilities matter (see our metrics article).

13. Open Questions

Open questions: why does boosting not overfit sooner?

Early experiments surprised researchers: AdaBoost’s test error often kept falling for hundreds of rounds after its training error reached zero, as if adding more complexity did no harm. In 1998 Schapire, Freund, Peter Bartlett and Wee Sun Lee proposed an explanation in terms of margins: even after every training point is classified correctly, boosting keeps pushing points further from the boundary, which their theory linked to better generalisation. [16] Later work showed that boosting can overfit noisy data, and that the margin explanation is incomplete. The question of when adding complexity helps or hurts generalisation connects boosting to the double descent of Part 1 and the puzzles of deep learning in Part 8.

14. Worked Example: Comparing Ensembles Fairly

  1. One data set. On the series’ moons, test accuracy: single deep tree 92%, bagging (100 trees) 93%, random forest (100 trees) 92%, AdaBoost (100 stumps) 92%. Logistic regression with polynomial features (Part 2) scored 95%. Taken at face value, the ensembles look useless.
  2. Many data sets. Over 30 freshly generated moons data sets, average test accuracy: tree 93.1% ± 4.3, bagging 95.0% ± 3.0, random forest 94.6% ± 2.9, AdaBoost 93.7% ± 2.7. The ensembles are better on average and more consistent.
  3. Regression. On the running example, gradient boosting with stumps reaches a test error of about 0.12 (ν = 0.1, 100 rounds), better than a single regression tree (0.125 at depth 2, Part 3) but not the smooth cubic (0.075): with one feature and 15 points, a smooth model with the right shape is hard to beat.
  4. Lesson. Ensembles shine with many features, many examples and complex interactions. On tiny, low-dimensional problems a well-chosen simple model can match them, and only careful, repeated evaluation can tell.

16. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: the million-dollar ensemble

The myth: the team that won the $1 million Netflix Prize in 2009 handed Netflix the algorithm that powers its recommendations.

The reality: the winning entry, which beat Netflix’s own rating predictor by more than the target of 10%, was a blend of hundreds of models built by several teams. Netflix did adopt two of the core methods from an earlier stage of the contest. But for the final ensemble, its engineers wrote in 2012 that the additional accuracy gains “did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into a production environment”, and by then the business had shifted from mailing DVDs to streaming, which changed what recommendations needed to do. [17, 18]

Why it matters: in practice, accuracy competes with speed, cost, maintainability and explainability. The best model on a leaderboard is not always the best model to deploy.

17. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Combining models helps when their errors differ; correlation sets a floor on what averaging can achieve.
  • Bagging trains models on bootstrap samples and averages them, reducing the variance of unstable models such as deep trees.
  • Random forests add random feature selection at each split to decorrelate the trees; they work well with little tuning and give out-of-bag estimates.
  • Boosting trains weak models in sequence, each focusing on the errors of the last; gradient boosting fits each tree to the residuals.
  • Boosting needs a small learning rate and early stopping; XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost are the standard tools for tabular data.
  • Stacking combines different models through a meta-model trained on out-of-fold predictions.
  • Judge ensembles over repeated splits; their advantage is often consistency as much as average accuracy.
