1. Data That Change Over Time

Temperatures, prices, website visits, heart rates, case counts, sales: a huge share of real data are time series, measurements of the same thing repeated over time. The questions we ask of them are about change. Is it rising? Is there a regular cycle? Did something happen on a particular date? Is the latest value unusual?

The line chart is the natural tool, and this part covers it and its relatives: decomposing a series into trend, seasonality and noise, smoothing with moving averages, handling many series at once, stacked area charts, index charts, the treacherous dual-axis chart, sparklines, and the trap of the cherry-picked time window.

3. The Line Chart

A line chart plots values against time and joins them with straight lines. The line says “values in between exist and change smoothly”, which is true for temperature or a share price but not for unordered categories, so never join points across categories such as countries or products. William Playfair’s line charts of England’s trade, published in 1786, are among the first. [1]

How to read a line chart
  1. Check the time axis: its span, its spacing (are the intervals equal?) and whether any periods are missing.
  2. Check the vertical axis: units, starting value, and linear or logarithmic scale (Part 1).
  3. Find the overall trend first, then repeating patterns (seasonality), then individual spikes and dips.
  4. Ask how the series was processed: raw values, averages, totals, or smoothed?

In Python, a pandas Series indexed by dates handles calendars, missing values and resampling. [2] Leaving missing values as NaN makes matplotlib break the line, which is more honest than drawing straight across the gap:

# pip install numpy pandas matplotlib
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
dates = pd.date_range("2022-01-01", periods=36, freq="MS")      # monthly, month start
visitors = 1000 + 15 * np.arange(36) + 120 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(36) / 12) + rng.normal(0, 40, 36)
series = pd.Series(visitors, index=dates)
series.iloc[20:22] = np.nan                                     # two missing months

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.8))
ax.plot(series.index, series.values, marker="o", ms=3)          # NaN leaves an honest gap
ax.set_ylabel("Visitors per month")
ax.set_title("Monthly visitors (gap = missing data)")
ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
fig.autofmt_xdate()                                             # tilt date labels to fit
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. Trend, Seasonality and Noise

Many series can be described as the sum of three parts: a slowly changing trend, a seasonal pattern that repeats with a fixed period (a year, a week, a day), and an irregular remainder. Separating them is called decomposition, and it is one of the first things to try with a new series. [3]

The classical method in the figure is simple but has weaknesses: it loses the ends of the series and is thrown off by outliers. A more robust method, STL (seasonal–trend decomposition using Loess), was published by Robert Cleveland and colleagues in 1990 and is available in the statsmodels library: [4, 5]

# pip install numpy pandas matplotlib statsmodels
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.arange(96)                                               # eight years of monthly data
trend = 50 + 0.3 * t
season = 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 12)
noise = rng.normal(0, 2, t.size)
y = pd.Series(trend + season + noise, index=pd.date_range("2017-01", periods=96, freq="MS"))

result = STL(y, period=12).fit()                                # Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess
fig = result.plot()                                             # data, trend, seasonal, residual
fig.set_size_inches(8, 6)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Reading a decomposition: look at the scale of each panel. A seasonal panel whose swings are tiny compared with the remainder means there is little real seasonality, however regular it looks.

5. Smoothing with Moving Averages

Noisy daily data are often shown with a moving average: each point is replaced by the mean of a window of neighbouring values. A 7-day average removes the weekly cycle in data such as hospital admissions; a 12-month average removes the seasons.

When you read a smoothed line, ask how wide the window is and whether it is trailing or centred. A trailing average of 14 days reports a sudden change about a week late; a centred one cannot be computed for the most recent days at all.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
days = pd.date_range("2024-01-01", periods=180, freq="D")
cases = 50 + 30 * np.sin(np.arange(180) / 30) + rng.normal(0, 12, 180)
s = pd.Series(cases, index=days)

trailing = s.rolling(window=14).mean()               # average of the last 14 days: lags behind
centred = s.rolling(window=14, center=True).mean()   # average around each day: no lag, but needs the future

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
ax.plot(s, color="lightgray", label="daily values")
ax.plot(trailing, color="#0072B2", label="14-day trailing average")
ax.plot(centred, color="#D55E00", label="14-day centred average")
ax.set_ylabel("Daily count")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Many Lines at Once

Drawing many series on one chart quickly produces a “spaghetti chart” in which no single line can be followed. Two remedies work well: highlight one or two series and grey out the rest, or use small multiples, a grid of small panels with identical axes, one per series. [6]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
years = np.arange(2000, 2025)
cities = [f"City {c}" for c in "ABCDEFGHI"]
data = {c: 20 + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0.1, 0.6, years.size)) for c in cities}   # synthetic

# One panel per city, all on the same axes so they can be compared
fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 3, figsize=(9, 6.5), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, city in zip(axes.flat, cities):
    for other in cities:                                    # all cities faintly, for context
        ax.plot(years, data[other], color="lightgray", lw=0.8)
    ax.plot(years, data[city], color="#0072B2", lw=2)       # highlight this panel's city
    ax.set_title(city, fontsize=10)
fig.supylabel("Average temperature (°C)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Area and Stacked Area Charts

An area chart is a line chart with the space below filled in, which emphasises volume. A stacked area chart piles several series on top of each other so that the top edge shows the total. It shares the weakness of stacked bars: only the bottom layer sits on a flat baseline, so the trends of the layers above are distorted by everything beneath them.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

years = np.arange(2000, 2025)
coal = np.linspace(40, 18, years.size)
gas = np.linspace(20, 35, years.size)
renew = 5 * np.exp((years - 2000) / 12)                     # synthetic electricity sources
labels = ["Coal", "Gas", "Renewables"]
colours = ["#555555", "#56B4E9", "#009E73"]

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
ax1.stackplot(years, coal, gas, renew, labels=labels, colors=colours)
ax1.set_title("Stacked area: totals and make-up")
ax1.set_ylabel("Generation (TWh)")
ax1.legend(loc="upper left")
for y, lab, col in zip((coal, gas, renew), labels, colours):
    ax2.plot(years, y, color=col, lw=2, label=lab)
ax2.set_title("Lines: each source's own trend")
ax2.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Index Charts

To compare the growth of things measured on very different scales, such as a share priced at 1,500 and one priced at 40, divide each series by its value on a chosen base date and multiply by 100. Every line then starts at 100, and a value of 120 means “20% higher than on the base date”. Price indices, such as consumer price indices, work the same way.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
days = np.arange(250)
stock_a = 1500 * np.exp(np.cumsum(rng.normal(0.0004, 0.012, days.size)))   # expensive share
stock_b = 40 * np.exp(np.cumsum(rng.normal(0.0012, 0.02, days.size)))      # cheap share

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
ax1.plot(days, stock_a, label="A")
ax1.plot(days, stock_b, label="B")
ax1.set_title("Prices: B looks flat")
ax1.set_ylabel("Price")
ax2.plot(days, 100 * stock_a / stock_a[0], label="A")    # index: value / first value × 100
ax2.plot(days, 100 * stock_b / stock_b[0], label="B")
ax2.axhline(100, color="gray", lw=0.8)
ax2.set_title("Indexed to 100 on day 0")
ax2.set_ylabel("Index (day 0 = 100)")
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_xlabel("Trading day")
    ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. Two Y-Axes: Handle With Care

A chart with a second y-axis on the right lets two series with different units share one time axis. It is popular and dangerous. Because the two scales are independent, the designer can make the lines overlap, cross or diverge simply by choosing the axis ranges, and readers tend to compare the lines as if they shared a scale. [7]

If you must use two axes, colour each axis like its line and say so in the title. Usually it is better to stack two panels with a shared time axis, to index both series, or to plot one against the other in a scatter plot.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
years = np.arange(2010, 2025)
ice_cream = 200 + 3 * (years - 2010) + rng.normal(0, 4, years.size)     # synthetic, unrelated
cinema = 50 + 0.4 * (years - 2010) + rng.normal(0, 1, years.size)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(years, ice_cream, color="#0072B2")
ax.set_ylabel("Ice cream sales", color="#0072B2")
ax2 = ax.twinx()                                  # a second y-axis sharing the x-axis
ax2.plot(years, cinema, color="#D55E00")
ax2.set_ylabel("Cinema visits", color="#D55E00")
ax.set_title("Two axes: the lines' overlap depends on the chosen scales")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# Often clearer: two panels stacked with a shared x-axis,
# plt.subplots(2, 1, sharex=True), or index both series to 100.

10. Sparklines

Edward Tufte coined the word sparkline for “small, intense, simple, word-sized graphics”: a line chart the size of a word, without axes, placed in a sentence or table to show the shape of a recent history at a glance. [8] Dashboards use them to show many indicators in little space, usually with the latest value marked and written beside it.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
names = ["Sales", "Visitors", "Returns", "Sign-ups"]
series = [np.cumsum(rng.normal(0.2, 1, 60)) for _ in names]

# Sparklines: tiny, word-sized line charts without axes (Tufte)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(len(names), 1, figsize=(3.2, 2.4))
for ax, name, s in zip(axes, names, series):
    ax.plot(s, color="black", lw=0.9)
    ax.plot(len(s) - 1, s[-1], "o", color="#D55E00", ms=3)   # mark the latest value
    ax.axis("off")
    ax.text(-3, s.mean(), name, ha="right", va="center", fontsize=8)
    ax.text(len(s) + 2, s[-1], f"{s[-1]:.1f}", va="center", fontsize=8)
plt.subplots_adjust(left=0.3, right=0.85)
plt.show()

11. The Cherry-Picked Window

Every time series chart has a start and an end, and both are choices. A short window that begins at a peak can make a rising series look flat; one that begins in a trough can exaggerate a rise. This is one of the most common ways that honest data are used to tell dishonest stories.

12. Common Misreadings

  • Mistaking seasonality for a trend. A rise from January to July may just be summer. Compare the same month across years, or look at a decomposition.
  • Forgetting the lag of smoothing. A trailing 14-day average shows today’s situation as it was about a week ago.
  • Reading the middle layers of a stacked area chart as trends. Their baselines move.
  • Comparing levels instead of growth. A line that looks flat on a big scale may be growing fast in percentage terms; use an index or a log scale.
  • Believing a dual-axis correlation. The overlap of two lines on different axes is set by the chart maker.
  • Ignoring the window. Ask why the chart starts and ends where it does.
  • Treating unequal intervals as equal. If dates are irregular, an axis of equally spaced labels distorts slopes.

13. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who drew the first time-series chart?

The legend: William Playfair invented the time-series chart in 1786.

The record: Playfair made line charts of economic data famous, but he was not the first to plot something against time. In 1936 the historian H. Gray Funkhouser described a diagram in a manuscript of around the tenth or eleventh century showing the positions of the planets over time as wavy lines on a grid. [9] In the 1760s and 1770s the scientist Johann Heinrich Lambert drew graphs of experimental measurements, such as the rate at which water evaporates and the variation of temperatures, and wrote about how to read them. [10] What Playfair added was the use of such charts for economic and political argument, for a general audience.

A second legend: sparklines are a modern invention of the dashboard era. The record: Tufte named them in 2006, but small inline charts appear much earlier in scientific notebooks and printed tables; his contribution was to define the idea and argue for using it. [8]
