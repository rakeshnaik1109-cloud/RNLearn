Modern Transistors: High-k Gates, FinFETs and Gate-All-Around

When gates became shorter than a virus and insulators thinner than five atoms, the classic transistor stopped working. Engineers rebuilt it from new materials and then turned it on its side.

1. When Transistors Got Too Small

By the early 2000s the gate length of a leading-edge transistor was below 50 nm and its gate oxide was about five atoms thick. The long-channel MOSFET of Part 8 assumes that the gate alone controls the channel and that the insulator is a perfect barrier. Both assumptions failed. The drain began to influence the channel, carriers hit a speed limit, and electrons tunnelled straight through the oxide. Rather than stopping, the industry reinvented the transistor several times: new materials, then new shapes. [1]

3. Short-Channel Effects

In a long transistor, the gate sets the height of the barrier between source and channel, and the drain merely collects what crosses. In a short one, the electric field lines from the drain reach under the gate and pull the barrier down too. The consequences:

  • Threshold roll-off: VT falls as the gate gets shorter, so tiny variations in length cause large variations in leakage.
  • Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL): VT falls further when the drain voltage is high, so off-current rises.
  • Degraded subthreshold swing: the gate controls the barrier less efficiently, so S rises above the ideal 60 mV per decade.
  • Velocity saturation: in fields above about 104 V/cm, electrons scatter so often that their drift speed saturates near 107 cm/s. The on-current then grows roughly linearly with VGS − VT, not as its square.

A useful way to think about this is the natural length λ, the distance over which the drain’s influence decays into the channel. Analysis of the electrostatics gives λ ≈ √((εSi/εox)toxtSi) for a single gate, smaller for two or more gates, and shows that short-channel effects grow as e−L/2λ. [2] A transistor behaves well if its gate is several λ long. To shrink L, one must shrink λ: thinner insulators, thinner channels, more gates.

4. Strain, High-k Dielectrics and Metal Gates

The first rescues were new materials. From its 90 nm generation in 2003, Intel deliberately strained the silicon lattice: stretching it for nMOS and compressing it, with embedded silicon–germanium, for pMOS. Strain changes the band structure and raises mobility, giving more current at the same voltage. [3]

The oxide was a harder problem. Electrons tunnel through an insulator with a probability that falls exponentially with thickness: for SiO₂, roughly tenfold for every 0.2 nm. [4] At about 1.2 nm, gate leakage was becoming a serious share of chip power. The answer was to use an insulator with a higher dielectric constant κ (“high-k”), such as hafnium oxide (κ ≈ 20–25, against 3.9 for SiO₂). Because capacitance depends on κ/t, a high-k layer five times thicker gives the same gate capacitance, and the extra thickness cuts tunnelling dramatically. The quantity that matters is the equivalent oxide thickness, EOT = thigh-k × 3.9/κ. [5] High-k materials did not work well with polysilicon gates, so they were paired with metal gates; Intel brought “high-k metal gate” transistors into production in 2007 at 45 nm. [6]

5. FinFETs: Wrapping the Gate

Materials bought time; geometry gave the decisive gain. If the channel is a thin vertical slab of silicon, a fin, a gate draped over it controls it from both sides and the top, and the thin fin has no deep substrate in which the drain’s field can sneak around. In 1989 Digh Hisamoto and colleagues at Hitachi built such a vertical double-gate transistor, called DELTA. [7] Working with Chenming Hu’s group at the University of California, Berkeley, Hisamoto and colleagues then demonstrated the self-aligned FinFET, which could be made with existing processes. [8]

Intel put FinFETs (which it called tri-gate transistors) into production at 22 nm, announced in 2011 and shipped in 2012; other manufacturers followed at 16 and 14 nm. [9] The current flows along both side walls, so a tall fin gives a wide effective channel in a small footprint. Fins come in whole numbers, so designers size transistors by choosing how many fins to use. [10]

6. Gate-All-Around Nanosheets

The logical end point is a gate that surrounds the channel completely. Turn the fin on its side, make it a thin sheet a few nanometres thick, stack several such sheets, and fill the space between them with gate: a gate-all-around (GAA) transistor. In 2017 researchers from IBM, GlobalFoundries and Samsung showed that stacked nanosheets could succeed FinFETs. [11] Nanosheets offer the best electrostatic control, smaller λ, and a width that can be set continuously by the lithography rather than in whole fins. Samsung began mass production of a 3 nm-class process with GAA transistors in 2022, and other leading manufacturers have followed with their 2 nm-class processes.

Making nanosheets is formidable: alternating layers of silicon and silicon–germanium are grown, patterned, and then the silicon–germanium is selectively etched away and replaced with gate dielectric and metal, all in gaps a few nanometres wide.

7. What “3 nm” Really Means

Process generations used to be named after a real dimension, roughly the gate length or half the pitch of the densest wires. That link broke in the late 1990s, when gate lengths shrank faster than the names, and broke the other way with FinFETs, when names kept shrinking faster than any dimension. In a so-called 3 nm process, the distance between neighbouring gates is around 45–50 nm and the tightest metal wires are spaced around 20–25 nm apart; nothing on the chip is 3 nm long except perhaps the thickness of a nanosheet or gate dielectric. The International Roadmap for Devices and Systems instead tracks gate pitch, metal pitch and the number of transistor tracks per standard cell. [12] The useful measure for users is transistor density and the power and performance it buys, and that has continued to improve, more slowly than before.

8. Beyond Nanosheets

Several ideas aim to keep density rising:

  • Complementary FET (CFET): stack an nMOS nanosheet transistor directly on top of a pMOS one, roughly halving the area of a logic cell. [13]
  • Backside power delivery: move the thick power wires to the back of the wafer, below the transistors, freeing the front-side wiring for signals and reducing voltage drops. Intel and TSMC have announced processes using it in the mid-2020s.
  • Two-dimensional semiconductors: materials such as molybdenum disulphide are only three atoms thick, so a channel made of one layer has an extremely small λ. Single-layer MoS₂ transistors were demonstrated in 2011, but making them reliably at wafer scale with good contacts is still a research problem. [14]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): equivalent oxide thickness

A gate stack uses 3.0 nm of HfO₂ with κ = 20. What is its EOT? What thickness of SiO₂ would give the same capacitance?

Solution

EOT = 3.0 × 3.9/20 ≈ 0.59 nm, which is the same thing as the thickness of SiO₂ with equal capacitance. That would be about two atomic layers of SiO₂, which would leak enormously; the 3 nm HfO₂ film does not. (Real stacks also include a thin SiO₂ interface layer, adding a few tenths of a nanometre.)

Problem 2 (easy): width per footprint

A FinFET has fins 50 nm tall and 7 nm wide at a 30 nm pitch. What effective width do three fins provide, and how does it compare with a planar transistor occupying the same 90 nm of chip width?

Solution

Each fin contributes 2 × 50 + 7 = 107 nm, so three give 321 nm, about 3.6 times the 90 nm of a planar device in the same footprint. Taller fins give even more, but are harder to etch and fill.

Problem 3 (medium): velocity saturation

For a short-channel transistor in full velocity saturation, ID ≈ W Cox(VGS − VT)vsat. With Cox = 1.7 µF/cm², an overdrive of 0.5 V and vsat = 107 cm/s, find the current per µm of width. Compare with the long-channel square law for L = 20 nm and μ = 200 cm²/(V s).

Solution

Velocity-saturated: 10−4 cm × 1.7 × 10−6 × 0.5 × 107 ≈ 0.85 mA/µm, close to real high-performance devices. Square law: ½ × 200 × 1.7 × 10−6 × (1 µm/20 nm = 50) × 0.25 ≈ 2.1 mA/µm. The long-channel model overestimates by a factor of 2.5: shrinking L no longer increases current as fast as it used to.

Problem 4 (medium): the cost of DIBL

A transistor has a DIBL of 100 mV/V and a subthreshold swing of 80 mV/decade. By how much does its off-current rise when the drain voltage goes from 0.05 V to 0.85 V?

Solution

VT falls by 100 mV/V × 0.8 V = 80 mV. At 80 mV per decade, the off-current rises by 1080/80 = 10 times. A GAA device with 30 mV/V DIBL and 65 mV/decade would see only 1024/65 ≈ 2.3 times.

Problem 5 (harder): tunnelling exponents

The tunnelling probability through a barrier of height φ and thickness t is roughly e−2κt with κ = √(2m*φ)/ħ. For SiO₂ (φ = 3.1 eV, m* = 0.4m₀), estimate κ and the factor by which leakage rises when the oxide thins from 1.5 nm to 1.2 nm.

Solution

κ = √(2 × 0.4 × 9.11 × 10−31 × 3.1 × 1.6 × 10−19)/1.055 × 10−34 ≈ 6.0 × 10−25/1.055 × 10−34 ≈ 5.7 nm−1. Thinning by 0.3 nm multiplies the probability by e2 × 5.7 × 0.3 = e3.4 ≈ 30. The exponential is why gate leakage went from negligible to a crisis within a couple of generations, and why replacing SiO₂ became urgent.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: the last few nanometres

At gate lengths near 10 nm, a transistor’s channel holds only tens of dopant atoms and electrons, and random variations in their number and position, in line-edge roughness and in metal grain structure make nominally identical transistors differ. Direct tunnelling from source to drain sets an ultimate limit for silicon channels somewhere around 5 nm. Whether stacked CFETs can be manufactured economically, whether 2D materials can replace silicon channels, and how much further density can grow before cost, variability and heat dominate are all open. Many researchers expect future gains to come increasingly from three-dimensional stacking and packaging rather than from shrinking individual transistors.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “3 nm chips have 3 nm transistors”

The myth: a 3 nm process makes transistors 3 nm across.

The reality: the number is a generation label. Gates are spaced around 45–50 nm apart and the channel length is over 10 nm. Different companies’ “nodes” with the same name can differ substantially in density. The names persist because they signal roughly a new generation of density and efficiency.

Why it matters: comparing chips by node name alone is misleading; transistor density, power and performance are the meaningful measures.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Short channels suffer threshold roll-off, DIBL, worse subthreshold swing and velocity saturation.
  • These effects scale as e−L/2λ; thinner channels and more gates shrink the natural length λ.
  • Strain raised mobility (2003); high-k dielectrics with metal gates cut tunnelling leakage at the same capacitance (2007).
  • FinFETs (production 2011–12) wrap the gate on three sides; GAA nanosheets (production 2022) surround the channel.
  • Node names such as “3 nm” are labels, not dimensions; CFETs, backside power and 2D materials are candidates for what comes next.
