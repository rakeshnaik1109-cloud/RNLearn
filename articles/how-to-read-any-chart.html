1. Why Plot Data at All?

A table of numbers can hold everything there is to know about a data set and still tell you almost nothing at a glance. A good plot turns the same numbers into a picture that your visual system can read in a second: a trend, a gap, a cluster, an outlier. A bad plot can just as quickly mislead you. This series is a field guide to the plots used in science, engineering, business and machine learning: what each one shows, how to read it, when to use it, how it can fool you, and how to make it yourself in Python.

This first part covers the skills that apply to every chart: reading its anatomy, asking the right questions, understanding scales, and knowing what the eye judges well and badly. Later parts go plot by plot, from bar charts and histograms to box plots, heatmaps, diagnostic plots and the plots of machine learning. Every Python example uses made-up (synthetic) data generated in the code itself, so you can copy it, run it and change it.

3. The Anatomy of a Chart

Almost every chart has the same parts: a title, axes with labels and units, tick marks that set the scale, the data marks themselves (points, lines or bars), a legend that identifies groups, perhaps gridlines and annotations, and a note of where the data came from. Before you look at the data, read this frame.

In Python, the most widely used plotting library is matplotlib. [1] Here is how to make the chart above, with every part labelled. Each line that sets a part of the frame has a comment.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)                      # synthetic data, reproducible
months = np.arange(1, 13)
site_a = 18 + 8 * np.sin((months - 4) * np.pi / 6) + rng.normal(0, 0.8, 12)
site_b = 15 + 7 * np.sin((months - 4) * np.pi / 6) + rng.normal(0, 0.8, 12)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.2))
ax.plot(months, site_a, marker="o", label="Site A")
ax.plot(months, site_b, marker="s", label="Site B")

ax.set_title("Average monthly temperature at two sites")   # say what is shown
ax.set_xlabel("Month")                                      # what the x-axis is
ax.set_ylabel("Temperature (°C)")                           # quantity AND unit
ax.set_xticks(months, ["J", "F", "M", "A", "M", "J", "J", "A", "S", "O", "N", "D"])
ax.grid(alpha=0.3)                                          # light grid helps reading values
ax.legend(title="Location")                                 # which line is which
peak = site_a.argmax()
ax.annotate("warmest month", xy=(months[peak], site_a[peak]),
            xytext=(months[peak] + 1.5, site_a[peak] - 4),
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->"))               # point out what matters
fig.text(0.01, 0.01, "Source: synthetic data for illustration", fontsize=8, color="gray")
plt.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("temperature.png", dpi=300)                     # high resolution for print
plt.show()

4. Five Questions to Ask of Any Chart

How to read any chart
  1. What is being measured, and in what units? Read the title and both axis labels. If a unit is missing, be suspicious.
  2. What is the scale? Where does each axis start? Are the steps equal (linear) or multiplying (logarithmic)? Are two panels on the same scale?
  3. What does each mark represent? One person, an average, a total? Do error bars show the spread of the data or the uncertainty of an average (Part 7)?
  4. What is the main pattern, and what breaks it? Look for the overall trend first, then for exceptions: outliers, gaps, clusters, sudden changes.
  5. What is missing? Who chose the data and the time window? What comparison would change the story?

The statistician John Tukey, whose box plot is the subject of Part 5, made this kind of looking a discipline in its own right: exploratory data analysis, which comes before any formal test. [2]

5. Anscombe’s Quartet

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small data sets, each with eleven pairs of numbers. [3] They have the same mean of x, the same mean of y, the same variances, the same correlation and the same best-fitting straight line. Judged by their statistics, they are identical. Then look at them.

Anscombe’s point was that statistics and graphs are partners: numbers are precise, but a plot shows the shape of the data, and that shape decides which numbers make sense. A straight-line fit is reasonable for set I, wrong for set II, distorted by one point in III and meaningless in IV. This code draws the quartet and prints its statistics:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Anscombe's quartet (1973): four data sets with nearly identical summary statistics
x = [10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5]
data = {
    "I":   (x, [8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68]),
    "II":  (x, [9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]),
    "III": (x, [7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]),
    "IV":  ([8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8],
            [6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89]),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(8, 6), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, (name, (xs, ys)) in zip(axes.flat, data.items()):
    xs, ys = np.array(xs), np.array(ys)
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(xs, ys, 1)
    r = np.corrcoef(xs, ys)[0, 1]
    print(f"{name}: mean x={xs.mean():.2f}, mean y={ys.mean():.2f}, "
          f"var y={ys.var(ddof=1):.2f}, r={r:.3f}, fit y={intercept:.2f}+{slope:.3f}x")
    ax.scatter(xs, ys)
    grid = np.array([3, 20])
    ax.plot(grid, intercept + slope * grid, color="gray", lw=1)
    ax.set_title(f"Data set {name}")
fig.supxlabel("x")
fig.supylabel("y")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Linear and Logarithmic Scales

On an ordinary linear axis, equal distances mean equal differences: 0, 10, 20, 30. On a logarithmic axis, equal distances mean equal ratios: 1, 10, 100, 1,000. Log scales are the right choice when values span several orders of magnitude, or when what matters is the rate of growth, because anything growing by a constant percentage, such as money with compound interest or an epidemic in its early stage, becomes a straight line.

The two views answer different questions. The linear plot shows how large the numbers are now; the log plot shows how fast they are growing. A common misreading is to see the steep end of a linear curve as “acceleration” when the growth rate has actually fallen.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

days = np.arange(0, 120)
# synthetic growth: doubles every 7 days, then every 20 days after day 60
rate = np.where(days < 60, np.log(2) / 7, np.log(2) / 20)
cases = 10 * np.exp(np.cumsum(rate))

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
ax1.plot(days, cases)
ax1.set_title("Linear scale")
ax2.plot(days, cases)
ax2.set_yscale("log")                    # equal steps now mean equal *ratios*
ax2.set_title("Logarithmic scale")
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_xlabel("Day")
    ax.set_ylabel("Count")
    ax.axvline(60, color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Where Should the Axis Start?

A bar represents a quantity by its length, so a bar chart whose axis does not start at zero distorts the comparison. Edward Tufte defined the lie factor as the size of an effect shown in a graphic divided by its size in the data: an honest chart has a lie factor of 1. [5]

The rule is different for line charts and dot plots, which encode values by position rather than length. There, zooming in on the range of the data is often the right thing to do, so that real variation is visible, provided the axis is clearly labelled. The question to ask is always: does the eye read this mark as a length, or as a position?

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

labels = ["Product A", "Product B"]
values = [50, 54]                        # B is 8% larger than A

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3.5))
ax1.bar(labels, values, color=["#0072B2", "#E69F00"])
ax1.set_ylim(48, 55)                     # truncated: B looks 3 times taller
ax1.set_title("Axis starts at 48 (misleading)")
ax2.bar(labels, values, color=["#0072B2", "#E69F00"])
ax2.set_ylim(0, 60)                      # bars should start at zero
ax2.set_title("Axis starts at 0 (honest)")
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_ylabel("Sales (thousand units)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. What the Eye Reads Best

Some ways of encoding a number are easier to read than others. In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill asked people to compare pairs of values shown in different ways and measured their errors. [6] People were most accurate judging positions along a common scale, followed by positions on separate but identical scales, then lengths, then angles and slopes, then areas, then volumes and colour shading. Test yourself:

This ranking explains much of the advice in this series. Dot plots and bar charts use position and length, so they are read accurately. Pie charts use angle and area, and bubble charts use area, so small differences get lost. Colour is best kept for showing categories, or for heatmaps where approximate values are enough.

10. Colour, Shape and Clutter

Colour. About one man in twelve and one woman in two hundred has some form of colour-vision deficiency, most often difficulty telling red from green. Use a palette designed to stay distinguishable, such as the Okabe–Ito palette, [7] and do not rely on colour alone: add labels, line styles or marker shapes. For continuous values, use a perceptually uniform colour map such as viridis, in which equal steps in the data look like equal steps in colour; the old “rainbow” map creates false boundaries. [8]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Okabe–Ito colours: distinguishable by people with common colour-vision deficiencies
okabe_ito = ["#0072B2", "#E69F00", "#009E73", "#D55E00", "#CC79A7", "#56B4E9", "#F0E442"]

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
for i, colour in enumerate(okabe_ito[:5]):
    ax1.plot(np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 1, 50)) + 4 * i, color=colour, label=f"Group {i + 1}")
ax1.legend(fontsize=8)
ax1.set_title("Categories: a colour-blind-safe palette")

z = np.outer(np.linspace(0, 1, 50), np.linspace(0, 1, 50))
im = ax2.imshow(z, cmap="viridis", origin="lower")   # perceptually uniform colour map
fig.colorbar(im, ax=ax2, label="value")
ax2.set_title("Continuous values: viridis")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Aspect ratio. The shape of a line chart changes how steep its slopes look. Cleveland suggested choosing the aspect ratio so that the important line segments are tilted at about 45°, where differences in slope are easiest to see, a rule called banking to 45°. [9]

Clutter. Tufte urged chart makers to maximise the share of ink that shows data, and to remove “chartjunk”: heavy gridlines, 3D effects, decorative pictures and shading that carry no information. [5] Later research found that some decoration can make a chart more memorable without making it less accurate, [10] so the rule is not absolute, but 3D bar and pie charts genuinely distort values and are best avoided.

11. Your Plotting Toolkit

All the Python examples in this series use three libraries. matplotlib draws everything and gives full control. [1] seaborn, built on matplotlib, makes statistical plots such as box plots, violin plots and heatmaps in one line. [11] plotly makes interactive charts for the web, and is used for a few plots such as Sankey diagrams. Install them once with pip install numpy matplotlib seaborn plotly.

In matplotlib, a Figure is the whole image and an Axes is one plot inside it, with its own x-axis and y-axis. The pattern fig, ax = plt.subplots() creates both, and almost everything else is a method of ax:

# Install once:  pip install numpy matplotlib seaborn
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.arange(1, 11)
y = x ** 1.5

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4))   # a Figure (the page) holding one Axes (the plot)
ax.plot(x, y, marker="o")                 # draw on the Axes
ax.set_xlabel("x")
ax.set_ylabel("y")
ax.set_title("My first plot")
plt.tight_layout()                        # stop labels from being cut off
plt.show()

Each example in this series is complete: copy it into a file or a Jupyter notebook and run it. Change the numbers and see what happens; that is the fastest way to learn what each setting does.

12. Common Misreadings

  • Reading the shape without the scale. Two charts that look alike may have very different axes. Check the labels and the ranges.
  • Mistaking a log scale for a linear one. A log-scale line that rises gently can represent enormous growth. Look for gridlines like 1, 10, 100.
  • Trusting the summary over the picture. As Anscombe showed, a correlation or an average can hide curves, outliers and clusters.
  • Treating a truncated bar chart as honest. Bars that do not start at zero exaggerate differences.
  • Over-reading colour and area. People judge areas and shades poorly; do not draw precise conclusions from bubble sizes or colour intensity.
  • Forgetting what is not shown. The chosen time window, the groups left out and the missing data can matter more than the plotted points.

13. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: where charts came from

The legend: graphs are as old as mathematics.

The record: statistical charts are surprisingly recent. In 1644 Michael Florent van Langren plotted twelve estimates of the longitude difference between Toledo and Rome along a line, showing their spread. The Scottish engineer William Playfair invented the line chart and the bar chart in his Commercial and Political Atlas of 1786, and the pie chart in his Statistical Breviary of 1801. [12, 13]

A second legend: Florence Nightingale invented the pie chart. The record: Playfair drew pie charts more than fifty years earlier. Nightingale’s famous diagrams of 1858 are polar area diagrams: each month is a wedge with the same angle, and the area of each coloured segment shows how many soldiers died of disease, of wounds or of other causes. They showed that most British soldiers who died in the Crimean War were killed by preventable disease, and helped persuade the government to reform army hospitals. [13]

A third: Charles Joseph Minard’s 1869 map of Napoleon’s Russian campaign, showing the size of the army, its route, the dates and the falling temperature on the retreat, has been called one of the best statistical graphics ever drawn. [5]
