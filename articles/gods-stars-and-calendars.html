Gods, Stars and Calendars: The Dawn of Systematic Order

When hunting bands settled the great river valleys of Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus, China, and Mesoamerica, survival came to depend on predicting the sky and partitioning the earth. This is the story of how temple priests, royal scribes, and tax assessors turned observation into arithmetic, geometry, and the first astronomical ephemerides.

1. Counting Grain

Picture a scribe in southern Mesopotamia (today's Iraq) about 4,000 years ago, sitting outside a granary with a lump of wet clay and a pointed reed. The barley harvest has just come in by boat along the canal. Before it can be handed out to bakers, brewers and field workers, someone has to work out how much grain is in the store and how many daily rations it will make. A standard ration bowl, the sila, held a little under a litre [1].

The scribe presses the reed into the clay: an upright wedge for each unit, a sideways wedge for each ten. This isn't poetry or philosophy. It's bookkeeping.

But this everyday chore is where systematic science starts. For hundreds of thousands of years people had lived in small family groups, shared food straight away and kept everything they needed to know in their heads. Farming, which began around 10,000 BCE in the "Fertile Crescent" of the Middle East, created a new problem: a surplus that had to be managed.

Grain had to be stored for the lean months. Canals had to be dug and cleaned by big teams. After floods, fields had to be measured again and taxed. Planting had to happen at the right time of year. That was too much for anyone to remember, so the early states needed a memory outside people's heads, made of numbers. In Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China and Central America, the first organised sciences grew not from idle curiosity but from the practical need to count, measure and predict.

2. The River and the Star: Egypt's Calendar

Nowhere did the sky matter more for survival than in Egypt. The Greek historian Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile", and he was right: it hardly ever rains there, so farming depended on the river's yearly flood. Every summer, monsoon rains in the highlands of Ethiopia swelled the Blue Nile and sent a surge of water and rich black mud down the valley. From about mid-July to September the fields were under water [3]. The Egyptians called this season Akhet, the Inundation.

Plant too early and the flood washes the seed away. Plant too late and the sun bakes the mud hard before the crop can grow. Knowing the date was a matter of eating or starving.

The obvious clock is the Moon, but a calendar of Moon months has a problem. Twelve lunar months add up to only about 354 days, roughly 11 days short of a full year of seasons. After three years such a calendar is more than a month out, and the festivals start drifting through the seasons.

The Egyptians found a better marker: Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, which they worshipped as the goddess Sopdet (the Greeks called it Sothis) [3].

For about 70 days each year Sirius is lost in the Sun's glare. Then, around 17–19 July in ancient times, it reappeared: for a few minutes just before dawn it could be seen low in the east, before the brightening sky washed it out. Astronomers call this first appearance the heliacal rising ("rising with the Sun"), and it came just as the flood was due [3].

Watching for this year after year showed the Egyptians that the year is about 365 days long. Early in the third millennium BCE they set up a civil calendar of 12 months of 30 days, plus 5 extra holiday days at the end, making 365 days [3]. The real year is about a quarter of a day longer, and there was no leap day, so this calendar slipped against the seasons by one day every four years. It took about 1,460 years to slip all the way round and line up again. Historians call this the Sothic cycle [3].

3. Counting in Sixties: Mesopotamian Numbers

Egypt had the practical calendar. Mesopotamia had the most powerful number system of the ancient world.

Around 3200 BCE in the city of Uruk, accountants kept track of goods with small clay tokens (cones, balls, discs), sealed inside hollow clay balls as a receipt. Eventually they realised they didn't need the tokens inside: they could just press the token shapes into the outside, and then onto a flat tablet. Writing began as a way of recording numbers.

By about 2000 BCE, Babylonian scribal schools were using a number system based on 60 [1]. Why 60? We count in tens because we have ten fingers, but 10 can only be split evenly into halves and fifths. Try dividing 10 by 3 and you get 3.333..., which never ends.

Sixty is much friendlier. It divides evenly by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30 and 60. So in base 60, a half, a third, a quarter, a fifth, a sixth and so on all come out as neat, finished numbers. For anyone sharing out grain, working hours or silver, that saved a lot of awkward fractions [1].

Even cleverer was the idea of place value. In Roman numerals, X always means ten wherever it sits. In Babylonian numbers, as in ours, a symbol's value depends on its column. In our system a "1" can mean one, ten or a hundred; in theirs a single wedge could mean 1, 60, 3,600 or 1/60. The scribe worked out which from the context [1]. Every time you read 60 minutes in an hour, 60 seconds in a minute, or 360 degrees in a circle (6 × 60), you are using Babylonian arithmetic.

4. Rope-Stretchers: Egyptian Geometry

In Egypt, measuring land was both a religious duty and a tax matter. After the flood went down each autumn, tax officials arrived with surveyors the Greeks later called harpedonaptai, "rope-stretchers" [4]. With knotted measuring cords they put back the field boundaries that the new layer of mud had covered.

Our best window into Egyptian maths is the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied around 1550 BCE by a scribe named Ahmes from an older original [4]. It is a scroll over five metres long with 84 worked problems: sums, fractions, the volume of granaries and the area of fields.

How Ahmes measured a round field

In Problem 50, Ahmes finds the area of a round field 9 khet across (a khet was 100 cubits, about 52 metres):

"Take away 1/9 of the diameter, namely 1. The remainder is 8. Multiply 8 times 8; it makes 64. The area is 64 setat."
In other words: knock a ninth off the width and square what's left. Written in modern symbols: $$A = \left( \tfrac{8}{9} d \right)^2 \approx 3.16\, r^2$$ We would write the area as $\pi r^2$, so the Egyptian rule works as if $\pi$ were about 3.16. The true value is 3.14, so the rule was out by only about 0.6% [4].

For the slopes of pyramids, Egyptian builders used the seked [4]. It wasn't an angle in degrees. It was how far the face steps in for every cubit it rises, measured in palms and fingers (a cubit was 7 palms, and a palm 4 fingers). A mason could check it directly against a block with a simple wooden set square and a plumb line.

5. Plimpton 322: A Babylonian Number Puzzle

At Columbia University in New York there is a small, broken clay tablet called Plimpton 322, named after the collector George Arthur Plimpton, who bought it from a dealer in the 1920s. It probably comes from the ancient city of Larsa in southern Iraq, from about 1800 BCE, and carries four columns of numbers in fifteen rows [1, 2].

In 1945 Otto Neugebauer and Abraham Sachs worked out what two of the columns are: the sides of right-angled triangles with whole-number lengths, like the familiar 3, 4, 5. These are called Pythagorean triples, because they fit the rule $$\text{width}^2 + \text{length}^2 = \text{diagonal}^2$$ that we name after Pythagoras, who lived more than a thousand years later [1]. The surprise was how big the numbers are. Row 4 has a width of 12,709 and a diagonal of 18,541. Nobody finds numbers like that by drawing triangles and measuring them. They must have been calculated [1, 2].

Over the years people claimed the tablet was Babylonian trigonometry, 1,500 years before the Greeks, or some kind of astronomical calculator. In 2001 Eleanor Robson, then at Oxford, looked again at the tablet in the light of how Babylonian scribes were actually taught [2]. She argued that it is most likely a teacher's list from a scribal school. Babylonian students learned to work with pairs of numbers that multiply to 1 (like 2 and 1/2, or in base 60, 2 and 30/60). Starting from two such numbers, there is a simple recipe for making whole-number triangles: $$\text{width} = p^2 - q^2, \quad \text{length} = 2pq, \quad \text{diagonal} = p^2 + q^2$$ The rows are arranged in order, with the triangles getting steadily flatter, from about 45° down to about 32° [2]. No mystery, then, but something just as impressive: one of the earliest known systematic methods for generating whole-number solutions.

6. The Indus Valley: Standard Bricks and Weights

At the same time, along the Indus river in what is now Pakistan and north-western India, there was another great civilisation: the Indus Valley or Harappan civilisation (about 2600–1900 BCE) [5].

We still can't read their writing, but their buildings and objects show a love of standards. Cities such as Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira and the port of Lothal were spread over a region larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia put together. Yet across all of it, builders made bricks in the same proportions: thickness : width : length = 1 : 2 : 4, typically about 7 × 14 × 28 cm. Those proportions make it easy to lay bricks in overlapping rows, some lengthways and some crossways, for a strong wall [5].

Their weights are even more impressive [5]. Archaeologists have found thousands of small, neatly polished cubes of hard banded stone (chert). When Jonathan Mark Kenoyer and others weighed them, they found a two-part system:

  • Small weights that double: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, with the smallest unit about 0.86 grams. These were for weighing precious things such as gold and beads.
  • The everyday weight: the 16-unit weight, about 13.7 grams, which turns up in homes, workshops and city gateways everywhere.
  • Big weights in tens: for bulk goods, multiples of that standard such as 160, 200, 320 and 640, up to weights of more than 10 kilograms [5].

Weights found far apart, from Lothal on the Arabian Sea to Shortugai in northern Afghanistan, match each other remarkably well [5]. With no written rulebook that we can read, Harappan weight-makers kept one system across a huge area for centuries.

7. China: Oracle Bones and Shadow Sticks

In the late 1800s, old bones carved with marks were being sold in Chinese medicine shops as "dragon bones", to be ground into powder. The often-told story is that in 1899 the scholar Wang Yirong recognised the marks as writing. Whatever the exact details, the bones turned out to be turtle shells and ox shoulder blades carrying an early form of Chinese script. They are the oracle bones from Anyang, the last capital of the Shang dynasty, dating to about 1250–1046 BCE [6].

Shang diviners asked the gods questions by heating a bone until it cracked, then reading the shape of the cracks to answer questions about the king's health, wars and the weather for the harvest. The question and sometimes the outcome were carved onto the bone. Mixed in with these are the oldest confirmed sky records from East Asia [6].

Shang scribes already counted in tens. Unlike Roman numerals, they had separate signs for 1 to 9 and signs for tens, hundreds, thousands and ten thousands, so they could write numbers up to about 30,000 quite compactly [6].

The bones record eclipses of the Sun and Moon, some of which can be matched to real eclipses calculated today, and possibly new stars and comets, although the reading of several famous inscriptions is still argued over [6]. To track the seasons, Chinese astronomers used a simple upright post called a gnomon (biao). The midday shadow is longest on the winter solstice and shortest on the summer solstice, so measuring it day by day pins down those dates. Centuries of such measurements later fed into the Sifen calendar, which took the year as 365¼ days.

8. The Maya and Their Calendars

On the other side of the world, with no contact with Egypt or Mesopotamia, the peoples of Mexico and Central America (the Olmec, Zapotec and Maya, from about 1200 BCE to 900 CE) built their own remarkable calendars [7].

They counted in twenties, on fingers and toes, using place value like the Babylonians. With just three signs (a shell for zero, a dot for one and a bar for five), Maya scribes could write numbers in the millions [7]. Crucially, they had a zero. The earliest calendar date that needs a zero place is from 36 BCE, on a stone at Chiapa de Corzo carved before the Classic Maya, and the Maya later wrote zero routinely. This zero was invented completely separately from the Indian zero that eventually reached the rest of the world.

The calendar ran on two cycles turning together, like two gear wheels:

  • The Tzolk'in (sacred count): 260 days, made by pairing the numbers 1 to 13 with 20 named days. Nobody is sure why 260; it is close to the length of a human pregnancy (about 266 days), and in the far south of the region it matches the gap between the two days each year when the Sun passes straight overhead.
  • The Haab' (solar year): 365 days, made of 18 months of 20 days plus 5 unlucky extra days called the Wayeb' (18 × 20 + 5 = 365) [7].

How long until both wheels are back where they started? You need a number of days that both 260 and 365 divide into, and the smallest is $$\frac{260 \times 365}{5} = 18{,}980\text{ days} = 52\text{ years}$$ (dividing by 5 because 5 goes into both). This 52-year Calendar Round was like a "century" for the peoples of the region. Among the Aztecs of central Mexico, when a 52-year cycle ended, every fire was put out and relit from a single new flame, the New Fire ceremony [7].

The Dresden Codex, a Maya book of bark paper now in Germany, contains famous tables for the planet Venus on pages 46–50 [7]. Maya astronomers followed Venus as morning star and evening star, and knew that five of its cycles come to almost exactly eight years: $$5 \times 583.92 = 2{,}919.6 \text{ days} \approx 8 \times 365.24 = 2{,}921.9 \text{ days}$$ To deal with the gap of about 2.3 days every eight years, they built corrections into the tables, which kept them in step with the sky for centuries [7].

9. Babylon's Sky-Watchers: Reading Omens

Back in Mesopotamia, in the first millennium BCE, something important happened: people started recording the sky systematically, night after night, for centuries.

The reason wasn't curiosity. It was fear. The kings of Assyria and Babylon believed the sky was where the gods wrote messages. An eclipse, Mars coming close to the Moon, or Venus vanishing earlier than expected could warn of plague, a failed harvest or the death of the king [8].

So the court employed trained observers in cities such as Babylon, Uruk and Nineveh, who watched the sky every night and wrote to the king about what they saw [8]. Their handbook was a huge collection called Enuma Anu Enlil: about 70 clay tablets listing some 7,000 sky omens and what each was thought to mean.

One part of it is the Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa (tablet 63), which lists when Venus disappeared and reappeared over 21 years in the reign of King Ammisaduqa of Babylon, in the 17th century BCE [8]. The scribes noted the date Venus vanished in the evening sky, how long it stayed hidden, and the date it reappeared in the east before dawn.

Here is the irony. Because the scribes believed the gods' will was written in the sky, they recorded what they saw with great care. By the 7th century BCE (the oldest surviving entry is from 652 BCE) Babylonian astronomers were keeping the Astronomical Diaries: a regular log of the Moon and planets, eclipses, the weather, the river level and even market prices. It went on for more than six hundred years [8], one of the longest-running series of observations in history.

10. Finding the Cycles

Keep careful records for long enough and patterns appear. Events that looked like the gods' whims turn out to repeat on a schedule.

Around 1000 BCE, Babylonian scribes put together MUL.APIN ("The Plough Star"), a two-tablet handbook that is often called the first astronomy textbook [8]. It lists stars and constellations, the dates they rise and set, the length of shadows through the year, and how night length changes with the seasons, measured with water clocks.

The long records also showed that eclipses don't strike at random. They come back in a cycle of 223 lunar months, about 18 years and 11 days. After that time the Sun, Earth and Moon line up again in almost the same way, giving a very similar eclipse. Today we call this the Saros cycle [8, 9].

For everyday life, the bigger prize was fixing the calendar, with the 19-year cycle. We call it the Metonic cycle after the Greek astronomer Meton of Athens, who may have learned it from Babylonian sources [8, 9].

Why 19 years works

A lunar month (new Moon to new Moon) is 29.53059 days, and a year of seasons is 365.2422 days. Now compare: $$235 \text{ months} \times 29.53059 = 6{,}939.69 \text{ days}$$ $$19 \text{ years} \times 365.2422 = 6{,}939.60 \text{ days}$$ They differ by only about two hours. Nineteen years of 12 months would be 228 months, so to reach 235 you add 7 extra months spread through the 19 years. Do that, and a Moon calendar stays in step with the seasons to within about two hours every 19 years [8, 9].

In the last few centuries BCE, Babylonian astronomers associated with names such as Nabû-rimanni and Kidinnu turned astronomy into pure calculation [8]. They had no geometry of the heavens and no picture of how the planets moved in space. Using only arithmetic on clay tablets (numbers that step up and down, or rise and fall in a zigzag, in regular patterns), they worked out the Moon's changing speed, predicted eclipses to within hours, and forecast where the planets would be.

How Did They Know? Telling Time by Shadow and Water

With no mechanical clocks, how did priests in Egypt and Mesopotamia tell the time? They used shadows by day and water by night [3].

1. The shadow stick

Put an upright post (a gnomon) on flat ground and its shadow swings round as the Sun crosses the sky. The shadow is shortest at local noon, which gives you a precise moment each day. And the line from the post to the tip of that shortest shadow points due north–south, so builders could line up temples and pyramids without a compass [3, 6].

2. The water clock

A shadow stick is useless at night or under cloud, yet Egyptian priests had rituals to perform at set hours of the night. Their answer was the water clock (later called a clepsydra, Greek for "water thief"): a pot that slowly drains through a small hole [3].

The oldest one that survives was found at the temple of Karnak and dates to the reign of Amenhotep III (about 1400 BCE). It is a stone pot with a tiny hole near the bottom. But there is a snag. Water runs out faster when the pot is full, because there is more water pressing down, and slower as it empties. (Physicists write the speed as $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, where $h$ is the depth of water.) In a straight-sided pot the level would fall quickly at first and slowly later, so evenly spaced hour marks would be wrong.

The Egyptians fixed this by giving the pot sloping sides, wider at the top (at about 70°) [3]. When the pot is full, the water spreads over a wide area, so the fast flow lowers the level only slowly; lower down the pot is narrower, which makes up for the slower flow. Inside are twelve columns of marks, one for each month, because the length of the night changes through the year [3].

Legend and Record: Did Aliens Build the Great Pyramid?

The popular story: For more than a century, books and TV programmes have claimed that the Great Pyramid of Giza (built for the pharaoh Khufu around 2560 BCE) holds secret, even alien, knowledge. They say it is lined up with true north too accurately (to within about 4 minutes of arc, roughly a fifteenth of a degree) to have been done without modern lasers, that its proportions hide the speed of light or the distance to the Sun, and that moving its 2.3 million blocks needed some lost technology.

What the evidence shows: In 2013 the French archaeologist Pierre Tallet made one of the great finds in Egyptology. At Wadi al-Jarf, an ancient harbour on the Red Sea, his team found the Diary of Merer, the oldest known written papyrus [10].

Merer was a middle-ranking official, an inspector in charge of a team of about forty men, in the 27th year of Khufu's reign. His diary is a work log, and it's wonderfully ordinary: loading fine white limestone from the Tura quarries onto boats, sailing it along canals to a harbour at Giza, and collecting grain rations from the royal stores [10]. This is how the pyramid got its outer casing: by boat, with a lot of organised labour.

And the alignment? In 2000 the British Egyptologist Kate Spence suggested a simple method [11]. In Khufu's time there was no bright pole star. But two stars, Kochab (in the Little Bear) and Mizar (in the Plough, or Big Dipper), sat on opposite sides of the true pole. When a hanging plumb line passed through both of them, it pointed to true north. Because the Earth's axis slowly wobbles, this trick drifts over the decades, and Spence showed that the small errors in pyramids built before and after Khufu change in just the way that drift predicts. That is strong support for the star method, though other methods, such as ones using the Sun, are still discussed [11]. The Great Pyramid is a monument not to aliens but to human organisation and practical geometry.

Philosophy Corner: Can Religion and Science Work Together?

A common modern view is that science and religion are natural enemies: where people believe in gods there can be no science, and where science arrives, belief must fade.

The ancient world doesn't fit that picture. In Babylon, Egypt, China and Central America, religion was what paid for, organised and justified careful observation [1, 8].

Think of a Babylonian astronomer-priest. He didn't watch Jupiter to understand how planets move. He watched it because Jupiter was the god Marduk, protector of Babylon, and if Marduk changed course the fate of the kingdom was at stake. Far from making him careless, his belief made accuracy a sacred duty. A scribe who got a date wrong hadn't just made a sum error; he had misheard the gods.

But there was a limit. Babylonian astronomers could predict an eclipse to within hours using number patterns, yet they never asked: what actually are the Sun and Moon, and what makes them move? Their astronomy predicted and described, but didn't explain [1, 8]. The tables said when; the reasons were left to stories about Marduk, Ra or the Maya hero twins.

Moving from when something will happen to why it happens, using only natural causes and leaving the gods out, needed a new way of thinking. That happened on the busy trading coasts of the Aegean Sea, in Greece and western Turkey.

15. What We Still Use

Look around and you will find these ancient river-valley cultures in everyday life.

The 60 minutes in an hour and 60 seconds in a minute come from Babylonian base-60 arithmetic. The 360 degrees of a circle come from Babylonian astronomy, which split the Sun's yearly path through the stars into 12 signs of 30 degrees. Our 365-day year goes back to the Egyptians who watched Sirius rise over the Nile. Place-value numbers were worked out in Babylon, and zero was invented separately by the Maya, though the zero we use came to us from India (Part 4). And the idea of one standard weight used everywhere was already practised by the weight-makers of the Indus.

These people built humanity's first book of records. In the next part we follow trade across the Mediterranean to the harbours of Ionia, on the coast of today's Turkey, where a few thinkers asked a bold new question: can we explain how the world works without bringing in the gods at all?
