Histograms and Density Plots: Seeing the Shape of Your Data

The same 500 numbers can show one peak or two, depending only on the width of the bars. How to read histograms and density curves, and how to see every data point when it matters.

1. What Is a Distribution?

Ask how long customers wait, how tall people are, or how exam scores came out, and a single number is not enough. The answers vary, and the pattern of that variation, which values are common, which are rare, how spread out they are, whether there are two kinds of answer, is called the distribution. Seeing a distribution is often the most important step in any analysis, because it decides which summaries make sense and which tests are valid.

This part covers the plots for one variable: the histogram, the density curve, and plots that show every individual value. Part 5 continues with box plots, violin plots and their relatives, which summarise and compare distributions.

3. The Histogram

A histogram divides the range of the data into intervals called bins and draws a bar for each, whose height is the number of values in that bin. Unlike a bar chart, the bars touch, because the bins cover a continuous scale. Karl Pearson introduced the word in lectures around 1891 and used it in print in 1895. [1]

How to read a histogram
  1. Centre: where is the bulk of the data?
  2. Spread: how wide is it, and are there values far from the rest?
  3. Shape: one peak or more? Symmetric or skewed? Flat?
  4. Gaps and spikes: do values cluster at round numbers, or is there a cliff where a limit was applied?
  5. The vertical axis: counts, percentages or density? With unequal bin widths, only density is honest.

In matplotlib, ax.hist takes the data and the number of bins (or their edges). With density=True the bars are rescaled so that their total area is 1, which allows histograms of different sample sizes to be compared:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
# synthetic waiting times (minutes) from two kinds of customer: a bimodal mixture
times = np.concatenate([rng.normal(12, 3, 300), rng.normal(25, 4, 200)])

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.6), sharey=False)
for ax, bins in zip(axes, [5, 25, 120]):
    ax.hist(times, bins=bins, color="#0072B2", edgecolor="white")
    ax.set_title(f"{bins} bins")
    ax.set_xlabel("Waiting time (minutes)")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Number of customers")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# density=True rescales so the total area is 1 (needed when bins have unequal widths)
fig2, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.6))
ax.hist(times, bins="fd", density=True, color="#0072B2", edgecolor="white")
ax.set_xlabel("Waiting time (minutes)")
ax.set_ylabel("Density (per minute)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. How Many Bins?

There is no single right bin width, but there are good starting points. Sturges’ rule (1926) uses log2(n) + 1 bins for n observations; [2] it works for small, bell-shaped samples but uses too few bins for large or skewed ones. Scott’s rule (1979) sets the width from the standard deviation, [3] and the Freedman–Diaconis rule (1981) uses the interquartile range, which is not thrown off by outliers. [4] NumPy offers all of them, and its default bins="auto" picks between Sturges and Freedman–Diaconis.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.lognormal(mean=3, sigma=0.5, size=800)          # skewed synthetic data

rules = ["sturges", "scott", "fd"]                       # three classic rules
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.5), sharey=True)
for ax, rule in zip(axes, rules):
    edges = np.histogram_bin_edges(x, bins=rule)
    ax.hist(x, bins=edges, color="#009E73", edgecolor="white")
    ax.set_title(f"{rule}: {len(edges) - 1} bins")
    ax.set_xlabel("Value")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Count")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Treat these rules as a first guess. Then try a width half as big and one twice as big; a feature that survives all three is probably real.

5. Shapes of Distributions

A few shapes come up again and again, and recognising them tells you a lot about the process behind the data.

Strongly right-skewed data, such as incomes, city sizes or file sizes, crowd into the left of a histogram with a long tail to the right. Plotting them on a logarithmic axis with bins that are equally spaced on that axis spreads out the crowded part and often reveals a bell shape (Part 1 explains log scales):

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
income = rng.lognormal(mean=10.3, sigma=0.8, size=5000)   # synthetic, strongly right-skewed

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.8))
ax1.hist(income, bins=60, color="#CC79A7", edgecolor="white")
ax1.axvline(np.mean(income), color="black", ls="--", label=f"mean {np.mean(income):,.0f}")
ax1.axvline(np.median(income), color="black", label=f"median {np.median(income):,.0f}")
ax1.legend()
ax1.set_title("Linear axis: a long right tail")
bins = np.logspace(np.log10(income.min()), np.log10(income.max()), 50)   # equal steps in log
ax2.hist(income, bins=bins, color="#CC79A7", edgecolor="white")
ax2.set_xscale("log")
ax2.set_title("Log axis with log-spaced bins")
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_xlabel("Annual income")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Density Curves

A kernel density estimate (KDE) is a smooth alternative to the histogram, proposed by Murray Rosenblatt in 1956 and developed by Emanuel Parzen in 1962. [5, 6] It places a small, smooth bump (the kernel, usually a bell curve) on every observation and adds them up. The width of the bumps, the bandwidth, controls the smoothness in the same way as the bin width of a histogram. [7]

Density curves are easier to overlay than histograms and do not depend on where bins start. Two cautions: the vertical axis is a density, not a count or a probability (the area under the curve between two values is the probability), and the curve can spill beyond natural limits, such as negative waiting times. The code below uses seaborn and SciPy: [8, 9]

# pip install numpy scipy matplotlib seaborn
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
times = np.concatenate([rng.normal(12, 3, 300), rng.normal(25, 4, 200)])

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.8))

# seaborn: histogram with a density curve on top
sns.histplot(times, stat="density", bins=30, color="#CCCCCC", ax=ax1)
sns.kdeplot(times, color="#0072B2", lw=2, ax=ax1)
ax1.set_title("Histogram + kernel density estimate")

# scipy: compare bandwidths (bw_method scales the default bandwidth)
grid = np.linspace(0, 40, 400)
for bw, col in [(0.1, "#D55E00"), (None, "#0072B2"), (1.0, "#009E73")]:
    kde = gaussian_kde(times, bw_method=bw)
    ax2.plot(grid, kde(grid), color=col, label=f"bw_method={bw}")
ax2.legend()
ax2.set_title("Too narrow, default, too wide")
for ax in (ax1, ax2):
    ax.set_xlabel("Waiting time (minutes)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Seeing Every Point: Rug, Strip and Dot Plots

Histograms and densities summarise; sometimes it is better to show every observation. A rug plot draws a tick for each value along the axis. A strip plot draws a dot for each value, with a little random vertical jitter so that ties do not hide each other. A dot plot in Leland Wilkinson’s sense stacks dots like a histogram made of individual observations. [10]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
scores = np.round(rng.normal(65, 12, 150))              # synthetic exam scores (whole numbers)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(8, 5), sharex=True)
axes[0].plot(scores, np.zeros_like(scores), "|", ms=25, color="#0072B2")   # rug plot
axes[0].set_title("Rug plot: one tick per value (ties hide each other)")
axes[1].scatter(scores, rng.uniform(-0.3, 0.3, scores.size), s=12, alpha=0.6)
axes[1].set_title("Strip plot with jitter (random vertical offset)")
sns.swarmplot(x=scores, ax=axes[2], size=3.5)              # beeswarm: no overlap
axes[2].set_title("Beeswarm (more in Part 5)")
for ax in axes:
    ax.set_yticks([])
axes[2].set_xlabel("Score")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

John Tukey’s stem-and-leaf display goes further: it is a histogram written with the data’s own digits, so that every value can be read back. [11] It needs no graphics at all:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
ages = np.sort(np.round(rng.normal(38, 11, 40)).astype(int))   # synthetic ages

# Tukey's stem-and-leaf display: stem = tens digit, leaves = units digits
stems = {}
for a in ages:
    stems.setdefault(a // 10, []).append(a % 10)
for stem in range(min(stems), max(stems) + 1):
    leaves = "".join(str(d) for d in stems.get(stem, []))
    print(f"{stem:>2} | {leaves}")
print("Key: 3 | 7 means 37")

8. Comparing Several Distributions

Comparing the distributions of several groups is one of the most common tasks in analysis. Overlaid histograms work for two groups; beyond that, colours mix and lines tangle. Small multiples (Part 2) and ridgeline plots, which stack density curves with a slight overlap, scale much better. [12]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
months = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun", "Jul", "Aug", "Sep", "Oct", "Nov", "Dec"]
# synthetic daily maximum temperatures for each month
temps = [rng.normal(8 + 10 * np.sin((m - 3) * np.pi / 6), 3, 31) for m in range(12)]

grid = np.linspace(-10, 35, 300)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 6))
for i, (name, t) in enumerate(zip(months, temps)):
    y = gaussian_kde(t)(grid)
    offset = (11 - i) * 0.08                             # January at the top
    colour = plt.cm.coolwarm((t.mean() + 2) / 20)        # colour by warmth
    ax.fill_between(grid, offset, offset + y, color=colour, alpha=0.85, lw=0)
    ax.plot(grid, offset + y, color="white", lw=1)
    ax.text(-11, offset + 0.005, name, ha="right", va="bottom", fontsize=9)
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.set_xlabel("Daily maximum temperature (°C)")
ax.set_title("Ridgeline plot: one density per month")
for side in ("left", "right", "top"):
    ax.spines[side].set_visible(False)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. Common Misreadings

  • Trusting one bin width. Peaks and gaps can be artefacts of the bins. Try several widths.
  • Reading a density as a probability. A density curve can exceed 1; probabilities are areas under the curve.
  • Comparing count histograms of different-sized groups. A group with twice as many observations has bars twice as tall. Use percentages or densities.
  • Using the mean of a skewed or bimodal distribution as “typical”. The median, or a description of each peak, is often more honest.
  • Mistaking heaping for real peaks. People report round numbers (ages ending in 0 or 5, weights of 70 kg), which creates spikes in fine histograms.
  • Over-trusting a smooth curve from few points. A KDE of ten observations looks authoritative but is mostly the shape of the kernel.

10. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: histograms and album covers

The legend: the word “histogram” comes from “history”, because it shows the history of a variable.

The record: nobody knows for certain. Pearson introduced the word in his lectures in 1891 and used it in a paper in 1895; it is usually traced to the Greek histos, something set upright such as a ship’s mast or a loom’s beam, plus -gram, something drawn. [1] Pearson did not explain his choice.

A second legend: the famous image on the cover of the band Joy Division’s 1979 album Unknown Pleasures is abstract art. The record: it is a stack of line plots of successive radio pulses from the first pulsar discovered, CP 1919, drawn for Harold Craft’s 1970 doctoral thesis at Cornell University and later reproduced in astronomy books. [13] Ridgeline plots are sometimes nicknamed “joyplots” after it.
