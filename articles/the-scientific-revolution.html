The Clockwork Heavens and the Open Eye: The Scientific Revolution

How seventeenth-century natural philosophers dismantled the Aristotelian cosmos: from Copernican geometry and Keplerian ellipses to Galileo’s telescope, Cartesian mechanics, and Newton’s grand universal synthesis.

1. A Small, Snug Universe Breaks Open

In 1500, educated Europeans lived in a neat, cosy universe. Built from Aristotle's ideas about nature and Ptolemy's astronomy (Parts 3 and 5), it was split in two at the Moon. [5, 8]

Below the Moon was our world, made of four elements (earth, water, air and fire), where things are born, change, decay and die. Here, natural motion was up or down: heavy things fell towards the centre of the Earth, their natural home, and light things rose.

Above the Moon were the heavens, made of a fifth, unchanging substance. The Sun, Moon, planets and stars were carried round the motionless Earth on nested, transparent spheres, turning in perfect circles. This picture was more than astronomy: it gave the universe a moral order, with human beings at its centre.

Between 1543 and 1687 this picture was taken apart. In its place came a vast universe, perhaps infinite, where the same laws that make a stone fall on Earth also steer the planets round the Sun, and where nature was seen less as a living thing with purposes and more as a great, predictable machine, like clockwork.

2. Copernicus Puts the Sun in the Middle

The first crack appeared in Frombork, a cathedral town on the Baltic coast of Poland. There a church official named Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) spent more than thirty years reworking astronomy. According to a letter from his friend Tiedemann Giese, he received the first printed copy of his great book, On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, on his deathbed in May 1543. [1, 4]

Copernicus wasn't a modern rebel. In some ways he was a traditionalist. What bothered him most was Ptolemy's equant (Part 5): a trick in which a planet's motion looked steady only when seen from an empty point in space. To Copernicus this broke the ancient rule that heavenly motions must be made of steady circles.

To fix this, Copernicus put the Sun, standing still, near the centre, and gave the Earth three motions: spinning daily on its axis, going round the Sun once a year, and a slow wobble of its axis. This rearrangement gave a neat explanation of an old puzzle: retrograde motion, the way planets such as Mars sometimes seem to stop, go backwards across the stars for a while, and then carry on.

In Ptolemy's system, explaining this needed an extra circle (an epicycle) for each planet, timed to match the Sun. In Copernicus's system it happens naturally, as a matter of viewpoint. The Earth moves round the Sun on a smaller, faster track than Mars, so every so often the Earth overtakes Mars, and Mars seems to slip backwards against the distant stars, just as a slower car seems to go backwards when you overtake it on the motorway.

But Copernicus still used medieval tools: heavenly spheres, small extra circles and orbits slightly off-centre. And when the book appeared, the Lutheran minister Andreas Osiander, worried about religious trouble, added an unsigned preface saying the Sun-centred system was just a handy way to calculate, not a claim about how things really are.

3. Tycho Brahe's Castle of the Stars

One big objection held back the Sun-centred idea for decades. If the Earth swings round the Sun, travelling hundreds of millions of kilometres a year, the nearer stars should seem to shift slightly against the farther ones over the year, just as a nearby tree seems to move against distant hills when you walk past. This shift is called stellar parallax, and nobody could see it. So either the Earth wasn't moving, or the stars were so far away that the shift was too small to detect. [1]

Settling questions like this needed far more accurate measurements. They came from the Danish nobleman Tycho Brahe (1546–1601). King Frederick II gave him the island of Hven, between Denmark and Sweden, and the money to build two observatories there: Uraniborg and Stjerneborg ("Star Castle").

This was before the telescope. Tycho built huge brass instruments with precise sights and finely divided scales, and cut the errors in star positions from the 10–15 minutes of arc usual since ancient times down to 1–2 minutes of arc, close to the limit of what the naked eye can do. (A minute of arc is a sixtieth of a degree; the full Moon is about 30 minutes across.)

Two of Tycho's observations struck hard at Aristotle's unchanging heavens:

  • The new star of 1572: in November 1572 a brilliant new star appeared in Cassiopeia, brighter than Venus. Tycho measured it night after night and found it didn't shift against the other stars at all, and observers elsewhere in Europe agreed. So it wasn't something in our air; it was far out among the stars. (We now know it was a supernova, an exploding star.) The heavens could change after all.
  • The great comet of 1577: by comparing its position from different places, Tycho showed this comet was at least four times farther away than the Moon, and that its path cut right through where the spheres of Mercury and Venus were supposed to be. Solid spheres couldn't exist; the planets must move freely through space.

Still unable to see any parallax, Tycho proposed a compromise: the Tychonic system, in which the Sun and Moon go round a still Earth while the other planets go round the Sun. For predicting what we see, it works the same as Copernicus's system, and it became the favourite of many 17th-century Jesuit astronomers.

4. Kepler's Battle with Mars

In 1600 Tycho invited a poor young German mathematician with mystical leanings to join him in Prague: Johannes Kepler (1571–1630). When Tycho died suddenly in October 1601, Kepler became Imperial Mathematician to the Emperor Rudolf II and, more importantly, inherited Tycho's mass of observations, including decades of careful measurements of Mars. [1]

Kepler began what he called his "war on Mars". At first he assumed the orbit must be built from circles, and tried model after model. One came very close, matching twelve of Tycho's observations to within 2 minutes of arc. But at other points it was off by 8 minutes of arc, about a quarter of the Moon's width:

"If I had believed that we could ignore these eight minutes, I would have patched up my hypothesis accordingly. But since it was not permissible to ignore them, those eight minutes alone pointed the way to a complete reformation of astronomy."
— Johannes Kepler, Astronomia Nova (1609)

Because he trusted Tycho's data more than his own favourite ideas, Kepler gave up two thousand years of circles. In New Astronomy (1609) and The Harmony of the World (1619) he set out his three laws of planetary motion:

  1. Orbits are ellipses (1609): each planet moves in an oval, an ellipse, with the Sun at one of its two special points (foci), not at the centre.
  2. Equal areas in equal times (1609): a line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. So a planet speeds up when it is close to the Sun and slows down when it is far away.
  3. The farther, the slower (1619): the square of a planet's year is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun: $$\frac{T^2}{a^3} = \text{the same for every planet}$$ For example, Jupiter is about 5.2 times farther from the Sun than Earth, so its year is about $\sqrt{5.2^3} \approx 12$ Earth years.

Kepler didn't see these as just mathematical patterns; he wanted physical causes. Inspired by William Gilbert's 1600 book on magnets, he suggested that the Sun gives off a kind of turning force, rather like magnetism, that pushes the planets round. For the first time, astronomy was tied to physics: not just where the planets go, but why.

5. Galileo Points a Telescope at the Sky

In the summer of 1609, news reached Venice of a Dutch invention: a spyglass that made distant things look near. In Padua, the mathematics professor Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) quickly ground his own lenses and built telescopes that magnified up to about twenty times. That autumn he turned one to the night sky. [3, 9]

In March 1610 he published what he had seen in a short book, The Starry Messenger (Sidereus Nuncius). All 550 copies sold quickly, and it caused a sensation across Europe.

One discovery after another undermined the old picture:

  • The Moon is rough: Aristotle's heavens were perfect and smooth. Galileo saw mountains, plains and craters. By measuring the shadows of mountains near the line between the Moon's light and dark halves, he even estimated their heights. The Moon was a rocky world, like Earth.
  • Jupiter has moons: in January 1610 he saw four small points of light next to Jupiter. Night after night they changed position, disappeared behind the planet and came out the other side. They were moons going round Jupiter. So not everything in the sky goes round the Earth.
  • Far more stars: the planets showed as small discs, but the stars stayed points of light, and thousands of stars too faint for the eye appeared. The Milky Way turned out to be a vast crowd of faint stars. The universe was far bigger than anyone had imagined.
  • Venus has phases (autumn 1610): like the Moon, Venus goes through phases, from a large thin crescent to a small, nearly full disc. In Ptolemy's arrangement Venus always stays between us and the Sun, so it could never look nearly full. Galileo's discovery ruled out Ptolemy's arrangement, though not Tycho's system, in which Venus also goes round the Sun.

6. Galileo and the Science of Falling

Galileo's telescope discoveries were spectacular, but his deepest work was on motion here on Earth. Aristotle had taught that heavier things fall faster, in proportion to their weight (so a 10-kilogram stone falls ten times faster than a 1-kilogram one), and that anything moving needs something to keep pushing it. [3]

In his last great book, Two New Sciences, published in 1638 while he was under house arrest, Galileo rebuilt the study of motion using mathematics.

He began with a clever thought experiment. Tie a heavy cannonball to a light musket ball. If heavier things fall faster, the light ball should hold the heavy one back, so the pair falls more slowly than the cannonball alone. But the pair is heavier than the cannonball, so it should fall faster. Both can't be true. The only way out: without air resistance, everything falls at the same rate, whatever its weight.

Falling happens too fast to time without good clocks, so Galileo "slowed gravity down" by rolling polished bronze balls down a long, smooth, gently sloping wooden ramp, timing them with a water clock (see section 13). He found the basic rules of motion:

  • Distance grows with the square of the time: in twice the time, a falling or rolling ball goes four times as far; in three times the time, nine times as far.
  • The odd-number rule: in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th... equal stretches of time, the ball covers distances in the ratio 1, 3, 5, 7... (adding these up gives 1, 4, 9, 16..., the squares).
  • Thrown objects follow a curve called a parabola: a thrown ball does two things at once: it keeps moving sideways at a steady speed, and at the same time falls faster and faster. Put together, these make a parabola, one of Apollonius's curves from Part 4.

7. Galileo's Trial: More Complicated than the Legend

Galileo's clash with the Catholic Church is often told as a simple story of heroic science against blind religion. The real history is messier, involving politics, the Thirty Years' War, wounded pride and genuine scientific uncertainty. [7, 9]

In February 1616, theologians advising the Church's Holy Office judged the claim that the Sun stands still at the centre to be "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical", because it seemed to contradict a literal reading of passages in the Bible (such as Joshua 10:12, where Joshua commands the Sun to stand still). In March, Copernicus's book was suspended "until corrected", and Cardinal Robert Bellarmine formally warned Galileo: he could discuss the Sun-centred idea as a mathematical hypothesis, but not hold or defend it as the truth unless conclusive proof was found.

In 1623 Galileo's admirer Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII. A patron of arts and science, he allowed Galileo to write a book comparing the Earth-centred and Sun-centred systems, as long as he treated them even-handedly, as hypotheses.

In 1632 Galileo published his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, written in lively Italian rather than scholarly Latin, as a four-day conversation between Salviati (a brilliant supporter of Copernicus), Sagredo (an open-minded layman) and Simplicio (a stubborn follower of Aristotle). The book was anything but even-handed. Worse, Galileo put the Pope's own favourite argument, that an all-powerful God could produce what we see in ways beyond our understanding, into the mouth of Simplicio, whose name sounds like "simpleton".

Urban VIII, already under political pressure during the Thirty Years' War, felt personally betrayed. In 1633 Galileo was summoned to Rome and tried by the Inquisition. In poor health, he was made to take back his Copernican views. His book was banned, and he spent the rest of his life under house arrest at his villa at Arcetri, near Florence.

8. Two Ways to Do Science: Bacon and Descartes

While Galileo was reshaping the science of motion in Italy, two thinkers in northern Europe set out new ideas about how to gain knowledge: Francis Bacon in England and René Descartes in France. [5]

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), Lord Chancellor of England, published the Novum Organum ("The New Tool") in 1620. He attacked university philosophy for spinning theories out of words without looking at nature, and warned of four mental traps, the idols of the mind (we meet them again in Part 12):

  1. Idols of the tribe: mistakes everyone tends to make, such as seeing patterns that aren't there.
  2. Idols of the cave: personal biases from our own upbringing and education.
  3. Idols of the marketplace: confusion caused by vague words.
  4. Idols of the theatre: mistaken beliefs inherited from established systems of thought.

Bacon wanted careful, shared observation and experiment, organised to improve people's lives: "Knowledge and human power are synonymous, since the ignorance of the cause frustrates the effect." In other words, knowledge is power.

René Descartes (1596–1650), who lived mostly in the Netherlands, took a very different route: reasoning from certain first principles. In his Discourse on Method (1637) he set out to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted, and found one thing he couldn't doubt: "I think, therefore I am."

Descartes developed the mechanical philosophy. He split reality into mind (which thinks) and matter (which takes up space). Matter, he said, is made of tiny particles that affect each other only by direct contact: pushing and pressing. There is no empty space, and no mysterious pulling at a distance; the planets are carried round by huge swirls of fine invisible matter, like leaves in a whirlpool. Along with his invention of coordinate geometry (the x–y graph), this helped replace the medieval living universe with the picture of a predictable, unthinking machine. In the same work he also published the law of how light bends, which the Dutch scientist Willebrord Snell had found some years earlier (Figure 5).

9. Science Gets Organised: Societies and Journals

The 1600s brought not just new theories but a new way of organising science: the scientific society. [5]

Until then, people studying nature mostly worked alone and kept in touch by letter, a network they called the "Republic of Letters". In November 1660 a group meeting at Gresham College in London founded what became the Royal Society, given a royal charter by King Charles II in 1662. Its early members included Robert Boyle, Christopher Wren and John Wilkins, and Robert Hooke soon became its Curator of Experiments.

The Royal Society chose a bold motto: Nullius in verba, "take nobody's word for it". Truth wasn't to be settled by quoting Aristotle, religious authorities or the king, but by experiments shown in front of knowledgeable witnesses and able to be repeated. In Paris in 1666, the minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert founded the state-funded Academy of Sciences, which paid salaries and built an observatory for scientists such as Christiaan Huygens and Giovanni Domenico Cassini.

In 1665 the Royal Society's secretary, Henry Oldenburg, started the Philosophical Transactions, one of the first two scientific journals (the French Journal des sçavans began a few weeks earlier). It was edited rather than peer-reviewed in the modern sense, which came much later. Printing the date of each discovery helped settle arguments about who found something first, kept a lasting record of research, and made sharing results normal.

10. The Hidden World: Hooke and Leeuwenhoek

While the telescope opened up outer space, the microscope opened up a world just as vast in the very small. [5]

In 1665 Robert Hooke (1635–1703) published Micrographia, the first major book about the microscope. Its large fold-out engravings, drawn by Hooke himself, showed the compound eye of a fly, the sting of a bee, the patterns of frost and a huge, alarming flea.

Looking at a thin slice of cork, Hooke saw that it was made of tiny box-like spaces. They reminded him of the little compartments of a honeycomb, so he called them cells. Two centuries later, the cell would become the basic unit of biology (Part 8).

In Delft, a Dutch cloth merchant named Antoni van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) took a different approach. Instead of a microscope with several lenses, he made tiny single lenses, almost perfect glass beads, mounted between brass plates. They magnified more than 250 times, remarkably clearly. With them he discovered a whole invisible living world: single-celled creatures in pond water, bacteria in the gunk between his teeth, red blood cells moving through tiny blood vessels, and sperm cells. Nature was teeming with life too small to see.

11. Isaac Newton's Miracle Years

In the summer of 1665 the Great Plague hit London and Cambridge University closed. A 23-year-old student named Isaac Newton (1642–1727) went home to his family's farmhouse at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire. Over the next eighteen months or so, his "miracle years", he produced an astonishing burst of new ideas. [2]

In that time he developed his "method of fluxions", which we now call calculus (the mathematics of change), worked out new results in algebra, began thinking about gravity reaching out to the Moon, and started experimenting with light.

The old view was that white light is pure and simple, and that colours are made when glass or other material spoils or changes it. In 1672, in his first published paper, Newton showed this was wrong with what he called his crucial experiment.

He darkened his room, let a narrow beam of sunlight in through a hole in the shutter, and passed it through a glass prism, spreading it into a rainbow of colours on the wall. Then, using a board with a narrow slit, he picked out just one colour, say red, and sent it through a second prism. If glass created colours, the second prism should have produced new ones. It didn't: the red light was bent again, but stayed red. White light, Newton concluded, isn't simple. It is a mixture of colours, each of which bends by its own fixed amount and can't be changed into another.

12. The Principia: One Law for Heaven and Earth

In August 1684 the young astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton in Cambridge with a question that had stumped members of the Royal Society: if the Sun pulls a planet with a force that gets weaker with the square of the distance (twice as far, a quarter of the pull), what shape will the planet's path be? [2, 6, 10]

Newton answered at once: "An ellipse." Astonished, Halley asked how he knew. "Why, I have calculated it," Newton said. He couldn't find his old notes, so Halley persuaded him to work it out again. The result, published in July 1687 with Halley's help, was one of the greatest books in science: the Principia (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy).

Written in the style of Euclid's geometry, the Principia set out the basic laws of motion:

  1. First law: a thing keeps still, or keeps moving at a steady speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. (This tendency is called inertia.)
  2. Second law: a force changes an object's motion, in the direction of the force, and the bigger the force the bigger the change. Today we write it as $F = ma$: force equals mass times acceleration.
  3. Third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Push on a wall, and the wall pushes back on you.

In Book III, The System of the World, Newton set out his law of universal gravitation:

$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

Every piece of matter in the universe pulls on every other piece, with a force that grows with their masses and gets weaker with the square of the distance between them.

The reach of this one law was enormous. It explained Galileo's falling objects, the paths of cannonballs, the tides (the Moon and Sun pulling unevenly on the oceans), the slow wobble of the Earth's axis, the paths of comets, and all three of Kepler's laws. Figure 7 shows his famous thought experiment: fire a cannonball fast enough from a high mountain and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls, so it goes into orbit. That is exactly what the Moon is doing. Physics on Earth and astronomy in the heavens had become one subject, ruled by the same laws.

13. How Did They Know? Galileo's Water Clock

How did they know? Timing a ball on a ramp with water

How did Galileo time a rolling ball to a fraction of a second, decades before pendulum clocks or stopwatches? [3]

In Two New Sciences (1638), he describes his timer:

"A large vessel of water was placed in an elevated position; to the bottom of this vessel was soldered a pipe of small diameter giving a thin jet of water, which we collected in a small glass during the time of each descent... The water thus collected was weighed, after each descent, on a very accurate balance; the differences and ratios of these weights gave us the differences and ratios of the times."

Because the tank was large and the pipe thin, the water level in the tank hardly dropped during a short roll, so the water flowed out at a steady rate. That meant the weight of water collected was a direct measure of the time.

Galileo says that repeated runs never differed by more than a tenth of a pulse beat. After repeating the experiment a hundred times, with different slopes and distances, he found that the distances always matched the squares of the times very closely.

14. Legend and Record: The Leaning Tower and the Apple

Legend and Record: separating stories from evidence

Two of the most famous stories about the Scientific Revolution are not quite what they seem: [9, 2]

  • Did Galileo drop balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa? The popular story has Galileo dropping a heavy and a light ball from the tower in front of jeering professors, and both hitting the ground together.
    The record: Galileo never mentions the Leaning Tower in any of his books or surviving letters. The story first appears in a biography written in 1654 by his young admirer Vincenzo Viviani, about sixty years after the supposed event. A similar experiment really was done, but by the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin, who in 1586 dropped two lead balls of different weights from a church tower in Delft and heard them land together. Galileo reached his law of falling mainly through thought experiments and careful measurements with ramps.
  • Did an apple fall on Newton's head? The legend says an apple hit Newton on the head under a tree at Woolsthorpe, and he instantly thought of gravity.
    The record: Newton never said an apple hit him. But the apple story isn't made up: four people who talked to Newton in his old age recorded it, including his friend William Stukeley. In 1726 Stukeley wrote down Newton's own memory: "After dinner, the weather being warm, we went into the garden & drank thea under the shade of some apple trees... He told me he was just in the same situation when formerly the notion of gravitation came into his mind. 'Why should that apple always descend perpendicularly to the ground,' thought he to himself..." The apple didn't knock an idea into him; it started a thought: if the Earth's pull reaches the top of an apple tree, does it reach higher, to the top of a mountain, to the clouds, and all the way to the Moon?

15. Philosophy Corner: The World Becomes a Machine

Philosophy Corner: what's real and what's in us?

The biggest change brought by the Scientific Revolution wasn't just in astronomy or physics but in how people pictured reality itself. The historian E. J. Dijksterhuis called it the mechanisation of the world picture. [8]

In ancient and medieval physics, things moved because of inner purposes: a stone fell because it "wanted" to reach its natural home at the centre of the Earth; an acorn grew to fulfil its nature as an oak. The universe was full of purpose, meaning and qualities.

Galileo, Descartes and later John Locke split the properties of things into two kinds:

  • Primary qualities: properties that belong to the object itself, whether anyone is looking or not: shape, size, number, position and motion. These can be measured and described with mathematics.
  • Secondary qualities: experiences that exist only in the person perceiving: colour, taste, sound, smell, warmth. A vibrating string really has a frequency (primary), but the musical note we hear is an experience in us (secondary). (Democritus had made the same point two thousand years earlier, as we saw in Part 3.)

Treating the physical world as just matter moving according to mathematical laws gave science enormous power to predict. But there was a price: purpose, meaning and qualities like beauty were removed from the scientific description of nature, leaving human beings as conscious observers in a silent, machine-like universe. Whether that price was worth paying, and whether the picture is complete, is still debated.
