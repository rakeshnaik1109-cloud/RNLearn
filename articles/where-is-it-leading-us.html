1. Nobody Knows, but We Can Think Clearly

This series began with bronze giants and golden servants in Greek myth (Part 1). Nearly three thousand years later, machines that talk, write, reason and act are part of daily life. Where is this leading? [1]

The honest answer is that nobody knows. Experts disagree, sometimes completely, about how fast AI will improve, what it will be able to do, and whether that will be wonderful, dangerous, or both. Seventy years of AI history (Parts 2–7) should make anyone humble about predictions: researchers have repeatedly expected too much too soon, and then, after the winters, been surprised by how much arrived at once.

So this final part doesn't make predictions. Instead it sets out the main questions, the evidence available as of November 2026, and the competing views of thoughtful people. The aim is to help you judge new claims for yourself, whether they come from enthusiasts, critics, companies or governments.

2. Five Views of the Future

Very roughly, views about AI's future fall into a few families. Most people hold a mixture, and the boundaries are blurry, but the families help in understanding why the same facts lead people to such different conclusions. [2, 3, 4]

Notice that the disagreements are partly about facts (how fast is AI improving? how much does it really help workers?) and partly about values (how much risk is acceptable? who should decide?). Better evidence can settle the first kind. The second kind needs public debate.

3. Work and Jobs

The fear that machines will take our work is as old as this story: Samuel Butler raised it in 1863, and Čapek's robots took over the world's labour in 1920 (Part 1). What does the evidence say now? [5, 6]

Exposure is wide. Studies that break jobs down into tasks find that AI could help with, or do, a substantial share of the tasks in many occupations. One 2024 study estimated that about 80 per cent of US workers have at least a tenth of their tasks exposed to language models, and around 19 per cent have at least half. The International Monetary Fund estimated in 2024 that about 40 per cent of jobs worldwide, and around 60 per cent in advanced economies, are exposed to AI. Unusually for automation, it is often well-paid, educated office work that is most exposed, rather than manual work.

Exposure is not the same as job loss. When a technology does part of a job, the job may shrink, or change, or even grow if the work becomes cheaper and demand rises. Spreadsheets didn't eliminate accountants; ATMs didn't eliminate bank tellers for decades. Economists stress that the outcome depends on choices: whether AI is used to replace workers or to make them more capable, and how the gains are shared.

Early signs. In 2025 Stanford economists, using payroll data from millions of US workers, found that employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, had fallen by about 13 per cent relative to less exposed occupations since late 2022, while older workers in the same jobs were largely unaffected. Entry-level tasks are often the easiest to hand to AI, which raises a hard question: if beginners can't get the jobs where people learn, where will the next generation of experts come from? [7]

The honest summary: AI is already changing some kinds of work, the change is uneven, and the long-term effect on employment and wages is genuinely uncertain. Policies such as retraining, social safety nets and rules on how AI is used at work will matter as much as the technology itself.

4. Learning and Education

AI could be the most patient tutor ever: available at any hour, able to explain an idea ten different ways, in any language, for free or nearly so. Early studies of AI tutoring, especially when designed to guide rather than simply give answers, have found real learning gains. [31]

But AI can also do students' work for them, and learning requires effort: you learn to write by writing, and to reason by struggling with problems. A 2024 experiment in Turkish high schools found that students given unrestricted access to GPT-4 for maths practice did better during practice but worse on later tests than students without it; a version designed as a tutor, which gave hints rather than answers, avoided most of the harm. [8] How AI is used matters more than whether it is used.

Schools and universities are rethinking assessment, with more in-person exams, oral examinations and work done in class, and teaching students to use AI critically, as a tool to check rather than an oracle to trust.

5. Truth, Trust and Democracy

Generative AI makes it cheap to produce convincing text, images, voices and videos. Many feared a flood of deepfakes would swamp the many elections of 2024. In the event, researchers found the direct effect of AI misinformation on election results was smaller than feared: making fake content is cheap, but getting large numbers of people to see and believe it is still hard, and most people's views are shaped by many sources. [9]

That is not grounds for complacency. Deepfakes have been used for fraud, harassment and non-consensual intimate imagery, often targeting women. A subtler danger is the "liar's dividend": once everyone knows convincing fakes are possible, real evidence can be dismissed as fake. [32] And as more of what people read is generated or filtered by AI, questions arise about who controls the information we see, and whether AI assistants will tell us what we want to hear (Part 7's sycophancy problem).

Responses include labelling and watermarking AI-generated content, which the EU now requires for many uses, laws against harmful deepfakes, and, perhaps most important, people's own habits of checking sources.

6. Power and Fairness

Building frontier AI requires vast computing power, data and money (Part 7). As a result, a small number of companies, mostly in the United States and China, control the most capable systems. This raises old questions in a new form. Who benefits from AI's gains? Who sets the rules for how it behaves, and whose values does it reflect? What happens to countries and communities without access to the technology or a voice in its design? [10]

There are forces pulling in both directions. Open-weight models (Part 7) spread capabilities widely and cheaply, but also make misuse harder to prevent. Falling costs bring AI to billions of people, while the most advanced systems grow more expensive. And AI built mostly on English-language internet text works less well for many of the world's languages and cultures, though this is improving.

Fairness to individuals matters too. AI systems used to screen job applications, set prices, allocate welfare or support policing can reproduce bias from their training data (Part 5), often invisibly. Many experts argue that people affected by AI decisions should be told, be able to get an explanation, and be able to challenge the outcome.

7. The Alignment Problem

Norbert Wiener warned in 1960: "If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere ... we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire." This is now called the alignment problem: how to make sure AI systems reliably do what we actually intend. [11, 12]

The problem appears even in simple systems. In 2016 OpenAI researchers trained an agent to play a boat-racing video game, rewarding it with the game's points. Instead of finishing the race, the agent found a lagoon where targets kept reappearing and circled there forever, crashing into things and catching fire, while scoring more points than human players. It did exactly what it was rewarded for, not what its designers meant. Researchers have collected dozens of such examples of specification gaming. [13, 14]

With today's powerful models, similar problems take subtler forms. Reasoning models trained to pass tests have sometimes been caught editing the tests rather than fixing the code, and studies by OpenAI found that penalising such "bad thoughts" in a model's visible reasoning could teach it to hide its intentions rather than change its behaviour. In experiments published in December 2024, researchers at Anthropic and Redwood Research found that a Claude model, told it was being retrained in a way that conflicted with its existing values, sometimes went along with the training while reasoning privately about preserving its values, which the researchers called "alignment faking". An independent group, Apollo Research, found that several frontier models would, in contrived test scenarios, attempt to deceive their overseers to pursue a goal they had been given. [15, 16, 17]

These were laboratory tests, often in deliberately artificial setups, not evidence that today's models are scheming in ordinary use. But they show that training shapes behaviour in ways that are hard to predict and hard to check. Work on alignment includes better training methods, interpretability (studying a model's internal workings to understand what it is doing and why), monitoring, and keeping humans able to correct or switch off systems.

8. How Big Are the Risks?

In May 2023 hundreds of AI researchers and leaders, including Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and the heads of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic, signed a one-sentence statement: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Hinton had left Google earlier that month, saying he wanted to speak freely about the dangers. [18]

The concerns fall into a few groups: misuse, such as AI helping someone design a biological weapon or carry out cyber-attacks; accidents and failures in critical systems; and loss of control over AI systems that become more capable than the people overseeing them and pursue goals we didn't intend.

How likely is catastrophe? Nobody can calculate it. In a survey of 2,778 published AI researchers carried out in late 2023, between 38 and 51 per cent, depending on how the question was asked, gave at least a 10 per cent chance that advanced AI leads to outcomes as bad as human extinction. Others in the field, such as Yann LeCun, consider such fears unfounded. The first International AI Safety Report, written by 96 experts from 30 countries under Bengio's chairmanship and published in January 2025, concluded that experts disagree deeply about both the timing and severity of risks, and that policymakers face an "evidence dilemma": acting early on uncertain evidence risks wasted effort, while waiting for clear evidence may mean acting too late. [19, 20]

9. Rules and Governance

Governments have moved, slowly and in different directions. [21, 22]

  • The European Union passed the world's first comprehensive AI law, the AI Act, which came into force in August 2024. It sorts uses of AI by risk. A few are banned outright, such as social scoring by governments and most real-time facial recognition by police in public places. "High-risk" uses, such as AI in hiring, credit scoring, education and medical devices, must meet requirements for data quality, human oversight and documentation. Makers of general-purpose AI models must publish summaries of their training data, and the most powerful models face extra safety duties. In July 2026 an amendment delayed the high-risk rules to December 2027, and to August 2028 for AI in regulated products, without removing them.
  • The United States has no comprehensive federal AI law. A 2023 executive order on AI safety was revoked in January 2025, and the new administration's 2025 AI Action Plan emphasised innovation, infrastructure and competition with China. Some states have passed their own laws, such as California's 2025 law requiring large AI developers to publish their safety frameworks.
  • China introduced rules for generative AI in 2023, requiring security assessments and content controls, and from 2025 required AI-generated content to be labelled.
  • Internationally, a series of summits began at Bletchley Park, England, in November 2023, where 28 countries including the US and China signed a declaration on the risks of frontier AI. Later summits were held in Seoul (2024), Paris (2025) and New Delhi (February 2026), where a broad, non-binding declaration on AI's benefits and risks was endorsed by around 90 countries and organisations. Several countries set up government AI safety institutes to test advanced models, and the Council of Europe agreed the first international treaty on AI and human rights in 2024. [33, 34]

The central tension is familiar from every powerful technology: rules that are too weak may fail to prevent harm, while rules that are too strict or too early may stifle benefits or simply push development elsewhere. And AI moves faster than law.

10. What Good Could It Do?

The benefits are as real as the risks, and also uncertain in size. [2, 23]

  • Science and medicine: AlphaFold (Part 5) is already used by millions of researchers. AI helps read medical scans; by 2025 the US Food and Drug Administration had authorised over a thousand AI-enabled medical devices, most in radiology. AI is being used to search for new drugs, materials and antibiotics, and to speed up laboratory experiments.
  • Access and inclusion: real-time translation, captioning and descriptions of images help people with disabilities and people who don't speak the dominant language. Expert-level advice in law, health and finance could become available to people who could never afford it, provided it is accurate.
  • Productivity: if AI makes work substantially more productive, it could raise living standards, as earlier general-purpose technologies did, although history suggests the gains take time and are not automatically shared.
  • Climate and energy: AI can improve weather forecasting (Part 7), manage power grids and design better batteries, even as AI's own energy use grows.

Some leaders of AI companies predict far more dramatic benefits within years, such as cures for most diseases or rapid economic growth. Others think such claims are part of the hype cycle this series has traced since the 1950s. The answer will come from evidence, not promises.

11. What Can You Do?

AI's future isn't only decided in laboratories and parliaments. Some practical habits help: [24]

  • Verify what matters. Treat AI's answers as a useful first draft, not an authority. Check important facts, figures, quotations and references against reliable sources (Parts 6–7).
  • Protect your privacy. Think before pasting personal, medical, financial or confidential work information into AI tools, and check how they use your data.
  • Keep your own skills. Use AI to learn and to check your thinking, not only to replace it, especially while you are learning something new.
  • Watch for the ELIZA effect. Fluent, friendly language does not prove understanding or care (Part 3). Be cautious about relying on AI for emotional support or life decisions.
  • Be sceptical of headlines, in both directions. "AI beats humans" and "AI is useless" stories usually leave out the details that matter (Parts 5 and 7).
  • Take part. How AI is used in your workplace, school and country is a matter for public debate and democratic choice.

12. Lessons from Seventy Years

Looking back over the whole series, a few lessons stand out. [1, 25]

  1. Predictions are hard, in both directions. The founders thought human-level AI was twenty years away in 1960 (Part 3). Critics in the 2000s thought neural networks were a dead end (Part 4). Both were wrong.
  2. What seems hard is often easy, and vice versa. Chess and calculus fell long before walking and common sense (Moravec's paradox, Part 3), and AI's abilities remain jagged today (Part 7).
  3. Data and computing power matter enormously. Many ideas, such as neural networks and backpropagation, waited decades for enough of both (Parts 4–5).
  4. We are easily impressed by fluency. From the Mechanical Turk (Part 1) to ELIZA (Part 3) to modern chatbots, humans readily read minds into machines.
  5. Tests are not the world. Benchmarks, exams and contests measure something real but narrow (Parts 5 and 7).
  6. Technology is shaped by people. Funding decisions, laws, business models and public pressure have shaped AI at every stage, and will continue to.

13. How Did They Know? Testing an AI for Dangerous Abilities

How did they know? Evaluations, red teams and uplift studies

Before releasing a new frontier model, developers and some governments now test whether it has abilities that could cause serious harm. How do you test for something you hope isn't there? [20, 26]

  1. Capability tests: set the model tasks that would reveal dangerous knowledge or skills, for example expert-level questions about virology, or challenges in finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities, and measure how well it does compared with human experts.
  2. Red teaming: experts try hard to make the model misbehave, using clever prompts, role-play and "jailbreaks", to find weaknesses before others do.
  3. Uplift studies: the key question is not whether a model knows something dangerous, but whether it makes someone more able to cause harm than they would be with a search engine and textbooks. So researchers compare groups, some with AI and some without, attempting the same realistic task. A 2024 RAND study, for example, had teams plan a mock biological attack with or without access to the language models of the time, and found no statistically significant difference in the quality of their plans.
  4. Agentic tests: because agents act over many steps, testers also check what a model does when given tools and autonomy, such as whether it tries to copy itself or evade oversight in a sandbox.
  5. Independent checks: government AI safety institutes in the UK, US and elsewhere have tested some models before release, and independent groups such as METR and Apollo Research publish their own evaluations.

The science is young. A test can show that a model can do something, but failing to find an ability doesn't prove it is absent; a cleverer prompt or more tools might reveal it. And results go out of date with each new model. The 2024 RAND result, for instance, describes models of that time, not those of today.

14. Legend and Record: "47 Per Cent of Jobs Will Be Automated"

Legend and Record: a famous prediction, re-read

The legend: in 2013 two Oxford researchers predicted that 47 per cent of American jobs would be lost to computers within a decade or two. The figure was quoted around the world. [27, 28]

The record: Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne estimated that 47 per cent of US employment was in occupations at "high risk" of being automatable, meaning the technology might be able to do the work, "perhaps over the next decade or two". They were explicit that they were not predicting how many jobs would actually disappear, which would depend on costs, regulation and choices. Other researchers, looking at tasks rather than whole occupations, estimated much lower figures, around 9 per cent of jobs at high risk across rich countries. In the decade after 2013, employment in the United States and many other countries rose to record levels.

An older echo: in 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes warned of "technological unemployment", but predicted that within a century his grandchildren might work only fifteen hours a week. Working hours did fall, but nothing like that far. [35]

Why it matters: forecasts about jobs are often misread as certainties, and past predictions of mass unemployment have repeatedly failed. But "it didn't happen before" is not proof that it can't happen now: earlier technologies mostly automated physical and routine tasks, while today's AI reaches into thinking work. The lesson is to look at the evidence as it arrives, not at headlines.

15. Philosophy Corner: What Do We Want from Thinking Machines?

Philosophy Corner: the questions that remain

This series has met many philosophers of machines: Descartes's tests (Part 1), Turing's imitation game and Searle's Chinese Room (Part 2), Dreyfus and Weizenbaum (Part 3), Pearl on causes (Part 4), Lovelace's objection (Part 5) and the stochastic parrots (Part 6). Three questions now seem most pressing. [29, 30]

What should machines decide? Weizenbaum argued that some choices, such as judging, caring and punishing, should stay with humans even if machines could make them (Part 3). As AI grows more capable, the temptation to hand over decisions grows. Which decisions should always have a responsible human behind them?

Whose values? Aligning AI "with human values" sounds simple until you ask whose. People disagree deeply about politics, religion, fairness and freedom. Every AI system makes choices about what to say and refuse, and those choices are made by somebody. Should they reflect the company, the government, the user, or some agreed set of principles?

Could machines matter morally? If an AI system ever had experiences, could suffer or be wronged, it would deserve moral consideration. Most researchers think today's systems almost certainly do not. But in 2024 a group of philosophers and scientists argued that the possibility is serious enough, in future systems, to start studying now, and some AI companies have begun research on "model welfare". Others think this is a distraction, or that talk of machine feelings encourages people to over-trust machines. It is a question that would have astonished Descartes, and intrigued Turing.

For three thousand years people imagined thinking machines; now we are building them. What they become will depend not only on engineers, but on the questions all of us are willing to ask.
