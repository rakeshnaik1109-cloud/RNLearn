1. A Distribution in Five Numbers

Part 4 showed distributions in full, with histograms and density curves. Often we need something more compact: a summary that fits several groups side by side, resists being dragged around by a few extreme values, and still shows centre, spread and skew. John Tukey’s answer was the box plot, built from five numbers: the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile and the maximum, with special treatment for outliers. [1]

This part explains how to read box plots, what they hide, and the plots designed to fix their weaknesses: violin plots, beeswarms and raincloud plots. It also argues against one of the most common charts in science, the bar chart of means with error bars, and introduces the empirical cumulative distribution function, a precise way to compare whole distributions.

3. The Box Plot

Sort the data and find three cut points: the median (half the values below it), the first quartile Q1 (a quarter below) and the third quartile Q3 (three quarters below). The box runs from Q1 to Q3 and so contains the middle half of the data; its length is the interquartile range (IQR). In Tukey’s version, each whisker extends to the furthest observation within 1.5 × IQR of the box, and anything beyond is plotted as an individual point, a possible outlier.

How to read a box plot
  1. Centre: the median line. Compare medians across boxes.
  2. Spread: the length of the box (IQR) and of the whiskers.
  3. Skew: if the median sits near one end of the box, or one whisker is much longer, the distribution is skewed towards the longer side.
  4. Outliers: points beyond the whiskers. They are not necessarily errors, just unusual; in large samples you expect some.
  5. The convention: check what the whiskers mean. Some software draws them to the minimum and maximum, or to the 5th and 95th percentiles.
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
times = np.append(rng.gamma(4, 5, 60), [62, 75])        # synthetic delivery times (minutes), two late ones

q1, med, q3 = np.percentile(times, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
print(f"Q1={q1:.1f}  median={med:.1f}  Q3={q3:.1f}  IQR={iqr:.1f}")
print("Points beyond 1.5×IQR:", np.sort(times[(times < q1 - 1.5 * iqr) | (times > q3 + 1.5 * iqr)]).round(1))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.8))
ax.boxplot(times, orientation="horizontal", whis=1.5, widths=0.5,   # matplotlib ≥ 3.10 (older: vert=False)
           # whiskers reach the furthest point within 1.5×IQR of the box
           flierprops=dict(marker="o", markerfacecolor="#D55E00", markeredgecolor="none"))
ax.plot(times, np.full_like(times, 1.4), "|", color="gray", alpha=0.6)   # the raw data, for comparison
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.set_xlabel("Delivery time (minutes)")
ax.set_title("Box plot with the raw data above it")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. What a Box Plot Hides

Five numbers cannot capture everything. A box plot does not show how many observations there are, whether the distribution has one peak or two, or where the gaps are.

This is the box-plot version of Anscombe’s quartet (Part 1). The cure is to show the raw data alongside the box, or to use one of the plots in Section 6.

5. Comparing Groups

Box plots shine when comparing many groups: a dozen boxes side by side fit where a dozen histograms would not. In 1978 Robert McGill, John Tukey and Wayne Larsen proposed two refinements: variable widths, proportional to the square root of each group’s size, and notches, which show a rough 95% confidence interval for the median. [2] If two notches do not overlap, the medians probably differ.

With seaborn, grouped box plots come from data in “long” format, with one row per observation and columns for the value and the group labels:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
rows = []
for method, centre in [("A", 50), ("B", 56), ("C", 53)]:
    for site in ["North", "South"]:
        shift = 3 if site == "South" else 0
        for v in rng.normal(centre + shift, 8, 40):
            rows.append({"method": method, "site": site, "yield": v})
df = pd.DataFrame(rows)                                   # long ("tidy") format: one row per observation

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
sns.boxplot(data=df, x="method", y="yield", hue="site", notch=True,   # notches: rough 95% CI of the median
            palette=["#0072B2", "#E69F00"], ax=ax)
ax.set_ylabel("Crop yield (units)")
ax.set_title("Yield by method and site")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Violins, Beeswarms and Rainclouds

A violin plot, introduced by Jerry Hintze and Ray Nelson in 1998, replaces or surrounds the box with a mirrored density curve (Part 4), so that the shape of the distribution, including multiple peaks, is visible. [3] A beeswarm draws every observation as a dot, nudged sideways so that no two overlap. A raincloud plot, described by Micah Allen and colleagues in 2019, combines a half-violin, a slim box plot and the jittered raw data. [4]

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "group": np.repeat(["Control", "Drug X", "Drug Y"], 50),
    "response": np.concatenate([
        rng.normal(10, 2, 50),
        np.concatenate([rng.normal(8, 1.2, 25), rng.normal(15, 1.2, 25)]),   # two kinds of responder
        rng.normal(12, 3, 50),
    ]),
})

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4), sharey=True)
sns.violinplot(data=df, x="group", y="response", inner="quartile", cut=0, color="#BBD7EA", ax=ax1)
ax1.set_title("Violin plot (lines = quartiles)")
sns.boxplot(data=df, x="group", y="response", color="white", width=0.4, fliersize=0, ax=ax2)
sns.swarmplot(data=df, x="group", y="response", size=3.5, color="#0072B2", ax=ax2)
ax2.set_title("Box plot with a beeswarm of every point")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Seaborn has no raincloud function, but one is easy to assemble from a density curve, a box plot and a scatter of jittered points:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
groups = {"Morning": rng.normal(14, 4, 60), "Midday": rng.normal(22, 5, 60), "Evening": rng.gamma(9, 2.8, 60)}
colours = ["#0072B2", "#E69F00", "#009E73"]

# Raincloud: half-violin (cloud) + box plot + jittered points (rain)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.5))
for i, ((name, y), col) in enumerate(zip(groups.items(), colours)):
    grid = np.linspace(y.min(), y.max(), 200)
    dens = gaussian_kde(y)(grid)
    ax.fill_between(grid, i + 0.1, i + 0.1 + dens / dens.max() * 0.45, color=col, alpha=0.6)
    ax.boxplot(y, positions=[i], orientation="horizontal", widths=0.12, showfliers=False)
    ax.scatter(y, i - 0.2 + rng.uniform(-0.07, 0.07, y.size), s=8, color=col, alpha=0.7)
ax.set_yticks(range(len(groups)), groups.keys())
ax.set_xlabel("Waiting time (minutes)")
ax.set_title("Raincloud plot")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. The Dynamite Plot Problem

Biomedical papers have long been full of bar charts showing the mean of each group with an error bar on top, nicknamed “dynamite plots” for their resemblance to a detonator. In 2015 Tracey Weissgerber and colleagues reviewed hundreds of papers in leading physiology journals and argued that such charts should be replaced by plots that show the data, because very different distributions can produce identical bars, especially with the small samples common in laboratory research. [5]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
a = rng.normal(20, 3, 12)
b = np.concatenate([rng.normal(14, 1.5, 8), rng.normal(32, 2, 4)])   # skewed by a subgroup
data, names = [a, b], ["A", "B"]

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 4), sharey=True)
means = [d.mean() for d in data]
sems = [d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(d.size) for d in data]
ax1.bar(names, means, yerr=sems, capsize=8, color=["#0072B2", "#E69F00"])
ax1.set_title('"Dynamite plot": mean ± standard error')
for i, d in enumerate(data):                              # show every observation instead
    ax2.scatter(np.full(d.size, i) + rng.uniform(-0.08, 0.08, d.size), d, color="#555555", s=18)
    ax2.hlines(np.median(d), i - 0.25, i + 0.25, color="#D55E00", lw=2)
ax2.set_xticks([0, 1], names)
ax2.set_title("The same data: every point, median in red")
ax1.set_ylabel("Measurement")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

A bar suggests that values fill the space from zero to the top, which is false for a mean of measurements. A dot plot of every observation with a line for the median, or a box plot with points, says more with the same space.

8. The Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function

An empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) plots, for each value x, the proportion of observations that are less than or equal to x. The curve climbs from 0 to 1 in a small step at each data point. It needs no bins or bandwidth, shows every observation, and makes percentiles easy to read.

ECDFs are especially useful for comparing tails, such as the slowest 10% of web pages, and they underlie a well-known statistical test, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which uses the largest vertical gap between two ECDFs.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
old = rng.gamma(5, 6, 200)                               # synthetic page-load times (ms ÷ 10)
new = rng.gamma(5, 5, 200)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.ecdf(old, label="Old server")                         # matplotlib ≥ 3.8; for older versions use np.sort
ax.ecdf(new, label="New server")
ax.axhline(0.9, color="gray", ls=":", lw=1)
for data, name in [(old, "old"), (new, "new")]:
    print(f"90th percentile ({name}): {np.percentile(data, 90):.1f}")
ax.set_xlabel("Load time")
ax.set_ylabel("Proportion of pages at or below this time")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

9. Which Should I Use?

Plot Best for Weakness
Box plotComparing many groups; robust summary; outliersHides shape, peaks and sample size
Violin plotShape of each group, multiple peaksSmoothing can mislead for small samples
Beeswarm / strip plotSmall to moderate samples; showing every pointCrowded beyond a few hundred points
RaincloudSummary and data togetherTakes more space
ECDFPrecise comparison of distributions and tailsLess familiar; shape is less intuitive
Bar of means ± errorCounts or totals (not measurements)Hides the distribution entirely

10. Common Misreadings

  • Assuming whiskers show the minimum and maximum. In the most common convention, they stop at 1.5 × IQR; check the caption.
  • Treating “outliers” as mistakes. In a sample of 1,000 normal values, about seven will fall beyond Tukey’s whiskers by chance.
  • Reading the box as a confidence interval. The box shows the spread of the data, not the uncertainty of the median; that is what notches are for.
  • Comparing boxes without knowing the sample sizes. A box from 5 observations is far less reliable than one from 500.
  • Over-reading violin shapes from small samples. A bimodal-looking violin from 15 points may be noise.
  • Believing a dynamite plot. Equal bars can hide completely different data; ask for the points.

11. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who invented the box plot?

The legend: John Tukey invented the box plot.

The record: Tukey introduced the box-and-whisker plot, with its whisker and outlier rules, in 1970 and in his 1977 book, and made it famous. [1] But in 1952 the American government statistician Mary Eleanor Spear had already shown a “range bar”: a horizontal bar spanning the middle half of the data, with a line at the median and lines to the extremes. [6] Tukey’s contributions were the whisker rule and the robust treatment of outliers, which made the plot a tool for exploring data rather than only presenting it.

A second legend: points beyond the whiskers should be removed. The record: Tukey called them “outside values” to flag them for a closer look, not for deletion. Some are errors; others are the most interesting observations in the data set.
