The MOSFET: How a Voltage Opens a Channel

A voltage on a gate, separated from silicon by a layer of glass a few atoms thick, conjures a sheet of electrons out of nowhere. That trick, repeated tens of billions of times per chip, runs the modern world.

At a glance

In this part: the metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET), the most manufactured object in history. The field-effect idea of the 1920s and why it took until 1959 to work; the MOS capacitor and its accumulation, depletion and inversion; the threshold voltage; the linear and saturation regions and the square law; and subthreshold conduction, the 60 mV-per-decade limit that keeps transistors from ever switching fully off.

You need: Parts 5 and 6 (bands, the Fermi level, junctions). Part 7 helps as a comparison.

Try: three interactive figures and five worked problems.

1. The Transistor That Took Over

The bipolar transistor of Part 7 is controlled by a current. The MOSFET is controlled by a voltage: a metal (or doped silicon) gate, separated from the silicon by a thin insulating layer, attracts or repels charge in the silicon beneath it and so opens or closes a channel between two terminals, the source and the drain. No steady current flows into the gate, so one MOSFET can drive many others, and a MOSFET can be made very small and very simple. Today well over 1021 have been made, more than any other manufactured object, and a single flagship chip contains tens of billions. [1, 2]

3. The Field-Effect Idea

The idea is older than the bipolar transistor. In 1925 Julius Edgar Lilienfeld, a physicist who had moved from Leipzig to the United States, filed patents for a device in which a voltage on a control electrode changes the conductivity of a thin semiconducting film. [3] Oskar Heil patented a similar insulated-gate device in Britain in 1935. [4] Neither could be made to work reliably with the materials of the day, and Bell Labs’ own attempts in the 1940s also failed: electrons trapped in surface states at the semiconductor’s surface screened the gate’s field, an obstacle that set Bardeen and Brattain on the path to the point-contact transistor instead (Part 7).

The solution was silicon’s own oxide. In 1957 Carl Frosch and Lincoln Derick at Bell Labs found that a layer of silicon dioxide grown on silicon by heating it in oxygen protected the surface and could mask it during doping. [5] Mohamed Atalla showed that such a thermally grown oxide dramatically reduced surface states, and in 1959 he and Dawon Kahng built the first working metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor, which they presented in 1960. [6] Early MOSFETs were slower than bipolar transistors, but they were simpler to make, and by the 1970s they were the basis of memory chips and microprocessors.

4. The MOS Capacitor: Accumulation, Depletion and Inversion

The heart of a MOSFET is a capacitor: gate, oxide and silicon. Take p-type silicon and vary the gate voltage:

  • Accumulation (gate negative): holes, the majority carriers, are pulled to the surface.
  • Depletion (gate slightly positive): holes are pushed away, leaving a layer of fixed, negatively charged acceptor ions, just as in a p–n junction.
  • Inversion (gate positive enough): the bands bend so strongly that the surface region becomes n-type. A thin sheet of electrons, the inversion layer, only a few nanometres thick, forms at the surface. This sheet is the transistor’s channel.

In band terms, the gate voltage bends the bands at the surface by the surface potential ψs. Strong inversion is conventionally reached when ψs = 2φF, where φF = (kT/q) ln(NA/ni) is the distance from the Fermi level to mid-gap in the bulk; then the surface has as many electrons as the bulk has holes. [2, 7]

5. The Threshold Voltage

The gate voltage at which strong inversion begins is the threshold voltage. It has three parts: a flat-band voltage VFB that accounts for the difference in work function between gate and silicon (and any charge in the oxide); the 2φF of band bending; and the voltage across the oxide needed to hold the depletion charge:

VT = VFB + 2φF + √(2εSiqNA·2φF) / Cox

where Cox = εox/tox is the oxide capacitance per unit area. Engineers set VT, typically 0.2–0.5 V in modern logic, by adjusting the doping just under the gate and by choosing the gate material. Above threshold, each extra volt on the gate adds a sheet of channel charge Qn ≈ −Cox(VGS − VT): a thinner oxide gives more charge, and more current, for the same voltage.

6. Current–Voltage Characteristics

Add an n+ source and drain on either side of the gate and the inversion layer connects them. With channel length L, width W and electron mobility μn, the classic long-channel model, set out by Chih-Tang Sah in 1964, gives two regions: [8]

  • Linear (triode) region, VDS < VGS − VT: ID = μnCox(W/L)[(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²/2]. For small VDS the channel is a resistor whose value the gate controls.
  • Saturation, VDS ≥ VGS − VT: the channel pinches off at the drain end, and ID = ½μnCox(W/L)(VGS − VT)², the square law.

The current is proportional to W/L, which is why designers specify transistors by their width and length, and why shorter channels give more current. In real short-channel devices the electrons reach a maximum velocity in high fields, and the current grows more nearly in proportion to VGS − VT than to its square (Part 11). [1]

7. Subthreshold Conduction: Never Fully Off

An ideal switch would carry no current below threshold. A MOSFET does not: electrons in the source have a thermal spread of energies, and a few always have enough to cross the barrier into the channel. Below threshold the barrier height changes in step with the gate voltage, so the current is exponential, like a diode’s:

ID ∝ 10(VGS − VT)/S, with S = (kT/q) ln 10 × (1 + Cdep/Cox) ≥ 60 mV/decade

The subthreshold swing S is the gate voltage needed to change the current tenfold. Boltzmann statistics set an absolute floor of about 60 mV per decade at room temperature, however well the transistor is built; real planar transistors managed 80–100 mV. The consequence is a fundamental trade-off: to switch a current of a milliamp on and leave picoamps off, a factor of 109, the gate must swing at least 9 × 60 = 540 mV. [1] This is why supply voltages, which fell from 5 V to about 1 V, have barely fallen since the mid-2000s, as Part 10 explains.

8. nMOS, pMOS and the Complementary Idea

Swap all the doping types and you get a p-channel MOSFET (pMOS): an n-type body, p+ source and drain, and a channel of holes that forms when the gate is pulled below the source voltage. Holes in silicon have two to three times lower mobility than electrons, so a pMOS transistor must be wider to carry the same current.

Early MOS chips used only one type: the Intel 4004 microprocessor of 1971 used pMOS, and later chips of the 1970s used nMOS with resistors or always-on transistors as loads. Like bipolar logic, these designs drew current whenever a gate’s output was low. In 1963 Frank Wanlass and Chih-Tang Sah at Fairchild showed a better way: pair an nMOS and a pMOS so that, in either logic state, one of them is off and almost no current flows. [9] This complementary MOS (CMOS) logic, the subject of Part 9, became the basis of essentially all digital chips from the 1980s on.

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): oxide capacitance and channel charge

A gate oxide of SiO₂ (εox = 3.45 × 10−13 F/cm) is 2 nm thick. Find Cox and the number of channel electrons per cm² when VGS − VT = 0.5 V.

Solution

Cox = 3.45 × 10−13 / 2 × 10−7 = 1.7 × 10−6 F/cm² (17 fF/µm²). Qn = Cox × 0.5 = 8.6 × 10−7 C/cm², which is 8.6 × 10−7 / 1.6 × 10−19 ≈ 5.4 × 1012 electrons/cm². A 20 nm × 20 nm patch of channel holds only about 20 electrons.

Problem 2 (easy): saturation current

An nMOS transistor has μnCox = 300 µA/V², W/L = 10 and VT = 0.4 V. Find the saturation current at VGS = 0.9 V, and the minimum VDS for saturation.

Solution

ID = ½ × 300 µA/V² × 10 × (0.5 V)² = 375 µA. Saturation requires VDS ≥ VGS − VT = 0.5 V. Doubling the overdrive to 1 V would quadruple the current in this long-channel model.

Problem 3 (medium): threshold voltage

Estimate VT for NA = 1017 cm−3, tox = 10 nm, and VFB = −0.98 V (an n+ polysilicon gate). Use εSi = 1.04 × 10−12 F/cm.

Solution

φF = 0.0259 × ln(107) = 0.417 V, so 2φF = 0.834 V. Depletion charge: √(2 × 1.04 × 10−12 × 1.6 × 10−19 × 1017 × 0.834) ≈ 1.67 × 10−7 C/cm². Cox = 3.45 × 10−7 F/cm², so that term is 0.48 V. VT = −0.98 + 0.83 + 0.48 ≈ 0.33 V.

Problem 4 (medium): leakage power

Transistors have a current of 1 µA per µm of width at VGS = VT = 0.3 V and a swing of 80 mV/decade. Estimate the off-current per µm at VGS = 0, and the leakage power of 1010 transistors, each 0.1 µm wide, at 0.8 V. What if VT is lowered to 0.2 V?

Solution

Going 0.3 V below threshold divides the current by 10300/80 = 103.75 ≈ 5,600: Ioff ≈ 0.18 nA/µm. Total: 1010 × 0.1 µm × 0.18 nA/µm = 0.18 A, so P = 0.18 × 0.8 ≈ 0.14 W. With VT = 0.2 V, leakage rises by 10100/80 ≈ 18 to about 2.5 W, spent doing nothing.

Problem 5 (harder): the 60 mV limit

Below threshold, the drain current is proportional to eqψs/kT, and the surface potential follows the gate through a capacitive divider, Δψs = ΔVGS × Cox/(Cox + Cdep). Derive S, find its minimum at 300 K, and evaluate it at 77 K (liquid nitrogen).

Solution

A tenfold change in current needs Δψs = (kT/q) ln 10. The gate must change by that divided by Cox/(Cox + Cdep), so S = (kT/q) ln 10 × (1 + Cdep/Cox). The bracket is at least 1, so S ≥ 0.02585 × 2.303 = 59.5 mV/decade at 300 K. At 77 K, S ≥ 59.5 × 77/300 ≈ 15 mV/decade, which is one reason cryogenic CMOS is studied for quantum-computer control electronics (Part 14). Beating 60 mV at room temperature needs a different injection mechanism, such as tunnelling.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: can a transistor beat 60 mV per decade?

Because the 60 mV limit comes from thermally injecting carriers over a barrier, several ideas try to avoid it. Tunnel FETs let electrons tunnel through a narrow band-to-band barrier, which can in principle switch more sharply; in practice their on-currents have been too low. Negative-capacitance FETs add a ferroelectric layer intended to amplify the gate voltage at the channel; whether the effect is robust and fast enough for logic has been vigorously debated. Mechanical relays switch perfectly but slowly. No steep-slope device has yet matched silicon MOSFETs for speed, current and manufacturability, and whether one ever will is a central open question for low-power computing.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a transistor is a perfect on–off switch”

The myth: digital chips are made of switches that are either fully on or fully off.

The reality: a MOSFET’s current changes smoothly and exponentially below threshold, so “off” means a current a million or so times smaller than “on”, not zero. Multiplied by billions of transistors, those small currents add up to watts of leakage. The digital abstraction of 0s and 1s rests on analogue devices pushed to extremes.

Why it matters: the gap between real and ideal switches is what limits how far supply voltages, and therefore power, can be reduced.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • The field-effect idea dates from 1925, but working MOSFETs needed a clean thermal oxide on silicon (Atalla and Kahng, 1959).
  • A MOS capacitor passes through accumulation, depletion and inversion as the gate voltage rises; the inversion layer is the channel.
  • VT = VFB + 2φF + √(2εSiqNA2φF)/Cox; thinner oxides give stronger gate control.
  • Long-channel MOSFETs have a linear region and a saturation region with ID = ½μCox(W/L)(VGS − VT)².
  • Below threshold the current falls only tenfold per S ≥ 60 mV, so off-transistors leak.
  • Pairing nMOS and pMOS (CMOS) eliminates static current in logic.
