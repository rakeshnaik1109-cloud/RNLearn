Neurons and Activation Functions: How Neural Networks Compute and Learn

A weighted sum and a simple function: that is all an artificial neuron does. Connect many of them in layers, choose the right activation, and train them with backpropagation, and you have the engine of modern AI.

1. From Brain Cells to Building Blocks

The second half of this series is about neural networks, the models behind modern image recognition, speech, translation and language models. Their basic unit, the artificial neuron, is very simple: it adds up its inputs, each multiplied by a weight, and passes the result through a function. What makes neural networks powerful is connecting many of these units in layers and training all their weights together from data.

This part builds that machinery from the ground up: the neuron, the functions that give networks their flexibility, and the backpropagation algorithm that trains them. The history of the field, from its early optimism through the “AI winters” to the deep-learning revolution, is told in our series How Machines Learned to Think; here the focus is on how the pieces work. [1, 2]

3. The Artificial Neuron

In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a mathematical model of a nerve cell: it receives signals, and fires if their combined strength passes a threshold. [3] Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron of 1958 added learning: the strengths of the connections, the weights, were adjusted from examples. [4] A modern artificial neuron computes

$$\text{output} = f\big(w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_dx_d + b\big) = f(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b),$$

where the x’s are inputs, the w’s weights, b a bias, and f an activation function. If f is the sigmoid, a single neuron is exactly logistic regression (Part 2); if f is a step, it is a perceptron. Either way, a single neuron draws a straight boundary.

4. The XOR Problem and Hidden Layers

The XOR (“exclusive or”) function outputs 1 when exactly one of its two inputs is 1. Its four points sit on the corners of a square, with the 1s on one diagonal and the 0s on the other: no straight line separates them, so no single neuron can compute XOR. In their 1969 book Perceptrons, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert analysed such limitations of single-layer networks rigorously, and their critique contributed to a long decline in neural-network research. [5]

The fix is to add a hidden layer between input and output. One hidden neuron can compute OR, another NAND, and an output neuron can combine them with AND: exactly XOR. More generally, hidden units create new features from the inputs, and later layers combine those features, like the hand-made features of Part 2, except that the network learns them. What was missing in 1969 was a practical way to train the hidden weights. That came with backpropagation (Section 8).

Check yourself: XOR from three neurons

With step activations: h1 = step(x1 + x2 − 0.5) is OR; h2 = step(−x1 − x2 + 1.5) is NAND; output = step(h1 + h2 − 1.5) is AND of the two. Check (1, 1): h1 = 1, h2 = 0, output 0. Check (1, 0): h1 = 1, h2 = 1, output 1. Use the weights in Figure 3 to confirm OR and NAND.

5. Activation Functions

The activation function decides how a neuron responds to its weighted input. Its shape matters for two things: what the network can represent, and how well gradients flow during training, which depends on the function’s derivative.

  • Sigmoid, σ(z) = 1/(1 + e−z), squashes inputs into (0, 1). It was the classic choice for decades, but its derivative is at most 0.25 and nearly zero for large inputs.
  • Tanh squashes into (−1, 1) and is centred on zero, which usually trains better than the sigmoid.
  • ReLU (rectified linear unit), max(0, z), is zero for negative inputs and the identity for positive ones. Vinod Nair and Geoffrey Hinton used it in 2010, and Xavier Glorot, Antoine Bordes and Yoshua Bengio showed in 2011 that it makes deep networks much easier to train. [6, 7] It became the default.
  • Leaky ReLU and ELU give negative inputs a small slope or a smooth curve, so units never switch off completely. [8, 9]
  • GELU (Gaussian error linear unit), z × Φ(z), and Swish (or SiLU), z × σ(z), are smooth versions of ReLU that let small negative values through. GELU is standard in transformers such as BERT and GPT; Swish was found by an automated search over candidate functions. [10, 11]

6. Why Nonlinearity Matters

Why not skip the activation function, or use a straight line? Because stacking linear layers gains nothing: a linear function of a linear function is still linear. A hundred linear layers collapse to one, and the network can draw only straight boundaries, like a single neuron. The nonlinearity is what lets each layer bend and fold the space, so that later layers can separate what earlier layers could not.

The maths: linear layers collapse

Two layers without activation compute

$$\mathbf{y} = W_2(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2 = (W_2W_1)\,\mathbf{x} + (W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2) = W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b},$$

a single linear layer with W = W2W1. Inserting a nonlinear f between them, y = W2 f(W1x + b1) + b2, breaks this collapse.

7. Universal Approximation

How much can a network with hidden layers represent? In 1989 George Cybenko proved that a network with a single hidden layer of sigmoid units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region as closely as desired, given enough units; Kurt Hornik extended the result to essentially any non-polynomial activation in 1991. [12, 13] This is the universal approximation theorem.

With ReLU units the idea is easy to see: each hidden unit contributes a hinge, and the output is a sum of hinges, a piecewise-linear curve. For the sine wave of the running example, 4 evenly placed hinges give a largest error of 0.19; 8 give 0.053, 16 give 0.013 and 40 give 0.002. The theorem says such a network exists; it says nothing about how many units are needed, or whether training will find the right weights. In practice, deep networks with several layers represent many functions far more economically than one very wide layer.

8. Backpropagation

Training a network means gradient descent (Part 2): compute how the loss changes with every weight, and step downhill. A network may have millions of weights, so computing each derivative separately would be hopeless. Backpropagation computes all of them in one backward sweep through the network, at a cost similar to a single forward pass.

The idea is the chain rule of calculus applied systematically. The forward pass computes each layer’s values from the inputs to the loss. The backward pass starts at the loss and moves towards the inputs, multiplying the gradient arriving at each node by that node’s local derivative. The general method, reverse-mode automatic differentiation, was described by Seppo Linnainmaa in 1970; [14] David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams’ 1986 paper in Nature showed that it lets hidden layers learn useful internal representations, and it became the engine of neural-network training. [15]

The maths: the chain rule in the tiny network

For z = w1x + b1, h = tanh(z), ŷ = w2h + b2 and L = ½(ŷ − y)²:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = (\hat y - y)\,h, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = \underbrace{(\hat y - y)}_{\partial L/\partial \hat y}\cdot\underbrace{w_2}_{\partial \hat y/\partial h}\cdot\underbrace{(1-h^2)}_{\partial h/\partial z}\cdot\underbrace{x}_{\partial z/\partial w_1}.$$

The factor (ŷ − y)·w2 is computed once and reused for every weight that feeds into h. In a big network, this reuse of intermediate gradients is what makes backpropagation efficient.

In code: a network trained with automatic differentiation in PyTorch 
import torch
from torch import nn
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

X, y = make_moons(n_samples=200, noise=0.2, random_state=0)
X = torch.tensor(X, dtype=torch.float32); y = torch.tensor(y, dtype=torch.float32)

model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, 8), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(8, 1))   # 2 → 8 → 1
loss_fn = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()             # sigmoid output + cross-entropy
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.03)

for step in range(3000):
    loss = loss_fn(model(X).squeeze(), y)    # forward pass
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()          # backpropagation
    opt.step()                                # gradient step
print(loss.item())

9. Vanishing and Exploding Gradients

Backpropagation multiplies gradients by a weight and an activation derivative at every layer. In a deep network these factors multiply up. If they are typically less than 1, the gradient reaching the early layers shrinks exponentially and those layers barely learn: the vanishing gradient problem, analysed by Sepp Hochreiter in 1991. [16] If they are typically greater than 1, gradients explode and training becomes unstable.

In a 20-layer network with sigmoid units and standard initial weights, the gradient at the first layer is about 10−12 of that at the last: the early layers receive essentially no learning signal. With tanh the ratio is about 0.15. ReLU with weights scaled by √(2/n), the “He initialisation”, keeps the gradient nearly constant through all 20 layers (a ratio of about 1.1); with weights twice as large, it explodes by a factor of nearly 800. Choosing activations and initial weights so that signals keep a steady size, together with normalisation layers and skip connections (Parts 8 and 9), is what made networks of dozens or hundreds of layers trainable. [17, 18]

10. Training a Small Network

Figure 8 puts the pieces together: a network with two inputs, one hidden layer and a sigmoid output, trained with backpropagation and gradient descent on the series’ classification data. Watch the decision boundary bend into shape as the loss falls.

With 8 tanh hidden units and 3,000 steps, the network reaches 92% test accuracy on the moons, 98% on the circles and 93% on an XOR pattern of four quadrants. With a single hidden unit it is just logistic regression and fails on the circles (58%) and XOR (70%). A surprise awaits with XOR and two hidden units: two are enough in principle, yet from 9 of 10 random starts training gets stuck at 75%, unable to find the solution. Unlike the linear models of Part 2 and the SVMs of Part 5, neural networks have non-convex losses with many local minima and flat regions, and where training ends up can depend on where it starts. Larger networks, perhaps surprisingly, are usually easier to train.

11. Output Layers

Hidden layers use activations such as ReLU or GELU, but the output layer’s activation is chosen to match the task:

  • Regression: no activation (linear output), so predictions can take any value; or softplus or exponential if they must be positive.
  • Binary classification: a single sigmoid unit giving a probability.
  • Multi-class classification: a softmax over one unit per class (Part 2).
  • Multi-label classification (several labels can be true at once): one sigmoid per label.

Each output activation pairs naturally with a loss: linear with squared error, sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, softmax with categorical cross-entropy. Part 7 explains why these pairings are so natural.

A sensible default today is ReLU for convolutional networks and GELU or a gated variant such as SwiGLU for transformers. Differences between the modern ReLU-like functions are usually small compared with getting the learning rate, initialisation and normalisation right (Part 8).

13. Open Questions

Open questions: why do some activations work better?

Many of today’s activation functions were found by trial and error rather than derived from theory. Swish came out of an automated search over hundreds of candidate formulas. [11] In 2020 Noam Shazeer reported that gated variants of the transformer’s feed-forward layer, such as SwiGLU, train noticeably better, and candidly admitted in the paper that he could offer no explanation for why, attributing their success, with tongue in cheek, to “divine benevolence”. [19] SwiGLU has since been adopted in many large language models. Why smooth, non-monotonic functions with gates help, and how the choice interacts with depth, normalisation and optimisation, remains largely an empirical question.

14. Worked Example: How Big Should the Network Be?

  1. One hidden unit (tanh): 87% test accuracy on the moons, 58% on the circles: logistic regression with a sigmoid output, so a straight boundary.
  2. Three units: 92% on the moons and 97% on the circles, already as good as any classical method in Parts 2–5.
  3. Eight units: 92% on the moons, 98% on the circles. With tanh, ReLU or sigmoid hidden units, results are within a few points of each other.
  4. Robustness to the starting point: across 10 different random initialisations, the 8-unit tanh network scored 92% on the moons nine times and 90% once.
  5. Thirty-two ReLU units: 90% on the moons, 97% on the circles: more capacity does not help on 140 points, and without regularisation or early stopping it starts to fit noise. Part 8 covers how to control this.

16. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “neural networks work like the brain”

The myth: artificial neural networks are simulations of the brain, and artificial neurons are simplified copies of real ones.

The reality: the McCulloch–Pitts neuron was inspired by biology, but it is a drastic abstraction. Real neurons communicate with timed electrical spikes, have elaborate branching dendrites that themselves compute, change their connections by mechanisms quite unlike backpropagation, and come in many types. In 2021 David Beniaguev, Idan Segev and Michael London found that reproducing the input–output behaviour of a single model cortical neuron with millisecond precision took a deep artificial network several layers deep. [20] One real neuron is closer to a whole artificial network than to one artificial unit.

Why it matters: neural networks are best understood as flexible mathematical functions trained by gradient descent. Their resemblance to brains is historical and loose, which is why claims that they “think like us” deserve caution.

17. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • An artificial neuron computes f(w·x + b); alone, it is a linear classifier such as logistic regression.
  • Hidden layers with nonlinear activations let networks learn their own features and solve problems like XOR; without nonlinearity, layers collapse into one.
  • One hidden layer can approximate any continuous function in principle (universal approximation), but depth is usually more efficient.
  • Backpropagation computes every gradient in one backward pass using the chain rule.
  • Saturating activations such as the sigmoid cause vanishing gradients in deep networks; ReLU-like activations with suitable initialisation keep gradients flowing.
  • Neural-network losses are non-convex: results can depend on the random start, and larger networks are often easier to train.
