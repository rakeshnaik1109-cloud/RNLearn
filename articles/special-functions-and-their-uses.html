1. Why Special Functions?

School mathematics gives us a small toolkit: powers and roots, exponentials and logarithms, sines and cosines. With these elementary functions you can describe a falling stone, a growing population or a swinging pendulum, as long as the swing is small. But ask a slightly harder question and the toolkit runs out. What fraction of a normal distribution lies within two standard deviations? How does a drum vibrate? What does an electron in a hydrogen atom look like? How long does a pendulum take when it swings through a large angle? None of the answers can be written with elementary functions.

The answers are special functions: the gamma function, the error function, Bessel functions, Legendre and Hermite polynomials, Airy functions, elliptic integrals and their relatives. They were discovered one problem at a time over three centuries, named after the people who studied them, and collected into great handbooks. Today they are built into every scientific library, and anyone who works with waves, heat, quantum mechanics, probability or signal processing meets them constantly.

This article introduces the most useful ones: where each comes from, what it looks like, what it is used for, and how to compute it in Python. You need only basic calculus to follow it.

3. What Makes a Function “Special”?

There is no formal definition. A special function is one that turns up so often, in so many problems, that it earns a name, a symbol and a place in the handbooks. Most arise in one of three ways:

  • As an integral that cannot be done in elementary terms. The area under the bell curve, \(\int e^{-t^2}\,dt\), is the classic case: it defines the error function.
  • As the solution of a differential equation. When the wave, heat or Schrödinger equation is solved in circular, spherical or other coordinates, it splits into simpler ordinary differential equations, and each one has its own family of solutions: Bessel functions for cylinders, Legendre functions for spheres, Hermite functions for a quantum spring.
  • As an extension of something discrete. The gamma function stretches the factorial n! from whole numbers to all real and complex numbers.

Many special functions come in families labelled by a whole number (J0, J1, J2… or P0, P1, P2…) and are orthogonal: like the sines and cosines of a Fourier series, they can be combined to represent almost any function, each capturing a different “mode”. That is why they appear whenever we break a complicated shape, vibration or wave into simple parts.

The handbooks

The Handbook of Mathematical Functions edited by Milton Abramowitz and Irene Stegun, published by the US National Bureau of Standards in 1964, became one of the most cited works in the mathematical sciences. [1] Its successor, the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions (DLMF), is free online and was published as a book in 2010. [2] When you need a formula, an identity or an asymptotic expansion, look there first.

4. The Gamma Function

The factorial n! = 1 × 2 × 3 × … × n is defined only for whole numbers. What should (½)! be? In 1729, in letters to Christian Goldbach, Leonhard Euler found an expression that gives the factorials at whole numbers and a smooth curve in between; a few months later he wrote it as an integral. [3] In modern form, with the shift by one introduced by Adrien-Marie Legendre, it is:

The gamma function
$$\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty t^{\,x-1} e^{-t}\,dt, \qquad \Gamma(x+1) = x\,\Gamma(x), \qquad \Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$$

Among all the curves that pass through the factorials, the gamma function is the natural one: in 1922 Harald Bohr and Johannes Mollerup proved that it is the only one whose logarithm is convex. One famous value is \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt{\pi}\), which connects the gamma function to the normal distribution.

Where it is used.

  • Probability and statistics: the gamma, beta, chi-square, Student’s t and F distributions all contain Γ in their formulas.
  • Geometry: the volume of a ball of radius r in n dimensions is \(\pi^{n/2} r^n / \Gamma(\tfrac n2 + 1)\), which works for every n.
  • Counting: Stirling’s approximation, \(n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n\), describes how fast factorials grow, and underlies entropy in statistical mechanics.
  • Physics: Γ appears in fractional calculus, in the scattering amplitudes that launched string theory, and in many integrals of physics.

Because Γ(x) grows faster than any exponential, software usually works with its logarithm, ln Γ(x), just as the logarithms article recommends for other very large numbers.

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import gamma, gammaln, beta

# Gamma extends the factorial: gamma(n) = (n - 1)! for whole numbers n
print([gamma(n) for n in range(1, 7)])         # [1, 1, 2, 6, 24, 120]
print(gamma(0.5) ** 2, math.pi)                 # gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi)

# Big factorials overflow; work with their logarithm instead
print(gamma(200.0))                             # inf
print(gammaln(200.0))                           # 857.93 = ln(199!)

# The beta function is built from gamma: B(a, b) = G(a) G(b) / G(a + b)
print(beta(2, 3), gamma(2) * gamma(3) / gamma(5))   # 0.08333 both

x = np.linspace(-4.5, 5, 2000)
y = gamma(x)
y[np.abs(y) > 30] = np.nan                      # break the curve at the poles
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.plot(x, y)
plt.plot(range(1, 6), [math.factorial(n - 1) for n in range(1, 6)], "o", label="(n - 1)!")
plt.axhline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5)
plt.ylim(-10, 25)
plt.xlabel("x")
plt.ylabel("Γ(x)")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5. The Beta Function and Probability

A close relative of gamma is the beta function, which Euler also studied:

The beta function
$$B(a,b) = \int_0^1 p^{\,a-1}(1-p)^{\,b-1}\,dp = \frac{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}$$

Dividing the integrand by B(a, b) makes it a probability distribution on the interval from 0 to 1, the beta distribution. It is the standard way to express uncertainty about a proportion: the success rate of a treatment, the click-through rate of a web page, the fraction of defective parts. After seeing s successes and f failures, starting from a flat prior, Bayes’ rule gives a beta distribution with a = s + 1 and b = f + 1.

The beta function also gives the binomial coefficients for non-integer arguments, and its incomplete version computes the tail probabilities of the binomial, t and F distributions inside statistics software.

6. The Error Function

The normal distribution has the familiar bell-shaped density, but its area, the probability of falling in a range, has no elementary formula. It needs the error function, named and given the symbol erf by the English mathematician James Glaisher in 1871. [4]

Error function
$$\operatorname{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt, \qquad P(|Z| < k) = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt 2}\right)$$

The name comes from the theory of errors of measurement, where the normal distribution first became important. Its complement, erfc(x) = 1 − erf(x), is the probability of a large deviation. The error function also describes diffusion: when heat flows into a cold slab, or dye spreads from a wall into still water, the profile is an erfc of distance divided by the square root of time. That square root is why a smell spreads across a still room so slowly, and why heating a thick roast takes far longer than a thin steak. In communications, erfc gives the bit-error rate of a digital signal in noise.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import erf, erfc, erfinv
from scipy.stats import norm

# Probability that a normal value lies within k standard deviations of the mean
for k in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(f"within {k} sd: {erf(k / np.sqrt(2)):.5f}   (scipy.stats: {norm.cdf(k) - norm.cdf(-k):.5f})")

# Tail probabilities far out need erfc, not 1 - erf (which rounds to zero)
print(1 - erf(7), erfc(7))                     # 0.0 vs 4.18e-23

# Inverse: how many sd contain 95% of a normal distribution?
print(np.sqrt(2) * erfinv(0.95))               # 1.95996

# Diffusion: dye released at a wall spreads as C/C0 = erfc(x / (2 sqrt(D t)))
D = 1e-9                                       # m^2/s, a small molecule in water
for hours in [1, 24]:
    t = hours * 3600
    print(f"after {hours:>2} h, concentration 5 mm away is {erfc(5e-3 / (2 * np.sqrt(D * t))):.3f} of the wall value")

7. Bessel Functions

Whenever waves or heat spread in a circle or a cylinder, Bessel functions appear. They are the solutions of Bessel’s equation,

Bessel’s equation
$$x^2 y'' + x y' + (x^2 - n^2)\,y = 0, \qquad J_n(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\cos(n\tau - x\sin\tau)\,d\tau$$

The functions Jn(x) look like slowly decaying cosine waves whose zeros are not evenly spaced. Daniel Bernoulli met J0 in 1732 while studying a swinging hanging chain; Euler met them studying the vibrations of a drum, and Joseph Fourier used them in 1822 for heat flow in a cylinder. [5] [6] The astronomer Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel made the first systematic study in 1824, while working out how the Sun’s motion disturbs the orbits of the planets. [7]

Where they are used.

  • Drums and membranes. The frequencies of a circular drum are proportional to the zeros of Bessel functions, which is why drums sound less tuned than strings.
  • Optics. Light through a circular lens or telescope mirror forms a bright spot surrounded by faint rings, the Airy disk, whose shape is \([2J_1(x)/x]^2\). George Airy computed it in 1835. [8] The first dark ring falls at an angle of 1.22 λ/D, which sets the sharpest detail any telescope or microscope of aperture D can resolve.
  • Radio and music. A frequency-modulated signal splits into sidebands whose strengths are Bessel functions of the modulation depth. John Chowning used this in 1973 to create FM synthesis, the sound of many 1980s keyboards. [9]
  • Engineering. Heat flow in pipes, vibrations of circular plates, electromagnetic waves in optical fibres and coaxial cables, and the flow of water through a well into an aquifer.
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import jv, jn_zeros

# Zeros of J_n set the frequencies of a circular drum
print(jn_zeros(0, 3))        # [2.405  5.520  8.654]
print(jn_zeros(1, 3))        # [3.832  7.016 10.173]
# A drum's overtones are not whole-number multiples of its fundamental:
print(jn_zeros(1, 1) / jn_zeros(0, 1))   # 1.593, not 2

# Shape of the drum mode with n = 2 nodal diameters and m = 1 nodal circle (plus the rim)
n, m = 2, 2
k = jn_zeros(n, m)[-1]
r, th = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(0, 1, 200), np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 400))
z = jv(n, k * r) * np.cos(n * th)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2))
x = np.linspace(0, 20, 500)
for order in range(3):
    ax1.plot(x, jv(order, x), label=f"J{order}(x)")
ax1.axhline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5)
ax1.set_xlabel("x")
ax1.legend()
ax2.pcolormesh(r * np.cos(th), r * np.sin(th), z, cmap="RdBu_r", shading="gouraud")
ax2.set_aspect("equal")
ax2.set_title(f"Drum mode (n = {n}, m = {m})")
ax2.axis("off")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Legendre Polynomials and Spherical Harmonics

For problems with spherical symmetry, such as the gravity of a planet, the field of an atom or the glow of the cosmic microwave background, the natural building blocks are Legendre polynomials. Legendre introduced them in 1785 to calculate the gravitational pull of a slightly flattened planet. [10] The first few are

Legendre polynomials
$$P_0 = 1,\quad P_1 = x,\quad P_2 = \tfrac12(3x^2-1),\quad P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3-3x), \qquad \int_{-1}^{1} P_l\,P_k\,dx = \frac{2\,\delta_{lk}}{2l+1}$$

with x = cos θ, the cosine of the angle from an axis. The integral says they are orthogonal: different degrees do not overlap, so any function of angle can be split into a sum of them, its multipole expansion. The distant field of any charge distribution is a sum of a monopole (P0), a dipole (P1), a quadrupole (P2) and so on.

Adding dependence on the angle around the axis gives the spherical harmonics Ylm(θ, φ), the analogue of a Fourier series on the surface of a sphere. They describe the shapes of atomic orbitals (s, p, d and f for l = 0, 1, 2, 3), the Earth’s gravity and magnetic fields, the patterns of hot and cold spots in the cosmic microwave background, and the lighting of 3D scenes in computer graphics. Legendre polynomials also supply the points of Gaussian quadrature, the most efficient general way to compute an integral numerically, which Gauss devised in 1814.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import eval_legendre, sph_harm_y
from scipy.integrate import quad

x = np.linspace(-1, 1, 400)
plt.figure(figsize=(6.5, 4))
for l in range(5):
    plt.plot(x, eval_legendre(l, x), label=f"P{l}(x)")
plt.xlabel("x = cos θ")
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# Orthogonality: the integral of P_l P_k over [-1, 1] is zero unless l = k
print(quad(lambda t: eval_legendre(2, t) * eval_legendre(3, t), -1, 1)[0])   # ~0
print(quad(lambda t: eval_legendre(3, t) ** 2, -1, 1)[0], 2 / 7)             # 2/(2l+1)

# Spherical harmonics Y_l^m(theta, phi): the angular shapes of atomic orbitals
theta, phi = np.pi / 3, 0.0                   # polar angle, then azimuth (SciPy >= 1.15 order)
print(sph_harm_y(1, 0, theta, phi))           # sqrt(3/(4 pi)) cos(theta) = 0.2443

9. Hermite and Laguerre: Special Functions of Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics made special functions indispensable. In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger solved his new wave equation for the hydrogen atom and for the harmonic oscillator, and found that the solutions were special functions that mathematicians had studied decades earlier. [11]

  • The quantum harmonic oscillator, a particle on a spring, has wavefunctions that are Hermite polynomials times a Gaussian bell. They are named after Charles Hermite, who studied them in 1864, [12] though Laplace and Chebyshev had met them earlier. Their energies are equally spaced, E = (n + ½)ħω.
  • The hydrogen atom has wavefunctions that are a Laguerre polynomial in the distance from the nucleus times a spherical harmonic in the direction.

The harmonic oscillator is the starting point for almost everything that vibrates in quantum physics: molecules, crystal lattices, and even the electromagnetic field, whose quanta are photons. Hermite functions are also the eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform, which makes them useful in signal processing and optics.

import math
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import eval_hermite

def psi(n, x):
    """Quantum harmonic oscillator eigenfunction, in units where m = omega = hbar = 1."""
    norm = 1 / math.sqrt(2**n * math.factorial(n) * math.sqrt(math.pi))
    return norm * eval_hermite(n, x) * np.exp(-x**2 / 2)

x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 600)
plt.figure(figsize=(6, 5))
plt.plot(x, x**2 / 2, color="grey", lw=1)           # the potential well
for n in range(5):
    E = n + 0.5                                        # energy levels are equally spaced
    plt.plot(x, E + 0.7 * psi(n, x), label=f"n = {n}, E = {E}")
    plt.axhline(E, color="grey", lw=0.3)
plt.ylim(0, 5.5)
plt.xlabel("position x")
plt.ylabel("energy, with wavefunctions drawn at their level")
plt.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

# Check the normalisation: total probability 1
print(f"{np.trapezoid(psi(3, x) ** 2, x):.6f}")         # 1.000000

10. Chebyshev Polynomials and Approximation

How does a calculator compute sin x or eˣ? Usually by a carefully chosen polynomial. The best choices come from the Chebyshev polynomials, Tn(cos θ) = cos(nθ), introduced by Pafnuty Chebyshev in 1854 while designing mechanical linkages for steam engines. Among all polynomials of degree n with leading coefficient 1, the scaled Chebyshev polynomial stays closest to zero over the interval from −1 to 1. Expanding a function in Chebyshev polynomials gives an approximation that is close to the best possible.

The choice of points matters greatly. In 1901 Carl Runge showed that fitting a polynomial through equally spaced points can go badly wrong: as the degree rises, the polynomial swings ever more wildly near the ends of the interval. [13] Using Chebyshev points, which cluster near the ends, cures the problem.

Chebyshev approximation underlies the mathematics libraries of computers, spectral methods for solving differential equations, the design of electronic filters (Chebyshev filters), and the software system Chebfun, which represents functions to machine precision by Chebyshev expansions. [14]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from numpy.polynomial import Chebyshev, Polynomial

f = lambda x: 1 / (1 + 25 * x**2)                    # Runge's function
n = 14
xs = np.linspace(-1, 1, 1000)

equi = np.linspace(-1, 1, n + 1)                      # equally spaced points
p_equi = Polynomial.fit(equi, f(equi), n)
cheb = np.cos((2 * np.arange(n + 1) + 1) * np.pi / (2 * n + 2))   # Chebyshev points
p_cheb = Chebyshev.fit(cheb, f(cheb), n)

print(f"max error, equally spaced: {np.max(np.abs(p_equi(xs) - f(xs))):.2f}")
print(f"max error, Chebyshev:      {np.max(np.abs(p_cheb(xs) - f(xs))):.4f}")

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4))
plt.plot(xs, f(xs), "k", lw=2, label="f(x) = 1 / (1 + 25x²)")
plt.plot(xs, p_equi(xs), label=f"degree {n}, equally spaced points")
plt.plot(xs, p_cheb(xs), label=f"degree {n}, Chebyshev points")
plt.plot(equi, f(equi), "o", ms=4, color="C0")
plt.plot(cheb, f(cheb), "s", ms=4, color="C1")
plt.ylim(-1, 2)
plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

11. Airy Functions

The simplest equation whose solutions change from oscillating to decaying is the Airy equation, y″ = xy. For negative x it behaves like a wave equation; for positive x, like exponential growth or decay. Its two solutions are written Ai(x) and Bi(x).

George Biddell Airy, Britain’s Astronomer Royal, introduced the function in 1838 to explain the rainbow. [15] Ray optics predicted infinite brightness at the rainbow’s edge, where rays bunch up into a caustic. Airy showed that waves smooth this into a bright band followed by fainter supernumerary bows, the pale pink and green fringes sometimes seen just inside a bright rainbow.

The same function describes a quantum particle near a classical turning point, which makes it central to the WKB approximation and to tunnelling; a particle bouncing on a floor under gravity; the bright lines at the bottom of a swimming pool; and “Airy beams” of light that seem to curve as they travel.

12. Elliptic Integrals and the True Pendulum

The arc length of an ellipse cannot be written in elementary terms. Neither can the period of a pendulum that swings through a large angle. Both need elliptic integrals, which occupied mathematicians through the eighteenth century and which Legendre organised into standard forms in his Traité des fonctions elliptiques (1825–1828). Niels Henrik Abel and Carl Jacobi then had the idea of inverting them, creating the elliptic functions that later led to elliptic curves and modern cryptography.

Complete elliptic integral and the pendulum
$$K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\phi}}, \qquad T = 4\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\;K\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}\right)$$

Elliptic integrals give the circumference of an ellipse (and so of planetary orbits), the magnetic field of a circular loop of wire, the sag of a cable, the shape of a bent elastic rod, and the motion of a spinning top. They can be computed extremely fast with the arithmetic–geometric mean, an iteration Gauss studied, which doubles the number of correct digits at every step.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import ellipk
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

g, L = 9.81, 1.0
T0 = 2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(L / g)                       # small-swing period, 2.006 s

def period_exact(theta0):
    # SciPy's ellipk takes the parameter m = k^2, with k = sin(theta0 / 2)
    return 4 * np.sqrt(L / g) * ellipk(np.sin(theta0 / 2) ** 2)

def period_numeric(theta0):
    # integrate the full equation theta'' = -(g/L) sin(theta) and time a quarter swing
    hit = lambda t, y: y[0]
    hit.terminal, hit.direction = True, -1
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, y: [y[1], -g / L * np.sin(y[0])], [0, 10], [theta0, 0],
                    events=hit, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
    return 4 * sol.t_events[0][0]

for deg in [5, 30, 90, 150, 179]:
    th = np.radians(deg)
    print(f"{deg:>3}°: exact {period_exact(th):.4f} s, numerical {period_numeric(th):.4f} s, "
          f"{period_exact(th) / T0:.3f} × the small-swing period")

13. Fresnel Integrals and the Cornu Spiral

When light passes the edge of an obstacle, the shadow is not sharp: there are bright and dark fringes on the lit side and a gradual fade into the dark. Augustin Fresnel explained this in his prize memoir of 1818 using two integrals now named after him:

Fresnel integrals
$$C(u) = \int_0^u \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)dt, \qquad S(u) = \int_0^u \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)dt$$

Plotting S against C draws a beautiful double spiral, the Cornu spiral, named after the French physicist Alfred Cornu, who drew it accurately in 1874. Euler had studied the same curve more than a century earlier. The brightness at any point past the edge is proportional to the squared length of a chord across the spiral.

The curve’s curvature grows in proportion to its length, so a car or train following it turns the steering wheel at a steady rate. Under the name clothoid or Euler spiral it forms the transition curves between straight and curved sections of railways and motorways, and the loops of some roller coasters.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import airy, fresnel

x = np.linspace(-15, 4, 800)
Ai, Aip, Bi, Bip = airy(x)

S, C = fresnel(np.linspace(-6, 6, 3000))              # scipy returns S first, then C
u = np.linspace(-3, 6, 800)
Su, Cu = fresnel(u)
edge = 0.5 * ((Cu + 0.5) ** 2 + (Su + 0.5) ** 2)       # light intensity past a straight edge

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 3.8))
axes[0].plot(x, Ai, label="Ai(x)")
axes[0].plot(x, Bi, label="Bi(x)")
axes[0].set_ylim(-0.6, 1.2)
axes[0].set_title("Airy functions")
axes[0].legend()
axes[1].plot(C, S, lw=1)
axes[1].set_aspect("equal")
axes[1].set_title("Cornu (Euler) spiral: (C(t), S(t))")
axes[2].plot(u, edge)
axes[2].axhline(1, color="grey", lw=0.5)
axes[2].axvline(0, color="grey", lw=0.5)
axes[2].set_title("Intensity past a straight edge")
axes[2].set_xlabel("distance into the lit side (scaled)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

14. The Riemann Zeta Function

The zeta function, \(\zeta(s) = 1 + 2^{-s} + 3^{-s} + \dots\), began with Euler’s 1734 solution of the Basel problem, ζ(2) = π²/6, and his discovery that ζ(s) can be written as a product over the prime numbers. Riemann extended it to complex numbers in 1859, and the unproved Riemann Hypothesis about its zeros is the most famous open problem in mathematics. Its story is told in The Riemann Hypothesis, and the Claimed Proof Found with Claude.

Zeta values also turn up in physics: ζ(4) = π⁴/90 fixes the constant in the Stefan–Boltzmann law of blackbody radiation, ζ(3/2) ≈ 2.612 sets the temperature at which a gas forms a Bose–Einstein condensate, and the zeta function is used to tame infinite sums in quantum field theory.

15. Hypergeometric Functions: One Family for Many

Many special functions turn out to be members of one family. The Gauss hypergeometric function, which Gauss studied systematically in 1812, [16] is the series

Gauss hypergeometric function
$$ {}_2F_1(a,b;c;z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_n (b)_n}{(c)_n}\frac{z^n}{n!}, \qquad (a)_n = a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)$$

By choosing a, b and c you get ln(1 + z), arcsin z, Legendre and Chebyshev polynomials, the complete elliptic integrals and many more. For example, K(k) = (π/2)·₂F₁(½, ½; 1; k²). Generalised hypergeometric functions include Bessel, Hermite, Laguerre and error functions too. This is why computer algebra systems can evaluate so many integrals: they reduce them to hypergeometric form.

16. The Lambert W Function

Some equations mix a variable with its exponential, like x eˣ = 3, and cannot be solved with logarithms. Their solutions use the Lambert W function, defined by W(z) eW(z) = z. Johann Lambert and Euler considered such equations in the eighteenth century, but the function was named and popularised only in 1996, in a paper by Robert Corless, Gaston Gonnet, David Hare, David Jeffrey and Donald Knuth. [17]

It gives the peak wavelength of blackbody radiation in Wien’s displacement law, the current in a diode with a series resistance, the time course of an enzyme reaction (the integrated Michaelis–Menten equation), the depth of water flowing over a weir, and the solution of equations such as 2ˣ = 4x. Like the logarithm of a negative number in complex analysis, it has several branches, and for −1/e < z < 0 there are two real solutions.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import lambertw
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# Lambert W solves w * exp(w) = z. Many equations mixing x and exp(x) reduce to it.

# Wien's displacement law: the peak of the blackbody spectrum needs x = 5 (1 - exp(-x))
x = 5 + lambertw(-5 * np.exp(-5)).real
print(f"x = {x:.6f}")                                  # 4.965114
h, c, k = 6.62607015e-34, 2.99792458e8, 1.380649e-23
print(f"Wien constant b = hc/(x k) = {h * c / (x * k) * 1e3:.4f} mm·K")   # 2.8978

# Check against a general-purpose root finder
print(brentq(lambda t: t - 5 * (1 - np.exp(-t)), 1, 10))

# Solve 2^x = 4x: rewrite as -x ln2 e^(-x ln2) = -ln2 / 4, which has two real solutions
z = -np.log(2) / 4
for branch in (0, -1):
    print(f"branch {branch:>2}: x = {(-lambertw(z, branch) / np.log(2)).real:.4f}")   # 0.3099 and 4

17. Computing Special Functions

You will rarely need to write your own. In Python, scipy.special provides several hundred special functions, vectorised over NumPy arrays and carefully tested. [18] Other options are mpmath for arbitrary precision, the GNU Scientific Library and Boost in C and C++, and Mathematica, Maple and Julia’s SpecialFunctions package.

It still helps to know how these libraries work, because the methods explain their limits:

  • Power series are accurate for small arguments but lose precision for large ones, when huge terms cancel.
  • Asymptotic expansions are accurate for large arguments. Libraries switch between series and asymptotics at a carefully chosen point.
  • Recurrence relations, such as (n + 1)Pn+1 = (2n + 1)xPn − nPn−1, are fast but may be unstable in one direction; Bessel functions of high order are computed by running the recurrence backwards.
  • Rational and Chebyshev approximations, fitted once by experts, give machine precision over fixed ranges with a handful of operations.
  • Logarithmic and scaled versions, such as gammaln, erfcx and jve, avoid overflow and underflow.

18. Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up conventions. Γ(n) = (n − 1)!, not n!. SciPy’s ellipk(m) takes the parameter m = k², not the modulus k. Spherical harmonics come with several sign and normalisation conventions, and SciPy’s newer sph_harm_y takes the polar angle before the azimuth, unlike the older sph_harm.
  • Computing 1 − erf(x) for large x. It rounds to zero. Use erfc, or the log of the normal tail.
  • Computing Γ(x) when you need a ratio. Γ(171) overflows a double. Compute gammaln(a) − gammaln(b) and exponentiate at the end.
  • Fitting high-degree polynomials at equally spaced points. Use Chebyshev points or a different method.
  • Using the small-angle pendulum formula for large swings. At 90° it is wrong by 18%.
  • Ignoring branches. Lambert W and the square root of a negative argument have more than one value; make sure the software returns the one you need.
  • Trusting values far outside the tested range. Check against a second method or mpmath at high precision when it matters.

19. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who discovered Bessel functions?

The legend: Bessel discovered Bessel functions in 1824.

The record: Daniel Bernoulli met the series for J0 in 1732 when studying a hanging chain, Euler met Bessel’s equation when studying a vibrating drum, and Fourier used J0 in his 1822 theory of heat. [6] [5] Bessel’s contribution, in his 1824 study of planetary perturbations, was the first systematic investigation of the whole family. [7] The name stuck, as names often do, to the person who made the subject his own rather than to the first to touch it.

A second legend: Galileo discovered that a pendulum takes the same time to swing however wide its arc. The record: Galileo did claim this, but it is only true for small swings: Figure 10 shows a 90° swing takes 18% longer. Christiaan Huygens found a clock that is truly isochronous by making the bob follow a cycloid instead of a circle, and published the theory in his Horologium Oscillatorium of 1673. [19] The exact period of an ordinary pendulum had to wait for elliptic integrals.
