Counting the Cosmos: How Many Stars and Galaxies, and the Matter We Cannot See

Nobody can count a hundred billion stars. Astronomers sample a patch and scale up, or weigh the whole and divide. Weighing galaxies revealed the biggest surprise of all: most of the matter in the universe is invisible.

1. How Many Are There?

“How many stars are there?” is one of the oldest questions children ask, and one of the hardest to answer. Parts 8 to 10 measured distances: to the stars, across the Galaxy, and to the edge of the observable universe. This part asks a different kind of question. How many stars are in the Milky Way? How many galaxies are in the universe? And how much do they weigh?

Nobody can count a hundred billion stars one by one. Astronomers use two tricks that anyone can use: count a small sample and scale up, or weigh the whole and divide by the weight of a typical member. Applied to galaxies, the second trick gave one of the biggest surprises in modern science: most of the matter in the universe is invisible. [1]

3. The Stars You Can See

The sky on a dark night seems to hold countless stars, but the number visible to the naked eye is surprisingly small. Ptolemy’s Almagest, written in Alexandria around 150 CE, catalogued 1,022 stars. The modern Yale Bright Star Catalogue lists about 9,100 stars brighter than magnitude 6.5, the limit for good eyes under excellent skies, and since half the sky is below the horizon at any time, you can see at most a few thousand at once. From a city, it may be only a few dozen.

Telescopes change everything. Galileo’s small telescope showed ten times more stars than the eye, and every larger telescope has revealed more. Counting them all individually was never an option.

4. Counting by Sampling

The solution is the same one used by opinion polls: count a sample and scale up. William Herschel’s star gauges of 1785, described in Part 9, counted stars in hundreds of small fields. In 1906 Jacobus Kapteyn proposed a systematic version: rather than trying to survey the whole sky, observatories around the world would concentrate on 206 “Selected Areas” spread evenly across it, measuring the numbers, brightnesses, colours and motions of the stars in each. [2] Dozens of observatories took part over the following decades.

Sampling only works if the sample is representative. The Milky Way is far from uniform: stars crowd into a band, and dust hides some regions completely. A few fields chosen at random could land mostly in the band or mostly outside it, which is why Kapteyn chose areas on a regular grid.

5. How Many Stars in the Milky Way?

Counting cannot see through the dust to the far side of the Galaxy. The second trick, weighing, can. The Sun orbits the centre of the Galaxy at about 230 km/s, at a distance of about 8.2 kiloparsecs (Part 9). Just as the Moon’s orbit weighs the Earth (Part 6), the speed of the Sun’s orbit tells us how much mass lies inside it: about a hundred billion times the mass of the Sun.

To turn mass into a number of stars, we need to know how much of the mass is in stars rather than gas and dark matter, and the average mass of a star. Most stars are faint red dwarfs, weighing a tenth to a half of the Sun, and many are too faint to count even nearby. Different reasonable choices give answers between about 100 billion and 400 billion stars, and that range is what you will find in textbooks.

6. Gaia’s Census

The Gaia spacecraft (Part 8) has come closest to a direct count. Its third data release, in 2022, catalogued about 1.8 billion objects, almost all of them stars in the Milky Way, with positions, brightnesses and, for most, distances and motions. [3] It is the largest census of stars ever made, and yet it covers only about one percent of the Galaxy’s stars: those bright enough, near enough and not hidden by dust.

7. How Many Galaxies?

In December 1995 Robert Williams, director of the Space Telescope Science Institute, used his discretionary time on the Hubble Space Telescope for a risky experiment. Hubble stared for ten days at a tiny patch of sky near the Big Dipper that looked completely empty, about one 24-millionth of the whole sky, the size of a grain of sand held at arm’s length. The resulting image, the Hubble Deep Field, showed about 3,000 galaxies, some of them seen as they were more than 10 billion years ago. [4]

A 2004 image, the Hubble Ultra Deep Field, went deeper and found about 10,000 galaxies in a patch about a tenth of the width of the full Moon, roughly one 13-millionth of the sky. [5] If the patch is typical, the whole sky contains about 10,000 × 13 million, or roughly 130 billion galaxies bright enough for Hubble to see. This is the sampling trick on the grandest scale.

8. Two Trillion, or Hundreds of Billions?

Hubble cannot see the faintest and most distant galaxies. In 2016, Christopher Conselice and colleagues combined deep surveys with models of how the numbers of galaxies of different masses change over cosmic time, and estimated that the observable universe contains about two trillion galaxies, ten times more than before, most of them too small and faint to see. [6] The figure was widely reported.

Then a spacecraft far from the Sun offered a check. All the galaxies in the universe, seen and unseen, add up to a faint background glow of the sky, which is almost impossible to measure from near the Earth because sunlight scattered by dust in the solar system is much brighter. In 2021, a team led by Tod Lauer used images taken by NASA’s New Horizons probe, beyond the orbit of Pluto where that dust is thin, to measure the glow. [7] The glow left little room for huge numbers of unseen galaxies, and the team argued that the total is more like a few hundred billion. The exact figure is still debated, but the order of magnitude is now hundreds of billions to around a trillion.

9. Weighing a Cluster: Zwicky’s Missing Mass

The weighing trick also works for groups of galaxies. In 1933 the Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky, at the California Institute of Technology, measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma cluster, a swarm of more than a thousand galaxies. They were moving relative to each other at about 1,000 km/s. Using a theorem from mechanics that links the average speed in a bound system to its mass, he calculated how much mass was needed to stop the cluster flying apart. It was hundreds of times more than the light of the galaxies suggested. Zwicky concluded that the cluster must contain far more “dunkle Materie”, dark matter, than luminous matter. [8]

Zwicky’s factor was too large, partly because he used Hubble’s distance scale (Part 10), and later astronomers found that clusters also contain enormous amounts of hot gas, visible only in X-rays. But even with the gas, the visible matter falls far short. Few astronomers took the problem seriously for forty years.

10. Vera Rubin’s Flat Rotation Curves

Vera Rubin, working at the Carnegie Institution’s Department of Terrestrial Magnetism in Washington, chose to study the rotation of galaxies partly because it seemed uncontroversial. With Kent Ford, who had built a sensitive electronic image-tube spectrograph, she measured the speeds of glowing gas clouds across the Andromeda galaxy in 1970. [9] The speeds did not fall off in the outer parts of the galaxy, as they would if most of the mass were in the bright centre.

In 1980 Rubin, Ford and Norbert Thonnard published the rotation curves of 21 spiral galaxies. All were flat or still rising at the outermost points they could measure. [10] Radio astronomers, tracking hydrogen gas even farther out with its 21-centimetre emission, found the same. Theorists such as Jeremiah Ostriker and James Peebles had meanwhile shown in 1973 that a flat spinning disc of stars would be unstable unless it were embedded in a massive halo. [11] By the early 1980s most astronomers accepted that galaxies sit inside halos of invisible matter several times more massive than their stars.

11. What Is Dark Matter?

Dark matter does not shine, absorb or reflect light; it is detected only through its gravity. Evidence has accumulated from many directions: galaxy rotation, cluster motions, the bending of light by clusters (gravitational lensing), the pattern of spots in the cosmic microwave background (Part 10), and computer simulations showing that galaxies could not have formed so quickly without it. Combined, these suggest that dark matter is about five times as abundant as ordinary matter.

A striking case is the Bullet Cluster, two clusters of galaxies that collided about 150 million years ago. Their hot gas, which contains most of the ordinary matter, collided and slowed down in the middle. But maps of the mass made from gravitational lensing show most of the mass moving on with the galaxies, separated from the gas. [12] Something massive passed through the collision without interacting.

What it is remains unknown. Most physicists suspect a new kind of particle, but decades of searches with ultra-sensitive detectors deep underground, and at particle colliders, have not found one. A minority of researchers prefer to modify the law of gravity itself, as Mordehai Milgrom proposed in 1983; such theories explain galaxy rotation well but struggle with clusters and the cosmic background.

12. The Observable Universe in Numbers

Putting the pieces together gives an inventory. The observable universe, the region from which light has had time to reach us since the big bang, is about 46 billion light-years in radius today, because space has expanded while the light travelled. It contains perhaps a few hundred billion galaxies, and if an average galaxy has tens of billions of stars, something like 1022 stars, with some estimates up to 1024: more than the number of grains of sand on all the Earth’s beaches, by most estimates. The number of atoms is around 1080.

Archimedes attempted the same kind of exercise in The Sand Reckoner (Part 8), estimating how many grains of sand would fill the universe as he understood it. His answer, 1063, required him to invent a new way of writing large numbers. [13] The method, estimating the size of the whole and the size of a part, has not changed.

14. Try It Yourself: Counting by Sampling

Try it yourself: how many stars can you see?

You need a dark, clear night, a cardboard tube (from a roll of kitchen paper) and a notebook.

  1. Look through the tube at a part of the sky away from the Moon and city glow, and count the stars you can see inside the circle.
  2. Repeat in about ten different directions, including some near the Milky Way if you can see it, and some away from it. Write down each count.
  3. Work out the fraction of the sky covered by the tube: if its length is L and its diameter d, the circle covers about d² ÷ (16 L²) of the whole sky (about 1/1,200 for a 4 cm tube 35 cm long).
  4. Multiply your average count by 1,200 (or your own number) and divide by two for the half of the sky above the horizon. That is roughly how many stars you could see at once from your site. Compare it with the few thousand visible under perfect skies.

Indoors, you can do the same with a jar of rice or a printed page of text: count a small square and scale up.

15. How Did They Know? Measuring a Galaxy’s Spin

How did they know? Doppler shifts across a galaxy

To measure rotation, Rubin and Ford placed the narrow slit of a spectrograph along the long axis of a tilted spiral galaxy. Gas clouds on one side of the centre are moving towards us, and their spectral lines are blueshifted; those on the other side are moving away and are redshifted. The spectrum shows each emission line as a tilted, S-shaped streak: its displacement at each point along the slit gives the speed at that distance from the centre (Part 10 explains the shift). [10]

The outer parts of galaxies are faint, and photographic plates alone would have needed impossibly long exposures. Ford’s spectrograph used an image tube, an electronic device that amplified the faint light before it reached the plate, cutting exposures from tens of hours to a few. After correcting for the tilt of the galaxy and its overall motion, each spectrum gave a rotation curve.

Radio telescopes do the same with the 21-centimetre emission of cold hydrogen gas, which extends far beyond a galaxy’s visible stars, so radio rotation curves reach farther out still.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who discovered dark matter?

The legend: Vera Rubin discovered dark matter.

The record: the idea of unseen mass is older. Kapteyn used the phrase “dark matter” in 1922, and Jan Oort estimated the amount of unseen matter near the Sun in 1932. Zwicky found the missing mass in clusters in 1933, Sinclair Smith found the same in the Virgo cluster in 1936, and Horace Babcock measured a puzzlingly high rotation speed in Andromeda’s outskirts in 1939. Radio astronomers found flat rotation curves in the 1970s, at the same time as Rubin. [1] Rubin’s careful optical measurements of many galaxies were crucial in convincing astronomers that the effect was real and universal, which is why her name is attached to it; she herself always emphasised the work of others.

A second legend: Rubin won the Nobel Prize. The record: she did not; she died in 2016. In 2020 the observatory in Chile that will carry out a ten-year survey of the sky was named the Vera C. Rubin Observatory.

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: believing in what cannot be seen

In 1846 Urbain Le Verrier noticed that Uranus was not following its predicted path and proposed an unseen planet pulling on it. Johann Galle found Neptune within a degree of the predicted position. Le Verrier then tried the same trick on the anomalous motion of Mercury and predicted another planet, Vulcan, inside its orbit. Vulcan was never found: the anomaly was explained in 1915 by Einstein’s new theory of gravity.

Dark matter is in the same position as Neptune and Vulcan before they were settled. Either there is unseen matter, or our law of gravity needs changing on large scales. Most astronomers favour unseen matter because the same amount explains many independent observations, but until it is detected directly, the question is not closed. When should we accept something we can only infer, never observe? Is an electron, a black hole or dark matter less real because we know it only through its effects?
