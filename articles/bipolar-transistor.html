The Bipolar Transistor: Amplifiers, Switches and the End of the Vacuum Tube

In December 1947 two physicists pressed gold foil onto germanium and made a crystal amplify. Within a decade transistors had replaced vacuum tubes, and the electronics age began.

1. The Invention That Changed Everything

By the 1940s, telephone networks and the first electronic computers depended on vacuum tubes: glass bulbs with hot filaments that amplified and switched signals. They were bulky, power-hungry and burned out regularly. The ENIAC computer of 1945 had about 17,500 of them. Bell Telephone Laboratories set up a group to find a solid-state replacement, and in December 1947 it succeeded. The transistor, a word coined from “transfer resistor”, amplifies and switches with no heater, in a crystal smaller than a grain of rice. [1]

3. The Point-Contact and Junction Transistors

The Bell Labs group, led by William Shockley, first tried to control a semiconductor’s current with an electric field, as an amplifier would need, and failed: electrons trapped at the surface screened the field. While studying those surface states, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain pressed two closely spaced gold contacts onto a slab of germanium and found that a small current into one contact controlled a larger current through the other. On 16 December 1947 they had a working amplifier, the point-contact transistor. [2]

Shockley, stung at being left out, worked out a better design within weeks: a sandwich of n-type, p-type and n-type semiconductor, the bipolar junction transistor (BJT), so called because both electrons and holes take part. He published its theory in 1949 and, with Morgan Sparks and Gordon Teal, demonstrated working junction transistors in 1951. [3, 4] Junction transistors were more reliable and easier to understand than point contacts, and they dominated electronics for the next two decades. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.

4. How a Bipolar Transistor Works

An npn transistor has a heavily doped n+ emitter, a thin p-type base and an n-type collector: two p–n junctions back to back. In normal (“active”) operation:

  • The base–emitter junction is forward-biased (by about 0.6–0.7 V), so the emitter injects a flood of electrons into the base, exponentially in VBE, exactly as in a diode.
  • The base is so thin (well under a micrometre in modern devices) that almost all those electrons diffuse across it before they can recombine with holes.
  • The base–collector junction is reverse-biased, and its strong field sweeps every electron that reaches it into the collector.

The result is a collector current IC = ISeVBE/VT controlled by the base–emitter voltage, while the base needs only a small current IB = IC/β to replace holes lost to recombination and to back-injection into the emitter. The current gain β is typically 50–300. A tiny base current controls a large collector current: that is amplification. [5, 6]

5. Characteristics, Regions and the Load Line

Plotting collector current against collector–emitter voltage for several base currents gives the transistor’s output characteristics. Three regions matter:

  • Cut-off: no base current, no collector current. The switch is open.
  • Active: IC ≈ βIB, nearly independent of VCE. The transistor behaves as a controlled current source and can amplify.
  • Saturation: both junctions forward-biased; VCE falls to about 0.2 V. The switch is closed.

The curves in the active region slope slightly upwards. In 1952 James Early explained why: raising VCE widens the collector depletion region, which eats into the base, making it thinner and the gain higher. [7] Jewell Ebers and John Moll’s 1954 model, which treats the transistor as two coupled diodes, describes all three regions and is still the basis of circuit simulators. [8]

6. The Transistor as an Amplifier

In the common-emitter amplifier, a resistor RC connects the collector to the supply. A bias network sets a steady collector current; a small signal voltage added to the base then wiggles the collector current, and RC converts the wiggle into a large voltage swing. Because IC is exponential in VBE, its sensitivity, the transconductance, is simply gm = IC/VT, and the voltage gain is

Av = −gmRC = −ICRC/VT

The minus sign means the output is inverted. With 1 mA and 5 kΩ the gain is about −190. The first mass-market product built on this was the pocket transistor radio: the Regency TR-1 of 1954 used four germanium transistors and ran on a small battery. [1]

7. The Transistor as a Switch: Bipolar Logic

Driven between cut-off and saturation, a transistor is a switch, and one transistor with two resistors makes an inverter. The first integrated logic families were built this way: resistor-transistor logic (RTL) in Fairchild’s Micrologic chips of 1961, then diode-transistor logic, then transistor-transistor logic (TTL), whose 7400-series chips were the standard building blocks of digital systems from the mid-1960s into the 1980s. Emitter-coupled logic (ECL), which never lets its transistors saturate, was the fastest of all and powered mainframes and supercomputers such as the Cray-1. [9]

But bipolar gates have a fatal flaw for dense chips: a gate with its transistor on draws current from the supply all the time, even when nothing is changing. A few thousand such gates are fine; a billion are not.

8. Where Bipolar Transistors Live Today

From the 1980s, CMOS (Parts 8 and 9) took over digital logic. Bipolar transistors survived wherever their strengths matter: high transconductance per unit current, excellent matching, low noise and very high speed. They are found in precision analogue circuits, voltage references and sensors, and in radio-frequency front ends. [10]

The fastest are heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs). In 1957 Herbert Kroemer proposed making the emitter from a semiconductor with a wider band gap than the base, so that holes are blocked from flowing back into the emitter even when the base is heavily doped. [11] Silicon–germanium HBTs reach hundreds of gigahertz and drive car radars and optical links, and gallium arsenide HBTs amplify the signals transmitted by most mobile phones. Kroemer shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics. In power electronics, the insulated-gate bipolar transistor (IGBT) combines a MOSFET input with bipolar conduction to switch the motors of electric trains and cars.

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): currents in a transistor

A transistor with β = 100 has a base current of 20 µA in the active region. Find IC, IE and α = IC/IE.

Solution

IC = βIB = 2.0 mA. By Kirchhoff’s current law, IE = IC + IB = 2.02 mA. α = β/(β + 1) = 0.990: 99% of the electrons the emitter injects reach the collector.

Problem 2 (easy): the exponential law

A transistor has IS = 10−15 A. Find IC at VBE = 0.65 V (VT = 25.85 mV), and the change in VBE needed to multiply IC by 10.

Solution

IC = 10−15 × e0.65/0.02585 = 10−15 × e25.1 ≈ 10−15 × 8.3 × 1010 ≈ 83 µA. A tenfold increase needs ΔVBE = VT ln 10 ≈ 60 mV, the same as for a diode.

Problem 3 (medium): amplifier gain

A common-emitter amplifier runs from 10 V with IC = 1 mA and RC = 5 kΩ. Find the collector voltage, gm and the small-signal voltage gain. What output do you expect for a 5 mV peak input?

Solution

VC = 10 − 1 mA × 5 kΩ = 5 V, in the middle of the range, which leaves room to swing both ways. gm = 1 mA / 25.85 mV ≈ 38.7 mS, so Av = −gmRC ≈ −193. A 5 mV input gives about 0.97 V peak output, inverted. (Because of the exponential, the actual swing is slightly larger downwards than upwards: some distortion. Real amplifiers use negative feedback, such as an emitter resistor, to trade gain for linearity.)

Problem 4 (medium): a transistor switch

A 3.3 V microcontroller pin must switch a 20 mA LED load through an npn transistor whose β is at least 100. Choose the base resistor so the transistor saturates with a safety factor of 2.

Solution

The minimum base current is IC/β = 20 mA/100 = 0.2 mA; with a factor of 2, aim for 0.4 mA. The resistor drops 3.3 − 0.7 = 2.6 V, so RB = 2.6 V / 0.4 mA = 6.5 kΩ; a standard 6.2 kΩ resistor works. Overdriving the base guarantees saturation (VCE ≈ 0.2 V, little power wasted in the transistor) even with a low-gain device.

Problem 5 (harder): how fast can it be?

Electrons cross the base by diffusion in an average time τB = WB²/(2Dn). With Dn = 25 cm²/s, estimate τB and the corresponding frequency limit f ≈ 1/(2πτB) for base widths of 1 µm and 0.1 µm.

Solution

For WB = 1 µm = 10−4 cm: τB = 10−8/50 = 200 ps, so f ≈ 0.8 GHz. For 0.1 µm: τB = 2 ps, f ≈ 80 GHz. Because τB ∝ WB², thinning the base tenfold makes it a hundred times faster. Modern silicon–germanium HBTs have bases a few tens of nanometres thick and also use a germanium gradient to add a built-in drift field, reaching cut-off frequencies of several hundred gigahertz.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: terahertz transistors

Wireless systems beyond 5G, imaging and sensing are pushing towards frequencies of 100 GHz to 1 THz, where transistors barely have gain. Silicon–germanium and indium phosphide HBTs are competing with high-electron-mobility transistors for this territory. The limits are set not only by base transit time but by parasitic resistances and capacitances of contacts and wiring that become harder to shrink. How far bipolar devices can be pushed, and whether they can be integrated cheaply alongside CMOS, are open engineering questions.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a transistor amplifies current, so it creates energy”

The myth: a transistor turns a small signal into a big one, so it must be generating power.

The reality: all the output power comes from the supply. The transistor is a valve: the small input controls how much of the supply’s energy reaches the load, and much of the rest is lost as heat in the transistor and resistors. An amplifier is a controlled copier of patterns, not a source of energy.

Why it matters: every amplifier, and every logic gate that restores a weakened signal to a clean 0 or 1, draws power from its supply to do so.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • The point-contact transistor (1947) and the junction transistor (1948–51) replaced vacuum tubes with solid crystals.
  • In an npn transistor, a forward-biased emitter junction injects electrons into a thin base; a reverse-biased collector collects them.
  • IC = ISeVBE/VT and IB = IC/β; regions are cut-off, active and saturation.
  • Amplifier gain is −gmRC with gm = IC/VT; large signals distort and clip.
  • Bipolar logic (RTL, TTL, ECL) drew static power, which is why CMOS replaced it; BJTs and HBTs still rule analogue and RF circuits.
