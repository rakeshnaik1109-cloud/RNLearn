CMOS: Logic Gates, Power and Memory from Complementary Transistors

Pair a transistor that turns on with one that turns off, and a logic gate stops wasting power at rest. That one idea from 1963 made billion-transistor chips possible.

1. The Perfect Partnership

Parts 7 and 8 ended with the same problem: logic gates that draw current whenever their output is low. In 1963 Frank Wanlass and Chih-Tang Sah at Fairchild showed the solution. Pair an n-channel MOSFET, which conducts when its gate is high, with a p-channel MOSFET, which conducts when its gate is low. Connect them in series between the supply and ground, drive both gates with the same input, and in either logic state one transistor is off. Their demonstration gate drew only nanowatts when idle. [1] Complementary MOS (CMOS) was at first slower and more complicated to make, but as chips grew denser its near-zero standby power became decisive. [2]

3. The CMOS Inverter

The CMOS inverter has just two transistors. The pMOS connects the output to VDD (the pull-up); the nMOS connects it to ground (the pull-down). With the input at 0, the pMOS is on and the nMOS off, so the output is 1. With the input at 1, the reverse. In both steady states the output is connected firmly to a supply rail by a conducting transistor, so it sits at a full 0 or VDD, and no current flows through the pair. Current flows only while the input is passing through the middle, when both transistors are partly on.

The voltage transfer characteristic plots output against input. It is flat near the rails and plunges steeply around the switching threshold VM, where Vout = Vin. Because holes have lower mobility, the pMOS is usually made two to three times wider than the nMOS to balance the two and place VM near VDD/2. [3]

4. Noise Margins and Regeneration

Real signals are never exactly 0 or VDD: wires pick up noise from their neighbours, and supply voltages droop. The transfer curve defines how much is tolerable. The input levels at which the slope is −1, VIL and VIH, mark the edges of the steep region. The noise margins are NML = VIL − VOL and NMH = VOH − VIH: how far a low or high signal can be disturbed and still be read correctly by the next gate.

The crucial property is gain. Around VM, a small change in input produces a much larger change in output, so a slightly degraded input is pushed back to a clean rail. A signal passing through a chain of gates is therefore restored at every stage, rather than accumulating errors as an analogue signal would. This regeneration is what makes digital computation with billions of imperfect devices possible, and it requires transistors with gain: a reason why passive switches alone cannot build large computers.

5. NAND, NOR and Complex Gates

Every static CMOS gate follows the same recipe: an nMOS pull-down network that connects the output to ground when the function should be 0, and a pMOS pull-up network that connects it to VDD when the function should be 1. The two are duals: transistors in series in one appear in parallel in the other.

  • NAND: nMOS in series (both inputs must be 1 to pull down), pMOS in parallel. Four transistors.
  • NOR: nMOS in parallel, pMOS in series. Four transistors.
  • Combined functions such as NOT(A·B + C) can be built as a single compound gate.

CMOS gates naturally invert, so AND and OR need an extra inverter. Since series transistors add resistance, and pMOS transistors are already weaker, designers prefer NAND-based logic to NOR-based logic and avoid long series stacks. [2]

6. Delay and Power

When a gate switches, its output must charge or discharge the capacitance of the wires and gate inputs it drives through the resistance of its on transistors. Its delay is roughly tp ≈ 0.69 RonCload (Part 4). Since Ron falls as the gate voltage rises above threshold, the delay grows rapidly as VDD approaches VT; Takayasu Sakurai and Richard Newton’s alpha-power law, tp ∝ VDD/(VDD − VT)α with α between 1 and 2, captures this for short-channel transistors. [4]

The power has two parts. Dynamic power, αCVDD²f, is spent charging capacitances. Static power, IleakVDD, comes from subthreshold and other leakage through the transistors that are off (Part 8). Lowering VDD cuts dynamic energy quadratically but slows everything down, giving leakage longer to act. The energy per operation therefore has a minimum, typically near or just below the threshold voltage, which ultra-low-power chips exploit. [5]

7. Memory Cells: SRAM, DRAM and Flash

The same transistors build memories, which occupy a large share of most chips:

  • SRAM stores a bit in two cross-coupled CMOS inverters (a latch, Part 3), with two access transistors connecting it to a pair of bit lines when its word line is raised: six transistors in all. It is fast and needs no refresh, and forms the caches of processors (Part 12). Hitachi showed in 1978 that CMOS SRAM could match the speed of nMOS memory at a fraction of the power. [6]
  • DRAM stores a bit as charge on a capacitor reached through one transistor (Robert Dennard’s 1966 cell). [7] It is much denser but must be refreshed, and reading it is delicate: the tiny cell shares its charge with a long bit line, producing a signal of only about a hundred millivolts that a sense amplifier must detect.
  • Flash stores a bit as charge trapped on a floating gate buried in the oxide, which shifts the transistor’s threshold voltage and keeps data for years without power. Dawon Kahng and Simon Sze proposed the floating gate in 1967; Fujio Masuoka’s team at Toshiba presented flash memory, erasable in whole blocks at once, in 1984. [8, 9] Modern flash stores several bits per cell in different charge levels and stacks cells in hundreds of layers.

8. Designing with Billions of Transistors

Nobody draws billions of transistors by hand. In 1980 Carver Mead and Lynn Conway published a textbook that separated chip design from manufacturing through simple, scalable layout rules, letting university students design working chips. [10] Their approach grew into today’s flow: designers describe behaviour in a hardware description language; synthesis software maps it onto a library of pre-designed standard cells (gates, flip-flops); placement and routing tools arrange the cells and wire them up; and timing analysis checks every path against the clock (Part 3).

CMOS has its own hazards. The pMOS and nMOS transistors, with their wells and substrate, form parasitic bipolar transistors that can latch into a short circuit from supply to ground (latch-up) if triggered, which is prevented by layout rules and guard rings. Variability between nominally identical transistors, and the electromigration of metal atoms in wires carrying high current densities, also have to be designed around. [2]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): counting transistors

How many transistors are needed for a 2-input AND gate in static CMOS, and for a 3-input NAND? Which transistors are in series in the 3-input NAND?

Solution

AND = NAND (4) + inverter (2) = 6 transistors. A 3-input NAND needs 6 transistors: three nMOS in series in the pull-down network (all inputs must be 1 to pull the output low) and three pMOS in parallel in the pull-up network.

Problem 2 (easy): noise margins

An inverter at VDD = 0.8 V has VOL = 0.02 V, VOH = 0.78 V, VIL = 0.34 V and VIH = 0.46 V. Find the noise margins.

Solution

NML = VIL − VOL = 0.34 − 0.02 = 0.32 V; NMH = VOH − VIH = 0.78 − 0.46 = 0.32 V. Symmetric margins of 40% of the supply: a well-balanced inverter.

Problem 3 (medium): gate delay and a ring oscillator

An inverter with an effective on-resistance of 5 kΩ drives 2 fF. Estimate its propagation delay. What is the oscillation frequency of a ring of 31 such inverters (an odd number connected in a loop)?

Solution

tp ≈ 0.69 × 5 kΩ × 2 fF = 6.9 ps. A transition must travel round the ring twice (once falling, once rising) for one full period: T = 2 × 31 × 6.9 ps ≈ 428 ps, so f ≈ 2.3 GHz. Chip makers build ring oscillators on every wafer to measure gate delay directly.

Problem 4 (medium): dynamic and static power

A chip has 2 × 109 switching nodes of 0.5 fF each, activity factor α = 0.05, f = 3 GHz and VDD = 0.75 V. Its leakage current is 5 A. Find the dynamic and static power.

Solution

Total capacitance C = 2 × 109 × 0.5 fF = 1 µF. Dynamic: αCV²f = 0.05 × 10−6 × 0.5625 × 3 × 109 ≈ 84 W. Static: 5 A × 0.75 V = 3.75 W. Dynamic power dominates while the chip is busy, but leakage is paid even when it is idle, which is why idle parts of chips are switched off entirely (“power gating”).

Problem 5 (harder): reading a DRAM cell

A DRAM cell of 20 fF holds either 1.0 V (a “1”) or 0 V (a “0”). Its bit line, of capacitance 80 fF, is precharged to 0.5 V. When the access transistor opens, charge is shared. What voltage does the bit line reach in each case, and why is a sense amplifier needed?

Solution

Charge is conserved: V = (CcellVcell + CBLVBL)/(Ccell + CBL). For a 1: (20 × 1.0 + 80 × 0.5)/100 = 0.60 V; for a 0: (0 + 40)/100 = 0.40 V. The signal is only ±100 mV around 0.5 V, and reading destroys the stored charge. A sense amplifier, a cross-coupled latch, amplifies the small difference to full levels and writes the value back into the cell. Larger cell-to-bit-line capacitance ratios give bigger signals, which is why DRAM makers fight to keep cell capacitance up as cells shrink.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: the memory wall and new memories

SRAM cells have stopped shrinking as fast as logic at the newest manufacturing nodes, and DRAM cell capacitors are becoming extremely tall and thin, so memory density is improving more slowly than it used to. Meanwhile, moving data between memory and processor costs far more energy than computing on it. Researchers are developing new non-volatile memories, such as magnetic (MRAM), phase-change and resistive (RRAM) memories, and placing computation inside or beside memory arrays. Which of these will find large markets beyond niches such as embedded MRAM is still uncertain.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “CMOS uses no power when idle”

The myth: CMOS gates only use energy when they switch, so an idle chip draws nothing.

The reality: that was nearly true for the CMOS of the 1970s and 1980s, with high threshold voltages and thick oxides. Modern transistors leak through subthreshold conduction, gate-oxide tunnelling and junctions, and in some chips leakage has reached a large fraction of total power. Phones and laptops therefore cut the power to idle blocks completely and lower voltages dynamically.

Why it matters: managing leakage is now as important to battery life as reducing switching energy.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • A CMOS inverter pairs a pMOS pull-up with an nMOS pull-down; in either state one is off, so idle gates draw almost no current.
  • The transfer curve’s steep, high-gain middle gives noise margins and restores degraded signals at every stage.
  • Static CMOS gates use dual pull-up and pull-down networks: NAND has series nMOS and parallel pMOS; NOR the reverse.
  • Delay ≈ 0.69 RonC; power = αCV²f + IleakV; energy per operation has a minimum near threshold.
  • SRAM (6 transistors), DRAM (1 transistor + 1 capacitor) and flash (floating gate) are all built from MOS transistors.
