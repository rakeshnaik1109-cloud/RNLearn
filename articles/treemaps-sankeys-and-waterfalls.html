A budget squeezed into a rectangle, a survey that looks like a brick wall when nothing is going on, a profit bridge and an energy balance. Charts for wholes, their parts and the flows between them.

1. Wholes, Parts and Flows

Some data are about how a whole divides and where things go: how a budget splits into departments and programmes, how survey answers depend on age, how revenue turns into profit, how energy flows from fuels to homes and losses. Part 2 covered the simplest part-to-whole charts, pies and stacked bars. This part covers charts for richer structures: treemaps and sunbursts for hierarchies, mosaic plots for tables of counts, waterfall charts for step-by-step changes, and Sankey diagrams for flows.

All of them use area or width to show size, which makes them vivid but imprecise. They are best for showing structure and the biggest pieces, not for exact comparisons.

3. Treemaps

In 1990 the computer scientist Ben Shneiderman wanted to know which files were filling his laboratory’s hard disk. His answer, published in 1992, was the treemap: a rectangle divided into smaller rectangles whose areas are proportional to size, nested to show the hierarchy of folders and files. [1] The original “slice-and-dice” layout produced long thin slivers; in 2000 Mark Bruls, Kees Huizing and Jarke van Wijk proposed squarified treemaps, which keep rectangles close to square so that their areas are easier to compare. [2]

How to read a treemap
  1. Find the biggest rectangles first: they are the largest items.
  2. Use the borders and colours to see the groups (sectors) and the items inside them.
  3. Do not try to compare areas precisely, especially between rectangles of different shapes; read the labels.
  4. Check whether colour shows a second variable, such as growth or profit, as it often does in financial “market maps”.

The squarify package draws simple treemaps with matplotlib:

# pip install matplotlib squarify
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import squarify

# synthetic city budget (millions)
items = {"Schools": 420, "Hospitals": 310, "Roads": 180, "Police": 150, "Parks": 60,
         "Libraries": 35, "Housing": 140, "Transit": 120, "Admin": 45}
items = dict(sorted(items.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1]))   # largest first gives tidier layouts
colours = ["#0072B2", "#E69F00", "#009E73", "#D55E00", "#CC79A7", "#56B4E9", "#F0E442", "#999999", "#000000"]
labels = [f"{k}\n{v}" for k, v in items.items()]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
squarify.plot(sizes=list(items.values()), label=labels, color=colours, alpha=0.8,
              text_kwargs=dict(color="white", fontsize=9), pad=True, ax=ax)
ax.axis("off")
ax.set_title("City budget (millions): area is proportional to spending")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. Sunburst Charts

A sunburst shows the same hierarchy as a set of rings: the centre is the whole, the first ring the main groups, and each further ring subdivides its parent, with angles proportional to size. John Stasko and Eugene Zhang studied these radial, space-filling displays in 2000. [3] Sunbursts make the levels of a hierarchy easy to follow and look striking, but outer segments are stretched and inner ones squeezed, so sizes are even harder to compare than in a treemap. Interactive versions, which zoom into a segment when you click it, work best. Plotly makes both:

# pip install plotly pandas
import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

df = pd.DataFrame({                                       # synthetic budget with two levels
    "sector": ["Education", "Education", "Health", "Health", "Transport", "Transport", "Other", "Other"],
    "item": ["Schools", "Libraries", "Hospitals", "Clinics", "Roads", "Transit", "Parks", "Admin"],
    "millions": [420, 35, 310, 90, 180, 120, 60, 45],
})

fig = px.sunburst(df, path=["sector", "item"], values="millions")    # inner ring: sector
fig.update_layout(title="Budget as a sunburst (click a sector to zoom)")
fig.show()          # opens in a browser or notebook; px.treemap(...) works the same way

5. Mosaic Plots

A mosaic plot, described by John Hartigan and Beat Kleiner in 1981, [4] shows a table of counts for two or more categorical variables. The width of each column is proportional to the size of a group; each column is then split vertically by the shares of the second variable. Every tile’s area is proportional to its count, and the pattern of the tiles shows whether the variables are related. [5]

# pip install statsmodels pandas matplotlib
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.graphics.mosaicplot import mosaic

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
age = rng.choice(["18–34", "35–54", "55+"], 600, p=[0.35, 0.4, 0.25])
prefs = {"18–34": [0.6, 0.3, 0.1], "35–54": [0.35, 0.45, 0.2], "55+": [0.15, 0.35, 0.5]}
choice = [rng.choice(["Phone", "Laptop", "Paper"], p=prefs[a]) for a in age]   # synthetic survey
df = pd.DataFrame({"age": age, "reads news on": choice})
ages, media = ["18–34", "35–54", "55+"], ["Phone", "Laptop", "Paper"]
counts = df.value_counts()                                # count each (age, medium) pair
data = {(a, m): counts.get((a, m), 0) for a in ages for m in media}   # fixes the order

colours = {"Phone": "#0072B2", "Laptop": "#E69F00", "Paper": "#009E73"}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 5))
mosaic(data, ax=ax, gap=0.02,
       properties=lambda key: {"color": colours[key[1]]},
       labelizer=lambda key: "")                          # widths: age groups; heights: shares
ax.set_title("Mosaic plot: how people read news, by age")
handles = [plt.Rectangle((0, 0), 1, 1, color=c) for c in colours.values()]
ax.legend(handles, colours.keys(), loc="upper right", bbox_to_anchor=(1.25, 1))
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Waterfall Charts

A waterfall chart, or bridge chart, explains how one total becomes another through a series of increases and decreases. Each step is a floating bar that starts where the previous one ended: green for additions, red for subtractions, with the start and end totals drawn from zero. It is a staple of financial reporting and consulting, used to explain changes in profit, cash flow or headcount.

Matplotlib has no waterfall function, but stacking each bar on a computed “bottom” builds one in a few lines:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

steps = [("Revenue", 500), ("Cost of goods", -210), ("Salaries", -120), ("Rent", -40),
         ("Marketing", -35), ("Other income", 15), ("Tax", -30)]       # synthetic, thousands
labels = [s[0] for s in steps] + ["Profit"]
vals = np.array([s[1] for s in steps])
running = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(vals)])          # level before each step
bottoms = np.where(vals >= 0, running[:-1], running[1:])
colours = ["#0072B2"] + ["#009E73" if v > 0 else "#D55E00" for v in vals[1:]]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4.5))
ax.bar(labels[:-1], np.abs(vals), bottom=bottoms, color=colours)
ax.bar("Profit", running[-1], color="#555555")            # final total from zero
for i, (b, v) in enumerate(zip(bottoms, vals)):
    ax.text(i, b + abs(v) + 6, f"{v:+d}" if i else str(v), ha="center", fontsize=9)
    if i < len(vals):                                     # connector lines between bars
        ax.plot([i + 0.4, i + 0.6], [running[i + 1]] * 2, color="gray", lw=0.8)
ax.text(len(vals), running[-1] + 6, str(running[-1]), ha="center", fontsize=9)
ax.set_ylabel("Thousands")
ax.set_title("Waterfall chart: from revenue to profit")
ax.tick_params(axis="x", rotation=25)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Sankey Diagrams

In 1898 the Irish engineer Captain Matthew Sankey published a diagram of the energy flowing through a steam engine, in which arrows split and narrowed in proportion to the heat going to useful work and to the various losses. [6] Sankey diagrams are now used for energy balances, material flows, budgets, website journeys and migration. The rule is simple: the width of each band is proportional to the quantity flowing, and at each intermediate node, inflow equals outflow.

How to read a Sankey diagram
  1. Follow the direction of flow, usually left to right.
  2. Compare band widths to see where most of the quantity goes; check the units.
  3. Look at what comes out of each node: big “loss” or “other” bands are often the story.
  4. Beware of crossing bands and colours that do not match; with many nodes, Sankeys become tangled.
# pip install plotly
import plotly.graph_objects as go

nodes = ["Coal", "Gas", "Renewables", "Nuclear",          # sources (synthetic energy flows)
         "Electricity", "Heating",
         "Homes", "Industry", "Transport", "Losses"]
links = [  # (source index, target index, amount)
    (0, 4, 20), (1, 4, 25), (1, 5, 20), (2, 4, 30), (3, 4, 15),
    (4, 6, 30), (4, 7, 25), (4, 8, 5), (4, 9, 30),
    (5, 6, 14), (5, 7, 4), (5, 9, 2),
]
fig = go.Figure(go.Sankey(
    node=dict(label=nodes, pad=18, thickness=16),
    link=dict(source=[l[0] for l in links], target=[l[1] for l in links], value=[l[2] for l in links]),
))
fig.update_layout(title="Sankey diagram: widths are proportional to energy", font_size=12)
fig.show()

Closely related alluvial diagrams show how individuals move between categories over several time points, such as voters switching parties between elections.

A large hierarchy and its biggest partsTreemapImprecise areas; tiny items unlabelled
The levels of a hierarchySunburst (interactive)Distorted outer rings
A table of counts for two or three categoriesMosaic plotHard to read with many levels
How one total becomes anotherWaterfall chartSteps in an arbitrary order
Flows between stagesSankey diagramTangles with many nodes
A few parts of one wholeBar chart or stacked bar (Part 2)Using a treemap where a bar chart would do

9. Common Misreadings

  • Comparing areas precisely. A treemap rectangle twice as large may not look it, especially if the shapes differ.
  • Reading position as meaning in a treemap. Layout algorithms place rectangles to fit, not in any meaningful order.
  • Judging sunburst segments by their outer length. Outer rings are stretched; compare angles.
  • Missing the column widths in a mosaic. Small groups get narrow columns; their percentages may be unreliable.
  • Forgetting that waterfall steps can be reordered. The order chosen can change which step looks decisive.
  • Assuming a Sankey shows causation. It shows accounting flows, not why they happen.

10. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who drew the first flow diagram?

The legend: Matthew Sankey invented flow diagrams with proportional widths.

The record: Sankey’s 1898 steam-engine diagram gave the diagrams their name, [6] but the idea is older. Charles Joseph Minard used bands of proportional width in the mid-nineteenth century to map the flows of goods and travellers, and in his famous 1869 map of Napoleon’s Russian campaign, the shrinking band shows the army dwindling from more than 400,000 men to about 10,000. [7]

A second record: treemaps were invented for a practical problem, not as an art form. Shneiderman’s laboratory disk was always full, and existing tree diagrams of folders could not show which branches were large. Squeezing the tree into a rectangle used every pixel to show size, an idea now found in disk-usage tools on most operating systems. [1]
