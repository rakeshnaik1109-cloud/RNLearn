Electricity and Circuits: Ohm, Kirchhoff, RC Delay and the Energy of a Bit

Every 1 and 0 in a computer is a voltage on a wire, and every change costs time and energy. A handful of nineteenth-century laws explain why chips run at about a volt and why they get hot.

At a glance

In this part: the physics underneath the 1s and 0s. Charge, current and voltage; why electrons in a wire crawl while signals race; resistance and Ohm’s law; Kirchhoff’s laws and the voltage divider; capacitors and the RC time constant that limits switching speed; and the energy every switching event costs, P ≈ αCV²f.

You need: school physics is helpful but not required. Parts 1–3 explain the logic these circuits implement.

1. From Logic to Electrons

Parts 1–3 treated 0 and 1 as abstract symbols. Inside a chip they are voltages, typically near 0 V and near the supply voltage of about 1 V, and every gate is a set of switches that connects a wire to one or the other. Whether a chip is fast, how hot it gets and how long a phone battery lasts are all decided by ordinary circuit physics: resistance, capacitance and energy. This part is a compact refresher of the ideas needed for the rest of the series; the next parts dig into the materials that make the switches. [1, 2]

3. Charge, Current and Voltage

In 1800 Alessandro Volta stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by brine-soaked card and obtained, for the first time, a steady flow of electricity. [3] What flows was identified in 1897, when J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays consist of particles about two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom: electrons, each carrying charge −e = −1.602 × 10−19 coulombs. [4]

  • Current I is the rate at which charge flows past a point: 1 ampere = 1 coulomb per second, about 6 × 1018 electrons per second.
  • Voltage V is the energy per unit charge between two points: 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb. A battery or power supply is an energy pump that raises charge to a higher voltage.
  • Power is energy per second: P = VI. A 1 V chip drawing 100 A uses 100 W.

A surprise: the electrons themselves move very slowly. In 1900 Paul Drude pictured a metal as a gas of free electrons bouncing off the ions of the crystal; an applied voltage adds only a small average drift to their furious random motion. [5] The current I = nqAvd, where n is the number of free electrons per cubic metre, so in copper (n ≈ 8.5 × 1028 m−3) an ampere in a 1 mm wire drifts at about a tenth of a millimetre per second. [6]

4. Resistance and Ohm’s Law

In 1827 Georg Simon Ohm published that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it: V = IR, where R is the resistance in ohms (Ω). [7] Resistance comes from collisions: the more often electrons scatter, the more energy they hand to the crystal as heat, at a rate P = I²R = V²/R.

For a wire, R = ρL/A: longer wires resist more, fatter wires less, and the material sets the resistivity ρ (copper: 1.7 × 10−8 Ω m). Its inverse, the conductivity, is σ = nqμ, where μ is the mobility, the drift speed per unit electric field. This formula is the key to Part 5: metals conduct because n is enormous; insulators do not because n is essentially zero; and semiconductors are interesting because n can be changed, by heat, light or a small amount of impurity, over many orders of magnitude.

5. Kirchhoff’s Laws and the Voltage Divider

In 1845 Gustav Kirchhoff, then a 21-year-old student, stated the two rules that solve any network of resistors: [8]

  • Current law: at any junction, the total current flowing in equals the total flowing out (charge is conserved).
  • Voltage law: around any closed loop, the voltage rises and drops sum to zero (energy is conserved).

From them follow the series rule (R = R₁ + R₂), the parallel rule (1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂) and the voltage divider: two resistors in series across a supply give an output Vout = V × R₂/(R₁ + R₂). The divider explains much of digital electronics. A logic gate’s output is a divider between a switch to the supply and a switch to ground: when one is on (low resistance) and the other off (very high resistance), the output sits firmly at 1 or 0.

6. Capacitors and the RC Time Constant

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator; the first, the Leyden jar, was made in 1745. It stores charge Q = CV, where the capacitance C is measured in farads. For parallel plates, C = εA/d: large area, thin insulator and high permittivity ε give large capacitance, a formula that returns in Part 8, because the gate of a transistor is a capacitor. [6]

A capacitor cannot change its voltage instantly; charge must flow in through whatever resistance is in the way. Charging through R from a step voltage gives V(t) = VDD(1 − e−t/RC), with time constant τ = RC: 63% of the way after τ, 95% after 3τ, and the 50% switching point after 0.69τ.

On a chip, every gate input and every wire is a small capacitor (femtofarads, 10−15 F), and every transistor that drives it has a resistance of kilohms when on. Their product, picoseconds, sets the delay of every gate. [9]

Watch out: wires are getting worse

Shrinking transistors makes them faster, but shrinking wires makes them slower: halving a wire’s width and height quadruples its resistance per unit length, while its capacitance per unit length stays roughly the same. In 1995 Mark Bohr of Intel warned that interconnect, not transistors, would become the real limit on chip speed, which drove the switch from aluminium to copper wiring and to insulators with lower permittivity. [10]

7. The Energy Cost of Switching

Charging a capacitor C to voltage V draws charge Q = CV from the supply at voltage V, so the supply delivers energy QV = CV². Only half, ½CV², ends up stored in the capacitor; the other half is turned into heat in the resistance on the way, and remarkably this is true whatever the resistance. When the node is discharged to 0, the stored half is turned into heat as well.

So each 0 → 1 → 0 cycle of a node costs CV². A chip with total switched capacitance C, clock frequency f and a fraction α of nodes switching each cycle dissipates the dynamic power

P ≈ α C V² f

This single formula explains why chips run at about 1 V rather than the 5 V of the 1980s, why clock speeds stopped rising in the mid-2000s (Part 10), and why phones slow down their processors to save battery. [9]

8. What We Need from a Switch

Putting the pieces together, an ideal switch for logic would have zero resistance when on (to charge capacitances quickly), infinite resistance when off (so that no current flows when it is idle), and would be controlled by a voltage without itself drawing current, so that one gate’s output can drive many others. It should also be tiny, cheap and able to switch billions of times per second for years.

Relays and vacuum tubes approximated this, but were large, slow or power-hungry. The device that comes closest, the field-effect transistor, turns a semiconductor from insulator to conductor with an electric field. To see how, we need to know why some materials conduct and others do not, which is the subject of Part 5.

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): an LED resistor

A light-emitting diode needs 10 mA and drops 2.0 V when lit. What series resistor is needed to run it from a 5 V supply, and how much power does the resistor dissipate?

Solution

By Kirchhoff’s voltage law, the resistor takes the remaining 5 − 2 = 3 V. By Ohm’s law, R = 3 V / 0.010 A = 300 Ω. Its power is P = VI = 3 × 0.010 = 30 mW, well within a standard 250 mW resistor. (The LED itself uses 2 × 0.010 = 20 mW.)

Problem 2 (easy): a loaded divider

Two 10 kΩ resistors divide a 5 V supply. What is the output voltage? What does it become when a 10 kΩ load is connected across the lower resistor?

Solution

Unloaded: Vout = 5 × 10/(10 + 10) = 2.5 V. With the load, the lower half is 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ, so Vout = 5 × 5/(10 + 5) = 1.67 V. Loading changed the output by a third. To keep this small, the load should be much larger than the divider resistors, which is one reason transistor gates, which draw almost no current, are so useful.

Problem 3 (medium): drift velocity

A copper wire with cross-section 1 mm² carries 1 A. Copper has 8.5 × 1028 free electrons per m³. Find the drift velocity, and the time an electron takes to travel 1 m.

Solution

vd = I/(nqA) = 1 / (8.5 × 1028 × 1.602 × 10−19 × 10−6) ≈ 7.3 × 10−5 m/s, about 0.07 mm/s. Travelling 1 m takes 1/7.3 × 10−5 ≈ 1.4 × 104 s, about 4 hours. The signal, by contrast, arrives in nanoseconds.

Problem 4 (medium): gate delay as RC

A transistor with on-resistance 2 kΩ drives a wire and gate inputs totalling 5 fF. Estimate the time for the output to rise from 0 to 50% of the supply. What happens if the load capacitance doubles?

Solution

τ = RC = 2 × 103 × 5 × 10−15 = 10−11 s = 10 ps. Setting 1 − e−t/τ = 0.5 gives t = τ ln 2 ≈ 6.9 ps. Doubling C doubles τ and the delay, to about 14 ps. This is why a gate that drives many others (high “fan-out”) is slow, and why designers insert buffers on long wires.

Problem 5 (harder): the energy lost in charging

(a) Show that charging a capacitor C from 0 to V through a resistor R dissipates ½CV² in the resistor, independent of R. (b) A processor switches an effective capacitance of 100 nF with activity α = 0.1 at 3 GHz and 1.0 V. Estimate its dynamic power, and the saving at 0.8 V.

Solution

(a) The current is i(t) = (V/R)e−t/RC. The heat is ∫ i²R dt = (V²/R) ∫0∞ e−2t/RC dt = (V²/R)(RC/2) = ½CV². R cancels: a smaller resistance gives a larger current for a shorter time. The supply delivered QV = CV², so the other ½CV² is stored.

(b) P = αCV²f = 0.1 × 10−7 × 1² × 3 × 109 = 30 W. At 0.8 V, P = 30 × 0.64 = 19.2 W, a 36% saving from a 20% voltage reduction. (Transistors switch more slowly at lower voltage, so in practice the frequency must often be reduced too, saving even more.)

10. Open Questions

Open questions: is there a minimum energy per operation?

Today’s logic gates use thousands of times more energy per switching event than Landauer’s limit of kT ln 2 for erasing a bit (Part 1). Part of the gap is the CV² cost of charging wires, and part is the need for a supply voltage well above the thermal voltage kT/q ≈ 26 mV so that transistors switch cleanly (Part 8). Researchers are exploring adiabatic circuits, which charge capacitances slowly through ramped supplies to recover most of the energy, and reversible logic, which avoids erasing information. Whether such approaches can become practical at large scale, given the cost in speed and circuit size, is still unsettled.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “electricity is electrons rushing through wires at the speed of light”

The myth: when you flip a switch, electrons race from the switch to the lamp at nearly the speed of light.

The reality: the electrons drift at fractions of a millimetre per second, and in alternating current they merely jiggle back and forth. What travels fast is the electromagnetic field, guided by the wires, which sets all the electrons along the circuit moving almost at once. The energy is carried by that field.

Why it matters: signal speed on a chip depends on how quickly wire capacitances can be charged, not on how fast electrons move.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Current is charge per second, voltage is energy per charge, and power is P = VI.
  • Ohm’s law V = IR; for a wire R = ρL/A, and conductivity σ = nqμ depends on the number of free carriers.
  • Kirchhoff’s laws (current in = current out; voltages round a loop sum to zero) solve any resistor network.
  • Capacitors charge with time constant τ = RC; on chips, RC is picoseconds and sets gate delay.
  • Each switching cycle costs CV², so dynamic power is P ≈ αCV²f, and lowering V saves the most.
  • An ideal logic switch is voltage-controlled, with near-zero on-resistance and near-infinite off-resistance.
