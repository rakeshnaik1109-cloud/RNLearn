Learning from Examples: Backpropagation, Big Data and Deep Blue

Instead of telling machines what to do, researchers taught them to learn from examples. Over twenty quiet years, learning machines began reading cheques, filtering spam and translating text, while a chess computer grabbed the headlines.

1. Learning Instead of Telling

Part 3 ended with expert systems in trouble. Writing down an expert's knowledge as thousands of rules was slow and expensive, and the rules broke as soon as the world didn't fit them. This part tells how AI found a different way: instead of telling machines what to do, let them learn it from examples. [1, 2]

Between the 1980s and about 2010, three things changed together:

  • New learning methods. Researchers found how to train neural networks with hidden layers (backpropagation), and developed powerful statistical tools that learned from data.
  • Much more data. As the world went digital, and then online, examples to learn from piled up: scanned cheques, recorded speech, translated documents, emails, clicks.
  • Much faster computers. Each year computers grew faster and cheaper, making experiments possible that would have taken years before.

This period has no single dramatic moment. Deep Blue's 1997 win at chess is the famous headline, and, ironically, it hardly learned at all. But by 2010, quietly, machine learning was reading cheques, filtering spam, recognising speech, translating text and recommending films. The stage was set for the explosion of Part 5.

3. How Do You Train a Network? Backpropagation

Part 3 ended with a puzzle. Networks with a hidden layer of neurons could, in principle, learn patterns like XOR, but nobody knew how to train them. If the network gives a wrong answer, which of its many weights is to blame, and by how much? [3]

The answer is a method called backpropagation ("backward propagation of errors"). To understand it, start with the idea of gradient descent. Imagine you are on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom of the valley. You can't see far, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, and take another step. Eventually you reach the bottom.

For a neural network, the "height" is the error: how wrong its answers are over all the training examples. Each weight is a direction you can move in. Backpropagation is a clever and efficient way of working out the slope for every weight at once. It starts with the error at the output and passes it backwards through the network, layer by layer, using the chain rule from calculus to share out the blame. Then every weight is nudged a little in the direction that reduces the error. Repeat this thousands of times and the network learns.

The key mathematics was found more than once. Seppo Linnainmaa described the general technique in 1970, and Paul Werbos proposed using it for neural networks in his 1974 PhD thesis. But it was a 1986 paper in Nature by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams that convinced the world, by showing what it could do. Networks trained this way learned to solve problems that had defeated the perceptron, and, most strikingly, their hidden neurons invented useful features of their own (section 13). [3, 4, 1]

Neural networks became fashionable again. A few years earlier, in 1982, the physicist John Hopfield had shown how a network of neurons could store and recall memories, borrowing ideas from the physics of magnets, and this too drew new people into the field. In 2024 Hopfield and Hinton shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for these foundations of machine learning. [5]

4. Teaching Machines to Read

The first big practical success came from reading handwriting. At Bell Labs in New Jersey, a young French researcher, Yann LeCun, trained a neural network to read the handwritten zip codes (postcodes) on US mail. His 1989 network did something new. Instead of connecting every pixel to every neuron, it used small filters, each looking at a tiny patch of the image, and slid the same filter across the whole picture. [6]

This design is called a convolutional neural network. It has two big advantages. A pattern such as a vertical stroke is recognised wherever it appears in the image, and the network needs far fewer weights to learn, because one small filter is reused everywhere. The first layer learns to spot simple things like edges; later layers combine them into curves, loops and corners, and finally into whole digits.

By 1998 LeCun and his colleagues had refined the design into LeNet-5, and a cheque-reading system built on this work was used by banks. LeCun has estimated that by the late 1990s it was reading 10 to 20 per cent of all cheques written in the United States. His team also released MNIST, a collection of 70,000 handwritten digits that became the standard test for new learning methods for the next twenty years. [7]

5. The Statistical Turn

While neural networks were reading digits, a quieter revolution was happening in speech and language. [2, 8]

At IBM, a team led by the Czech-born engineer Frederick Jelinek worked on speech recognition from the 1970s onwards. Instead of writing rules of grammar and pronunciation, they treated speech as a problem of probabilities: given these sounds, which sequence of words is most likely? They estimated the probabilities from large amounts of recorded and transcribed speech, using a mathematical tool called a hidden Markov model. It worked better than the rule-based systems, and Jelinek is reported to have joked: "Every time I fire a linguist, the performance of the speech recogniser goes up."

Around 1990 the same IBM group applied the idea to translation. Their system learned to translate between French and English by studying millions of sentences from the records of the Canadian parliament, which are published in both languages. It learned which words and phrases tend to correspond, and how word order changes, without being told any grammar at all. This statistical machine translation became the basis of online translation services in the 2000s. [8]

The lesson was startling to many AI researchers: with enough data, crude statistical models could beat carefully hand-crafted knowledge.

6. Reasoning with Probabilities: Spam Filters

Probability also solved an old problem of expert systems: real life is uncertain. A symptom makes a disease more likely but not certain; a word makes an email more likely to be spam but not certainly so. In 1988 the computer scientist Judea Pearl published Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems, showing how machines could reason with uncertainty using Bayesian networks, built on a rule for updating beliefs in the light of evidence that goes back to the 18th-century minister Thomas Bayes. Pearl later won the Turing Award, computing's highest honour. [9]

A simple version of this idea protected millions of inboxes. By the late 1990s junk email was a plague. Researchers proposed filters that learn, and in 2002 an essay by the programmer Paul Graham, "A Plan for Spam", popularised them widely. The filter counts how often each word appears in spam and in normal email. To judge a new message, it combines the evidence from every word: "prize" and "click" push towards spam, "meeting" and "lecture" push away from it. As spammers changed their tricks, the filter simply learned from new examples. [10, 11]

7. Learning by Playing: TD-Gammon

Reinforcement learning, learning by reward and punishment like MENACE in Part 2, also made a leap. In 1988 Richard Sutton published a method called temporal-difference learning, which improves predictions by making them agree with slightly later, better-informed predictions, as Samuel's draughts program had done. In 1989 Chris Watkins described Q-learning, a way for an agent to learn the value of each action in each situation purely from experience. [12, 13]

At IBM, Gerald Tesauro combined temporal-difference learning with a neural network to play backgammon. TD-Gammon (1992 onwards) started knowing only the rules and learned by playing hundreds of thousands of games against itself. It reached a level close to the world's best human players, and some of its unusual opening moves were adopted by human experts. A program had discovered knowledge about a game that people had played for centuries. [14]

8. Deep Blue Beats the World Champion

The most famous AI event of the decade was a chess match. IBM's Deep Blue grew out of a project begun by students at Carnegie Mellon University, led by Feng-hsiung Hsu. In February 1996, in Philadelphia, it played world champion Garry Kasparov, widely considered one of the greatest players ever. Deep Blue won the first game, the first time a computer had beaten a reigning world champion in a game played at normal tournament speed. But Kasparov won the match 4–2. [15]

An upgraded Deep Blue played a rematch in New York in May 1997. This time, after six tense games, it won 3½–2½. Newspapers around the world announced that a machine had beaten humanity's best at the ultimate game of intellect.

But Deep Blue barely learned at all. It used Shannon's 1950 recipe (Part 2): look ahead and score positions. What made it strong was speed: hundreds of custom chess chips let it examine about 200 million positions per second. Its scoring rule weighed thousands of features of a position, and the weights were tuned using grandmasters' advice and databases of master games. It was a triumph of engineering and search, and many people concluded, wrongly as it turned out, that this showed little about intelligence. After the match IBM retired the machine. [15, 16]

9. The Quiet Workhorses

For most practical problems in the late 1990s and 2000s, neural networks weren't the favourite tool. Other learning methods were more reliable and easier to use. [2]

  • Decision trees, developed by Ross Quinlan and others from the 1980s, learn a flowchart of yes/no questions ("Is income above £30,000? Has the customer missed a payment?") that leads to an answer. They are easy for people to read. [17]
  • Support vector machines, published by Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik in 1995, find the dividing line (or surface) that leaves the widest possible gap between two classes. A mathematical trick, the "kernel trick", lets them draw curved boundaries as easily as straight ones. They came with solid mathematical guarantees and often gave the best results of their day. [18]
  • Random forests, introduced by Leo Breiman in 2001, grow hundreds of decision trees, each on a slightly different random sample of the data, and let them vote. The crowd is far more accurate than any single tree. [19]

Neural networks, meanwhile, had a problem. Adding more layers should have made them more powerful, but deep networks were very hard to train. As the error signal was passed backwards through many layers, it tended to shrink until it almost vanished, so the early layers barely learned. Sepp Hochreiter analysed this vanishing gradient problem in 1991. In 1997 he and Jürgen Schmidhuber designed a network for sequences, the LSTM ("long short-term memory"), with special memory cells that let information and error signals flow over long distances. It would later power speech recognition and translation in phones. But for most of the 2000s, many researchers regarded neural networks as an old-fashioned dead end. [20]

10. Training, Testing and Overfitting

Learning from examples brought a new danger. A model can do brilliantly on the examples it was trained on and badly on new ones, because instead of learning the real pattern, it has memorised the quirks and noise of its training data. This is called overfitting. It is like a student who memorises the answers to last year's exam questions without understanding the subject. [21]

The defence is simple but essential: always keep some data back. A model is trained on a training set and judged on a separate test set it has never seen. Only the test score tells you how well it will do in the real world.

This discipline, together with shared benchmark datasets like MNIST, turned machine learning into an experimental science. Researchers could compare methods fairly by testing them on the same unseen data, and progress could be measured year by year.

11. More Data Beats Cleverer Algorithms

The rise of the internet in the late 1990s and 2000s created data on a scale never seen before: billions of web pages, emails, searches, photos and purchases. Researchers began noticing something surprising. [22, 23]

In 2001, Michele Banko and Eric Brill at Microsoft tested several learning methods on a language task as they gave them more and more training data. The amount of data mattered more than which method they used: a simple method with a billion words of training text beat a sophisticated one with a million. In 2009 three Google researchers, Alon Halevy, Peter Norvig and Fernando Pereira, summed up the lesson in an essay titled "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data".

Two contests showed the power of learning from data. In 2005 a robot car called Stanley, built by Sebastian Thrun's team at Stanford, won the DARPA Grand Challenge by driving itself 212 km across the Mojave Desert. It used machine learning to tell drivable ground from obstacles. A year earlier, no entrant had managed even 12 km. And from 2006 to 2009, Netflix offered a $1 million prize to anyone who could improve its film recommendations by 10%. The winning team, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, did it by blending hundreds of different learned models. [24, 25]

12. Keeping the Flame Alive

Through the 2000s, a small group of researchers kept faith with neural networks: Geoffrey Hinton in Toronto, Yann LeCun in New York, Yoshua Bengio in Montreal and Jürgen Schmidhuber in Switzerland, among others. A Canadian research organisation, CIFAR, funded a programme from 2004 that let Hinton, LeCun and Bengio meet and share ideas. They sometimes joked that they were a "conspiracy". [27]

In 2006 Hinton and his colleagues showed a way to train networks with many layers: first train each layer on its own to capture patterns in the data, one layer at a time, then fine-tune the whole network with backpropagation. It worked, and to signal a fresh start they started calling the field deep learning. [26, 27]

Two more ingredients were falling into place. Graphics chips (GPUs), designed for video games, turned out to be very good at the kind of arithmetic neural networks need, and researchers began using them to train networks many times faster. And in 2009 a team led by Fei-Fei Li at Princeton released ImageNet: more than three million labelled photos at first, growing to over fourteen million, sorted into thousands of categories by people working through an online crowdsourcing service. [28]

Deep networks, fast GPUs and a huge labelled dataset: in 2012 these three came together in a contest, and the result changed the course of AI. That is the story of Part 5.

13. How Did They Know? A Network That Invented Its Own Ideas

How did they know? The family-tree experiment

Backpropagation could fit data, but did the hidden neurons learn anything meaningful, or just an arbitrary jumble of numbers? Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams designed an experiment to find out. [3]

  1. Make up a task with hidden structure. They drew two family trees with the same shape, one of 12 English people and one of 12 Italian people, and turned them into 104 facts like "Colin's father is James" and "Sophia's mother is Lucia".
  2. Give the network no hints. Each person and each relationship was fed in as a separate on/off input. Nothing told the network that some people were English and some Italian, or which generation anyone belonged to.
  3. Train it on 100 of the facts with backpropagation, so that given a person and a relationship it would output the right relative.
  4. Hold back the other 4 as a test. The network answered all or most of these unseen facts correctly, so it had learned something of the structure, not just memorised.
  5. Look inside. The researchers examined what each hidden neuron responded to.

They found hidden neurons that had come to stand for things nobody had told the network about: one picked up whether a person was English or Italian, another which generation they were in, another which branch of the family they were on. The network had invented useful concepts for itself, in order to do its job. This ability, learning its own internal representations from raw examples, is exactly what makes deep learning powerful today.

14. Legend and Record: Did a Bug Beat Kasparov?

Legend and Record: Deep Blue's mysterious moves

The legends: two stories grew up around the 1997 match. One says IBM cheated, with a human grandmaster secretly helping the computer. The other says Deep Blue won because of a software bug that rattled Kasparov. [16, 29, 30]

The record on cheating: in game 2, Deep Blue played a subtle, patient move of a kind Kasparov didn't expect from a computer, and Kasparov later resigned in a position that analysts found he could have drawn. Shaken, he publicly suggested that humans might have intervened, and asked to see the machine's logs; IBM declined a rematch and dismantled the machine, which fed the suspicion. No evidence of cheating has ever emerged. Twenty years later, in his book Deep Thinking (2017), Kasparov wrote that he no longer believed there had been any.

The record on the bug: according to Deep Blue's team member Murray Campbell, as reported by the statistician Nate Silver in 2012, a bug at the end of game 1 made the computer play an essentially random move. Silver suggested Kasparov may have read deep meaning into it and overestimated the machine. Kasparov won that game anyway, and his own account plays down its importance. The bug is real, but how much it affected the match is a matter of interpretation.

Why it matters: as with the Mechanical Turk (Part 1), people struggled to judge what a machine was really doing. Kasparov thought a clever move must have come from a human; others thought a random move might be genius. Our instincts about what machines can do are unreliable, in both directions.

15. Philosophy Corner: Can We Trust What We Can't Explain?

Philosophy Corner: the black box and the question of cause

Expert systems had one great virtue: you could ask them "why?" and they would show you their rules (Part 3). Learned models gave that up. A trained neural network's knowledge is spread across thousands or millions of numbers, and nobody can read off why it made a particular decision. It is a black box. [9, 31]

Does that matter? If a model reads cheques more accurately than people, perhaps we needn't know how. But when a model decides who gets a loan, a job interview or medical treatment, people have a right to an explanation. And a model that learns from data can quietly absorb the unfairness in that data. If past hiring favoured one group, a model trained on it will learn to do the same.

Judea Pearl raised a deeper point. Learning from data finds correlations: things that tend to occur together. But correlation is not causation. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together, because both increase in hot weather, not because ice cream causes drowning. Pearl argued that real understanding means knowing about causes, being able to answer "what would happen if I did this?", and that learning patterns from data, however much, cannot by itself give you that.

Is a system that predicts brilliantly but cannot explain itself, and does not grasp causes, truly intelligent? Or is that also true of much of our own intuition? These questions become sharper with each part of this series.
