Memory and Time: Latches, Flip-Flops, Counters and State Machines

A circuit that only reacts to its present inputs cannot count, store or follow a sequence. One wire looped back on itself changes everything, and a ticking clock keeps billions of such loops in step.

At a glance

In this part: sequential circuits, which remember. Feedback turns two gates into a latch; a clock edge turns latches into flip-flops; flip-flops build registers, counters and state machines; and arrays of tiny cells become SRAM and DRAM. We finish with timing: how fast a clocked circuit can run, and the strange problem of metastability.

You need: Parts 1 and 2 (gates and combinational circuits).

Try: five interactive figures, including a working latch, a counter and a traffic-light controller, and five worked problems.

1. Circuits That Remember

The circuits of Part 2 forget everything the moment their inputs change. A calculator that could not remember the first number while you typed the second would be useless. Circuits whose outputs depend on their past as well as their present inputs are called sequential. The single idea that gives them memory is feedback: an output wired back to an input. Add a clock to keep everything in step and you have the framework of every digital system, from a wristwatch to a supercomputer. [1, 2]

3. Latches: Feedback Makes Memory

In 1918 the British physicists William Eccles and Frank Jordan connected two vacuum tubes so that each held the other in its state: one on, one off, until a pulse flipped them. Their “trigger relay”, published in 1919, was the first electronic flip-flop. [3] The same idea works with gates. Take two NOR gates and feed each output into one input of the other. This SR latch has two inputs:

  • S (set) = 1 forces Q = 1.
  • R (reset) = 1 forces Q = 0.
  • S = R = 0: the loop holds whichever value it had. This is memory.
  • S = R = 1 is forbidden: both outputs go to 0, and if both inputs are released together the latch falls into an unpredictable state.

The D latch removes the forbidden case. It has a data input D and an enable input E, and gates them so that S = E·D and R = E·D̅. While E = 1 the latch is transparent and Q follows D; when E returns to 0, Q holds the last value.

4. Flip-Flops and the Clock

Transparency is a problem when circuits feed each other: while the enable is high, a change can race through one latch, through the logic and into the next latch, many times over. The fix is the edge-triggered D flip-flop, usually built from two latches in series (a master and a slave) enabled on opposite halves of the clock. The result copies D to Q only at the instant the clock rises, and holds it for the whole cycle.

A clock is a square wave that ticks billions of times per second in a modern processor. In a synchronous design every flip-flop samples on the same edge, so the whole system moves forward one step per tick. Between ticks, combinational logic has one clock period to compute the next values. This discipline, simple as it is, is what makes chips with billions of transistors possible to design and test.

5. Registers and Counters

Put n flip-flops side by side with a shared clock and you have an n-bit register, which stores a number. A processor’s registers hold the values it is working on right now. Connect each flip-flop’s output to the next one’s input and you have a shift register, which moves bits along one place per clock: the basis of serial links such as USB.

Feed a register’s output through an adder that adds 1 and back into its input, and you have a counter. Its lowest bit toggles every cycle, the next every two cycles, and so on: each bit divides the clock frequency by 2. Counters time events, generate addresses and, in the form of a processor’s program counter, step through the instructions of every program.

6. Memory Arrays: From Cores to Chips

Registers built from flip-flops are fast but large. Early computers needed something denser. In 1951 Jay Forrester at MIT described storing each bit in the magnetisation of a tiny ferrite ring, threaded on a grid of wires; magnetic-core memory dominated computing for twenty years. [4]

Semiconductor memory replaced it. Static RAM (SRAM) stores each bit in a pair of cross-coupled inverters, essentially a latch, using six transistors: fast, and it keeps data as long as power is on. In 1966 Robert Dennard at IBM conceived dynamic RAM (DRAM), which stores a bit as charge on a single capacitor reached through one transistor; he patented it in 1968. [5] Intel’s 1103 chip of 1970 was the first DRAM to sell widely and started the end of core memory. [6]

DRAM’s single-transistor cell makes it dense and cheap, but the charge leaks away within milliseconds, so every row must be read and rewritten, or refreshed, regularly; a typical standard requires every cell to be refreshed within 64 milliseconds. [7] That is why computers use a little fast SRAM (for caches) and a lot of dense DRAM (for main memory), a hierarchy we return to in Part 12. How the cells are built from transistors appears in Part 9.

7. Finite-State Machines

A finite-state machine (FSM) is the general recipe for a sequential circuit: a register holds the current state, and combinational logic computes the next state from the current state and the inputs. With k flip-flops it can have up to 2k states. In 1955 George Mealy described machines whose outputs depend on both state and inputs; in 1956 Edward Moore analysed machines whose outputs depend only on the state. [8, 9] Both styles are used everywhere, from vending machines and lift controllers to the control unit that drives a processor and the protocol logic of a network card.

Designing an FSM follows a fixed path: draw the state diagram, choose binary codes for the states, write the next-state and output truth tables, simplify them (Part 2), and build them with flip-flops and gates.

8. Timing: Setup, Hold and Metastability

A flip-flop needs its input to be steady for a short setup time before the clock edge and a hold time after it. Its output appears a clock-to-Q delay after the edge. So in a synchronous circuit, a signal leaving one flip-flop must pass through the logic and arrive at the next flip-flop before the next edge, giving the central timing rule:

Tclock ≥ tclk→Q + tlogic,max + tsetup

The slowest such path, the critical path, sets the maximum clock frequency. A second rule protects hold time: the fastest path must not be so quick that new data overwrites the old before the receiving flip-flop has captured it.

When an input arrives from outside the clock domain, such as a button press or data from another chip, nothing guarantees these rules. If D changes right at the clock edge, the flip-flop can balance between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time, like a ball on a hilltop: this is metastability. Thomas Chaney and Charles Molnar demonstrated it experimentally in 1973, overturning a widespread belief that a well-designed flip-flop always decides promptly. [10] The chance of still being undecided falls exponentially with waiting time, so designers pass asynchronous signals through two or more flip-flops in a row, a synchroniser. [11]

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): trace an SR latch

A NOR SR latch starts with Q = 0. The inputs (S, R) take the values (1, 0), (0, 0), (0, 1), (0, 0), (1, 0) in turn. What is Q after each step?

Solution

(1, 0) sets: Q = 1. (0, 0) holds: Q = 1. (0, 1) resets: Q = 0. (0, 0) holds: Q = 0. (1, 0) sets: Q = 1. So Q goes 1, 1, 0, 0, 1. The “hold” steps are where memory shows: the same inputs (0, 0) give different outputs depending on history.

Problem 2 (easy): a watch from a crystal

A quartz watch crystal oscillates at 32,768 Hz. How many divide-by-2 flip-flop stages are needed to produce one pulse per second? How many flip-flops does a counter that counts 0–59 seconds need?

Solution

32,768 = 215, so 15 stages give exactly 1 Hz; that is why watch crystals use this odd-looking frequency. For 60 states, we need 2k ≥ 60, so k = 6 flip-flops (26 = 64), with logic that resets the count to 0 after 59.

Problem 3 (medium): maximum clock frequency

Flip-flops have clock-to-Q delay 50 ps, setup time 30 ps and hold time 20 ps. The slowest logic path between two flip-flops takes 400 ps and the fastest takes 10 ps. What is the maximum clock frequency? Is the hold time met?

Solution

T ≥ 50 + 400 + 30 = 480 ps, so fmax = 1/480 ps ≈ 2.08 GHz. For hold, the earliest new data arrives tclk→Q + tlogic,min = 50 + 10 = 60 ps after the edge, which is later than the 20 ps hold time, so hold is met (with no clock skew). Note that hold violations cannot be fixed by slowing the clock, which is why they are feared.

Problem 4 (medium): design a “101” detector

Design a Moore machine that reads one bit per clock and outputs 1 whenever the last three bits were 1, 0, 1 (overlaps allowed). How many states and flip-flops are needed?

Solution

States record how much of the pattern has been seen: S0 (nothing useful), S1 (seen “1”), S2 (seen “10”), S3 (seen “101”, output 1). Transitions (input 0 / input 1): S0 → S0 / S1; S1 → S2 / S1; S2 → S0 / S3; S3 → S2 / S1. The last line handles overlap: after “101”, a 0 means “10” has been seen again. Four states, two flip-flops. The input 1 0 1 0 1 gives outputs 0 0 1 0 1.

Problem 5 (harder): synchroniser reliability

The mean time between failures of a synchroniser is MTBF = et/τ / (Tw fclk fdata). Take τ = 20 ps, Tw = 20 ps, fclk = 1 GHz and fdata = 100 MHz. Compare the MTBF if the flip-flop has t = 0.5 ns or t = 1 ns to settle.

Solution

The denominator is 20 × 10−12 × 109 × 108 = 2 × 106 s−1. For t = 0.5 ns, t/τ = 25 and e25 ≈ 7.2 × 1010, so MTBF ≈ 3.6 × 104 s, about 10 hours: unacceptable. For t = 1 ns, t/τ = 50 and e50 ≈ 5.2 × 1021, so MTBF ≈ 2.6 × 1015 s, about 80 million years. Doubling the waiting time squared the safety factor.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: must every chip march to one clock?

Distributing one clock across a large chip is hard: the wires that carry it consume a large share of the power, and the edge arrives at slightly different times in different places (skew). Modern chips therefore split into many clock domains joined by synchronisers, and some designs drop the global clock altogether in favour of asynchronous circuits that signal when each step is done. Asynchronous logic promises lower power and robustness to variation but is harder to design and verify, and how far it should replace clocked design remains debated. Arbiters, which must decide which of two near-simultaneous requests came first, can never be made free of metastability, only made extremely unlikely to fail. [11]

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a higher clock speed always means a faster computer”

The myth: a 4 GHz processor is always faster than a 3 GHz one.

The reality: the clock sets only how often the flip-flops update. How much useful work is done per tick depends on the design: how many instructions are started per cycle, how often the processor waits for memory, and how many cores share the work. A design can raise its clock by splitting work into more, shorter steps, yet do less per tick. Clock frequencies have barely risen since the mid-2000s, while performance has kept growing through other means (Parts 11–13).

Why it matters: performance is frequency × work per cycle × number of cores, and the last two now matter most.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Feedback gives circuits memory: two cross-coupled gates form a latch.
  • Edge-triggered flip-flops sample their input only at clock edges, so synchronous systems move one step per tick.
  • Registers store numbers, shift registers move bits, and counters divide frequencies by powers of 2.
  • SRAM stores bits in latches (fast); DRAM stores them as leaking charge on capacitors (dense, needs refresh).
  • A finite-state machine is a state register plus next-state logic; k flip-flops give up to 2k states.
  • Tclock ≥ tclk→Q + tlogic + tsetup; asynchronous inputs need synchronisers because of metastability.
