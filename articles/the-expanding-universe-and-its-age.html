The Expanding Universe and Its Age: Redshifts, the Big Bang and the Hubble Tension

The galaxies are rushing apart. Run the film backwards and they meet. Measuring when took a century, from an age younger than the Earth to 13.8 billion years, and the best measurements still disagree.

1. Has the Universe Always Existed?

In 1925, as Part 9 described, Hubble showed that the spiral nebulae are galaxies far beyond our own. Most astronomers still assumed that the universe as a whole was eternal and unchanging: galaxies might move about, but the cosmos itself had no history. Within a few years that assumption collapsed. The galaxies turned out to be rushing apart, the universe to be expanding, and running the expansion backwards suggested a beginning.

Measuring when that beginning happened has been one of the great projects of modern science. The first answer, around 1930, was about two billion years, which was embarrassingly younger than the Earth (Part 7). Today the answer is 13.8 billion years, known to better than one percent. But the two best ways of measuring the expansion rate now disagree, and nobody yet knows why.

3. Slipher’s Speeding Nebulae

The story starts before anyone knew what the spiral nebulae were. At the Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, Vesto Slipher set out to photograph their spectra: the light spread into its colours, crossed by dark lines where atoms absorb particular wavelengths. Spiral nebulae are so faint that a single spectrum needed exposures lasting many nights.

In 1912 Slipher found that the lines in the spectrum of the Andromeda nebula were shifted towards the blue, meaning that it is approaching us at about 300 km/s: faster than any star then known. As he measured more spirals, a pattern emerged. By 1917 he had velocities for 25, and 21 of them were moving away from us, some at more than 1,000 km/s. [1] Their spectral lines were shifted towards the red: a redshift.

Why should nebulae flee from us? Slipher wondered whether our Galaxy was moving through space, but the speeds were too large and too one-sided for that alone.

4. Einstein’s Static Universe

Meanwhile, theory was moving too. In 1917 Albert Einstein applied his new general theory of relativity to the universe as a whole. He found that a universe filled with matter could not stand still: gravity would make it collapse. Like nearly everyone, he believed the universe was static, so he added an extra term to his equations, the cosmological constant, a kind of repulsion that balanced gravity. [2]

In 1922 the Russian mathematician Alexander Friedmann showed that Einstein’s equations also allow universes whose size changes with time, expanding or contracting. [3] Einstein first thought Friedmann had made an error, then accepted that the mathematics was right but doubted that it had any physical meaning.

5. Lemaître’s Expanding Universe

Georges Lemaître, a Belgian priest and physicist who had studied with Eddington in Cambridge and at Harvard and MIT, independently found expanding solutions and connected them with observations. In a 1927 paper in a Belgian journal, he showed that in an expanding universe the galaxies should recede with speeds proportional to their distances, and combined Slipher’s velocities with Hubble’s distances to estimate the rate: about 625 km/s for every megaparsec of distance. [4]

Hardly anyone noticed. Einstein, meeting Lemaître in 1927, reportedly told him that his calculations were correct but his physics “abominable”. When the paper was translated into English for the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1931, the paragraph with the estimate of the expansion rate was left out. For years this was blamed on others, until in 2011 a letter was found showing that Lemaître himself had made the translation and dropped the paragraph, since Hubble’s better data had by then appeared. [5]

6. Hubble’s Law

In 1929 Hubble published a graph of the speeds of 24 galaxies against their distances, measured with Cepheids and with the brightest stars in each galaxy. The speeds were mostly Slipher’s. Despite large scatter, the trend was clear: the farther away a galaxy, the faster it recedes, with the speed roughly proportional to the distance. [6] With Milton Humason, who obtained spectra of ever fainter galaxies, he extended the relation to much greater distances in 1931.

$$v = H_0\, d$$

The constant of proportionality, H0, is now called the Hubble constant, and is usually given in kilometres per second per megaparsec. Hubble’s value was about 500. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended renaming the relation the Hubble–Lemaître law, to recognise Lemaître’s earlier work.

An expanding universe explains the pattern naturally. If every distance between galaxies is stretched by the same factor, then a galaxy twice as far away moves away twice as fast, and every observer, in every galaxy, sees the same thing. We are not at the centre of the expansion; there is no centre.

7. Too Young for Its Own Rocks

If galaxies have been moving apart at steady speeds, then running the film backwards brings them together at a time 1/H0 ago: distance divided by speed. With Hubble’s value of 500 km/s/Mpc, that is only about two billion years. But by the 1930s, radioactive dating of rocks was giving ages of more than two billion years for the Earth (Part 7). The universe could not be younger than the planets in it.

The problem was in the distances. Walter Baade’s discovery in 1952 of two kinds of Cepheid (Part 9) roughly doubled them. Then Allan Sandage, Hubble’s successor at Mount Wilson and Palomar, found that what Hubble had taken for the brightest stars in distant galaxies were often glowing clouds of gas, much brighter still. In 1958 he estimated a Hubble constant of about 75 km/s/Mpc, “with a possible uncertainty of a factor of 2”. [7] The universe now appeared to be more than ten billion years old, comfortably older than the Earth.

8. The Primeval Atom and the Big Bang

In 1931 Lemaître took the next step: if the universe is expanding, it was once compressed into an extremely dense state, which he called a “primeval atom”, whose break-up started the expansion. [8] In the late 1940s George Gamow, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman developed the idea of a hot, dense early universe in which the lightest elements were forged. In 1948 Alpher and Herman pointed out that the radiation from this hot phase should still fill the universe, cooled by the expansion to about five degrees above absolute zero. [9]

Not everyone liked the idea of a beginning. In 1948 Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle proposed a steady-state universe that expands forever but stays the same, as new matter is continuously created to fill the gaps. It was Hoyle who, in a 1949 BBC radio talk, called the rival theory the “big bang”. The name stuck.

9. The Echo of the Beginning

In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, at Bell Telephone Laboratories in New Jersey, were using a large horn-shaped radio antenna for astronomy. They found a faint hiss of microwave noise that would not go away: it came equally from every direction, day and night, whatever they did, including removing the droppings of pigeons that nested in the horn. It corresponded to a temperature of about 3.5 kelvin. [10] Nearby at Princeton, Robert Dicke’s group was building an instrument to look for exactly this radiation. Hearing of the result, Dicke reportedly told his colleagues: “Boys, we’ve been scooped.” The two groups published side by side in 1965.

The cosmic microwave background is light released about 380,000 years after the beginning, when the universe had cooled enough for atoms to form and light to travel freely. Its wavelength has been stretched about 1,100 times by the expansion since then. In 1990 the COBE satellite showed that its spectrum matches that of an ideal glowing body at 2.725 kelvin with extraordinary precision, [11] and in 1992 COBE found tiny variations in its temperature, about one part in 100,000, the seeds of galaxies. The steady-state theory could not explain it, and the big bang became the standard picture.

10. Climbing the Distance Ladder

Measuring H0 precisely needs distances to galaxies far enough away that their own random motions, a few hundred km/s, are small compared with the expansion. Cepheids cannot be seen that far. The solution is the cosmic distance ladder: each method is calibrated with the one below it.

The brightest rungs are Type Ia supernovae: explosions of white dwarf stars in binary systems that, for a few weeks, can outshine their whole galaxy. In 1993 Mark Phillips showed that the brightest of them fade most slowly, so that their peak brightness can be corrected to a standard value with an accuracy of about 10%. [12] They can be seen across billions of light-years. Calibrating them with Cepheids in nearby galaxies that host supernovae, and Cepheids with parallaxes, connects the expansion of the universe to the geometry of the Earth’s orbit.

11. The Accelerating Universe

Everyone expected gravity to be slowing the expansion. In the 1990s two teams, the Supernova Cosmology Project led by Saul Perlmutter and the High-z Supernova Search Team including Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess, set out to measure how much, by comparing distant supernovae with nearby ones. In 1998 they reported the opposite of what they expected: distant supernovae were roughly 10 to 25% fainter than predicted for a slowing universe. The expansion has been speeding up for the last several billion years. [13, 14]

The cause is called dark energy, and its simplest form is Einstein’s cosmological constant, returned with the opposite purpose. Combined with measurements of the cosmic microwave background, the results imply that ordinary matter makes up only about 5% of the universe’s content, dark matter about 27% (Part 11), and dark energy about 68%. The three team leaders shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

12. 13.8 Billion Years, and a Puzzle

The most precise age comes from the cosmic microwave background. The European Space Agency’s Planck satellite (2009–2013) mapped its temperature variations across the whole sky. The pattern of spots depends on the contents and expansion history of the universe, much as the tone of a bell depends on its shape and material. Fitting the standard cosmological model to Planck’s map gives an age of 13.79 billion years with an uncertainty of about 20 million years, and a Hubble constant of 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc. [15] This age agrees with the ages of the oldest stars, about 12 to 13 billion years.

But the distance ladder gives a different expansion rate. In 2022 the SH0ES team, led by Riess, using Cepheids observed with the Hubble Space Telescope and Gaia parallaxes, found 73.0 ± 1.0 km/s/Mpc. [16] The difference of about 8% is far larger than either team’s stated uncertainty. Other ladder measurements, such as those of Wendy Freedman’s team using red giant stars instead of Cepheids, have found values near 70, between the two. [17] This disagreement, the Hubble tension, is one of the main open questions in cosmology. Either some measurements have a hidden systematic error, or the standard model of the universe is missing something.

14. Try It Yourself: A Balloon Universe

Try it yourself: Hubble’s law on a balloon
  1. Blow up a balloon a little, and hold it closed with a clip without tying it. Draw about ten dots on it with a marker: these are galaxies. Label one dot “home”.
  2. Using a piece of string and a ruler, measure the distance over the surface from home to each of the other dots, and write the distances down.
  3. Blow the balloon up further, to about twice the size, and measure the distances again.
  4. For each dot, work out how far it moved (the new distance minus the old). Plot distance moved against the original distance. You should get roughly a straight line: farther dots moved farther in the same time, so they moved faster.
  5. Choose a different dot as home and repeat. You should find the same pattern: every dot sees the others moving away.

The balloon has a limitation: its surface is two-dimensional, and the inside of the balloon is not part of the “universe”. The real expansion has no centre and no outside that we know of.

15. How Did They Know? Splitting Faint Light

How did they know? Spectra of faint galaxies

Slipher’s achievement was technical. A spiral nebula is a faint, spread-out glow, and spreading its light into a spectrum makes it fainter still. Slipher used a spectrograph with a fast camera lens and a narrow slit on the Lowell Observatory’s 24-inch refractor, and exposed photographic plates for many hours, often tens of hours spread over several nights, keeping the telescope precisely guided. [1]

The result was a tiny smudge of a spectrum, a millimetre or two long, which he measured under a microscope against a reference spectrum of known lines from a lamp, photographed on the same plate. The shift of a few hundredths of a millimetre gave the velocity. Doppler shifts of light had been measured for stars since the 1860s and 1880s, so the principle was well established; the challenge was getting enough light.

Today, spectrographs fed by thousands of optical fibres can record the spectra of thousands of galaxies at once, and surveys have measured the redshifts of tens of millions of galaxies, mapping the universe in three dimensions.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: who discovered the expanding universe?

The legend: Edwin Hubble discovered that the universe is expanding.

The record: the speeds came mostly from Slipher; the theory of expansion came from Friedmann and Lemaître; and Lemaître had already derived the speed–distance relation and estimated its slope two years before Hubble. Hubble’s contribution was the distances, and a graph that convinced astronomers. Hubble himself was cautious about interpreting redshifts as a real expansion and long referred to them as “apparent velocities”. [5, 6]

A second legend: Einstein called the cosmological constant his “biggest blunder”. The record: the phrase comes from George Gamow’s memoir, published after Einstein’s death, and it has not been found in Einstein’s own writings. Einstein did drop the constant after the discovery of expansion. Whether he said those words is uncertain, and since 1998 the constant has been back in favour.

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: how can a universe have an age?

“The age of the universe” sounds like a measurement, but it is really a conclusion drawn from a model: the standard cosmological model, with its dark matter and dark energy, whose parameters are fitted to observations. The figure of 13.8 billion years is very precise given the model. If the model is wrong in some way, as the Hubble tension may be hinting, the age could shift.

Does this make the number unreliable? Not necessarily. Several quite different methods, the expansion rate, the cosmic background, and the ages of the oldest stars and of radioactive elements in them, all point to a universe between about 12 and 15 billion years old. The tension is about the details, not the big picture. In science, a disagreement between careful measurements is not a failure; it is often where the next discovery is hiding.

Lemaître, a Catholic priest, was careful to keep his science separate from his faith. When Pope Pius XII suggested in 1951 that the big bang supported the idea of creation, Lemaître urged caution. Should a scientific theory ever be used to answer religious questions?
