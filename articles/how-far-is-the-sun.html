How Far Is the Sun? Half Moons, Transits of Venus and Radar Echoes

For two thousand years the distance to the Sun was the great unknown of astronomy. Finding it took geometry, a worldwide race to watch Venus cross the Sun, and finally radar.

1. The Hardest Distance

In Part 3 we found the Moon's distance by shadows and parallax: seen from two places on Earth, the Moon shifts against the stars by about a degree, which is easy to measure. Try the same with the Sun, and the shift is about 9 seconds of arc, some 400 times smaller. That is the width of a coin seen from 600 metres away. For most of history, nobody could measure it at all.

Yet the Sun's distance mattered more than almost any other number in astronomy. By the 1600s astronomers knew the shape of the solar system very well: they could say that Mars is about one and a half times as far from the Sun as the Earth is, and Jupiter about five times. What they did not know was the scale. The Earth–Sun distance, today called the astronomical unit (AU), was the missing number on the map. Find it, and every distance in the solar system would follow.

This part follows the long hunt for that number: from a Greek geometer watching the half Moon, through a map of the planets without a scale, to the first truly worldwide scientific project, two transits of Venus that sent astronomers to Siberia, India, the Arctic and Tahiti. It ends with radar echoes from Venus, and with the AU becoming so precisely known that it was finally fixed by definition.

3. Aristarchus and the Half Moon

The first attempt was a beautiful piece of geometry by Aristarchus of Samos, the astronomer we met in Part 3. His idea was this. At the moment the Moon is exactly half lit, the Sun's light must strike it at a right angle to our line of sight. So the Earth, the Moon and the Sun form a right-angled triangle, with the right angle at the Moon. If you measure the angle at the Earth, between the half Moon and the Sun, the shape of the triangle is fixed, and so is the ratio of the Sun's distance to the Moon's. [2, 1]

Aristarchus took this angle to be 87°, and concluded that the Sun is between 18 and 20 times farther away than the Moon. Since the Sun and the Moon look the same size in the sky, the Sun must also be 18 to 20 times bigger than the Moon, and much bigger than the Earth. Some historians think this is what led Aristarchus to his most daring idea: that the huge Sun, not the small Earth, is at the centre. [2]

The method was perfectly sound. The trouble is that the true angle is about 89.85°, which makes the Sun about 390 times farther away than the Moon. The difference between 89.85° and 90° is a fifth of the Moon's apparent width, and it is almost impossible to judge the exact moment of half Moon: the edge of the shadow on the Moon is rough with mountains and craters. As Figure 2 shows, the half Moon would have to be timed to within minutes. Aristarchus's answer was twenty times too small, but it was the first attempt ever to measure the distance to the Sun, and it showed that the Sun is vast.

4. A Small Universe: Ptolemy to Copernicus

Later Greek astronomers used other arguments, based on eclipses and on the assumption that the Sun's parallax was too small to see. Hipparchus and Ptolemy arrived at distances of around a thousand Earth radii. In the Almagest, around AD 150, Ptolemy put the Sun about 1,210 Earth radii away, about 7.7 million km. [3, 1]

That figure, or something close to it, was accepted for about 1,500 years: by astronomers in the Islamic world, in medieval Europe and even by Copernicus, whose Sun-centred system of 1543 used a similar value. It was not a foolish number. It was close to the limit of what could be ruled out by observation: a closer Sun would have had a visible parallax. But it was nearly twenty times too small. [1]

5. Kepler's Map Without a Scale

In 1619 Johannes Kepler published the third of his laws of planetary motion. It states that the square of a planet's orbital period is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun. Because the periods were known accurately from centuries of observation, the law gave the relative distances of all the planets at once. [4, 1]

Planet Orbital period Distance from the Sun (Earth = 1)
Mercury88 days0.39
Venus225 days0.72
Earth1 year1
Mars1.88 years1.52
Jupiter11.9 years5.20
Saturn29.5 years9.54

This was a perfect map with no scale bar. Every distance was known in "Earth–Sun distances", but nobody knew how many kilometres (or miles, or Earth radii) that was. The good news was that measuring any distance in the solar system, Earth to Mars or Earth to Venus, would now give all the others. The hunt for the Sun's distance became a hunt for the nearest planets.

Kepler himself, working with Tycho Brahe's precise observations of Mars, could find no parallax for the Sun at all. He concluded that its parallax was at most about one minute of arc, which put the Sun at least some 3,400 Earth radii away: about three times Ptolemy's figure, and still far too small, but a step in the right direction. [1]

6. Mars from Two Continents: Cassini and Richer

In 1672 Mars came unusually close to the Earth. Giovanni Domenico Cassini, director of the new Paris Observatory, seized the chance. He sent the astronomer Jean Richer to Cayenne in French Guiana, about 7,000 km away, while he and his colleagues observed from Paris. Both measured the position of Mars against nearby stars at agreed times; the slight difference between the two views gave the parallax of Mars, and Kepler's law turned the Earth–Mars distance into the Earth–Sun distance. [1]

Cassini's result put the Sun about 140 million km away (in modern units), only about 6 per cent short of the true value. It was the first estimate in the right range, and a staggering jump from Ptolemy's 7.7 million km: the solar system was suddenly almost twenty times bigger than anyone had believed. But the parallax of Mars was only about 20 seconds of arc, close to the limit of what telescopes of the day could measure, so many astronomers regarded the result as a lucky guess rather than a measurement. A better method was needed. [1, 5]

7. Halley's Plan: Timing Venus

Occasionally Venus passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, and appears as a small black dot crossing the Sun's face. These transits of Venus are rare: they come in pairs eight years apart, separated by more than a century. The last pair was in 2004 and 2012; the next will not come until December 2117 and December 2125. [21] In 1639 a young English astronomer, Jeremiah Horrocks, corrected Kepler's tables, predicted a transit, and with his friend William Crabtree became the first person known to see one. [22, 20]

In 1716 Edmond Halley, of comet fame, published a plan to use transits to find the Sun's distance. [7] Venus is much closer to us than the Sun is, so observers at different places on Earth see it cross the Sun along slightly different paths. An observer far to the north sees Venus's path shifted south across the Sun, and an observer in the south sees it shifted north. Because the Sun is round, one path is a longer chord than the other, so the transit lasts a different length of time at the two places. Measure the two durations, and the geometry gives the shift between the paths, the parallax of Venus, and from that the Sun's distance.

The beauty of the plan was that it turned a tiny angle into a time difference that ordinary clocks could measure. Halley estimated that observers in suitable places would see durations differing by about a quarter of an hour, which could be timed to a few seconds. He calculated that the Sun's distance could be found to better than one part in 500. [7, 5] Halley knew he would not live to see it: the next transits were due in 1761 and 1769, and he was born in 1656. He died in 1742, leaving his plan as a challenge to the next generation.

8. 1761 and 1769: The World's First Big Science Project

The challenge was taken up on a scale never seen before. For the transit of 6 June 1761, observers went to dozens of stations across the world, despite the Seven Years' War raging between Britain and France. For 3–4 June 1769 there were even more. Scientific academies, governments and navies paid for expeditions, and astronomers on opposite sides of the war shared their results. [5, 6] Some of their stories are among the most dramatic in the history of science.

  • Guillaume Le Gentil sailed from France in 1760 to observe from Pondicherry, the French colony in south India. By the time his ship arrived, the British had captured Pondicherry, and he had to watch the 1761 transit from the rolling deck of a ship, where no accurate measurement was possible. Rather than return, he stayed in the region for eight years to wait for 1769, and went back to Pondicherry. The weather was clear for weeks; on the morning of the transit, clouds covered the Sun, and cleared soon after it ended. When he finally reached France in 1771, more than eleven years after leaving, he found that he had been declared dead, his property had been divided up, and his place at the Academy given away. [9, 6]
  • Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon, later famous for surveying the Mason–Dixon line in America, set out in 1761 for Sumatra. A French warship attacked their ship in the English Channel; they made it instead to the Cape of Good Hope, where they observed the transit successfully. [6]
  • Jean-Baptiste Chappe d'Auteroche crossed Russia by sledge to observe the 1761 transit in Siberia. In 1769 he observed from Baja California, in Mexico, where an epidemic was raging. He died there on 1 August 1769, a few weeks after the transit; his results were brought back to France by a member of his party. [6]
  • Maximilian Hell, a Jesuit astronomer from Vienna, observed the 1769 transit from Vardø, an island in the Arctic north of Norway. [6]
  • James Cook commanded HMS Endeavour on a Royal Navy and Royal Society expedition to the newly visited island of Tahiti in the Pacific. On 3 June 1769, under a clear sky, Cook, the astronomer Charles Green and the naturalist Daniel Solander timed the transit from a fortified camp on a spit of land still called Point Venus. [8, 6]

When all the observations were collected, they did not agree as well as Halley had hoped. Different astronomers derived solar parallaxes ranging from about 8.4 to 8.9 seconds of arc. In 1824 the German astronomer Johann Encke reanalysed all the observations of 1761 and 1769 and obtained a solar parallax of 8.58 seconds of arc, a distance of about 153 million km. [10, 5] That was within about 2.5 per cent of the true value: a triumph of international cooperation, even if not the 1-in-500 precision Halley had promised. Encke's value was used for decades.

9. The Black Drop

Why did the timings disagree? The main culprit was an unexpected effect at the moments of contact. Halley's method needs the exact instant when Venus's black disc just touches the inside edge of the Sun. But as the two edges separated, observers saw a dark band or "ligament" stretch between them, like a drop of ink clinging to the edge, before it suddenly snapped. Observers standing side by side disagreed by many seconds about when contact happened. Cook himself recorded that he, Green and Solander differed by much more than expected. [8, 5]

For a long time the black drop effect was blamed on Venus's atmosphere. Modern studies, including space-telescope observations of transits of Mercury, a planet with no atmosphere, show that it comes mainly from the blurring of the telescope (and of the Earth's air) combined with the darkening of the Sun towards its edge. Venus's atmosphere adds only a thin bright ring. [11] In other words, the problem was in the observing, not in Venus: an "optical gremlin" of exactly the kind discussed in our tutorial on optical artifacts.

10. Cameras, Asteroids and an Agreed Number

By the transits of 1874 and 1882, photography had arrived. Many nations sent expeditions with cameras, hoping that photographs would remove the uncertainties of the human eye. The results were disappointing: the photographs were not sharp or stable enough to beat a skilled observer. [19, 18] Meanwhile other methods improved: measurements of the parallax of Mars and of small asteroids, and indirect methods based on the speed of light and on the Moon's motion.

The American astronomer Simon Newcomb combined all the available methods into a single system of astronomical constants. In 1896 an international conference in Paris adopted a solar parallax of 8.80 seconds of arc, corresponding to about 149.5 million km. [12, 17] Then, in 1930–31, the small asteroid Eros passed within about 26 million km of the Earth, closer than any planet. Observatories around the world photographed its position against the stars, and in 1941 the British Astronomer Royal, Harold Spencer Jones, announced a solar parallax of 8.790 seconds of arc: about 149.7 million km. [13, 10]

11. Radar Echoes from Venus

In Part 3 we saw radar echoes return from the Moon in 1946. Even at its closest, Venus is about a hundred times farther away, and because a radar echo weakens with the fourth power of distance, an echo from Venus is millions of times weaker. In 1958 a team at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory announced that they had detected echoes from Venus, but later measurements showed that the claimed result was not genuine. [14]

The real breakthrough came in 1961, when Venus was at its closest. On 10 March 1961 the Jet Propulsion Laboratory's Goldstone antennas in California began receiving strong echoes, and teams at Lincoln Laboratory and at Jodrell Bank in England also detected Venus that year. By timing the round trip of the radio pulses, the JPL team found an astronomical unit of 149,598,500 km, with an uncertainty of about 500 km. [15, 14] In one step, the uncertainty in the Sun's distance had shrunk by more than a hundred times. It turned out that the values used until then were off by tens of thousands of kilometres, which mattered for aiming the first spacecraft at Venus.

Since then, radar ranging to the planets, and the tracking of spacecraft by radio, has refined the AU to within a few metres. By 2012 it was known so precisely that the International Astronomical Union decided to stop measuring it and instead define it: the astronomical unit is now exactly 149,597,870,700 metres. [16] Light takes about 8 minutes and 19 seconds to cover it.

13. Try It Yourself: Weigh Up the Sun with a Pinhole

Try it yourself: how big is the Sun?

Never look at the Sun directly, or through binoculars, a telescope or a camera. This experiment only looks at the Sun's image on a card.

  1. Make a small, clean hole (1–2 mm) in a piece of card or aluminium foil.
  2. On a sunny day, let sunlight pass through the hole onto a sheet of white paper in the shade, a metre or two away. You will see a small round image of the Sun.
  3. Measure the diameter of the image and the distance from the hole to the paper.
  4. Divide the distance by the diameter. You should get about 108: the Sun is 108 times farther away than it is wide.

Now use the astronomers' result. If the Sun is 149.6 million km away, its diameter must be about 149.6 million ÷ 108 ≈ 1.4 million km, about 109 times the diameter of the Earth. You have just done the last step that every astronomer from Aristarchus onwards did: once you know the distance, the size follows from the angle. (The same pinhole trick works for the Moon, as in Part 3.)

14. How Did They Know? Clocks, Longitude and Halley's Trick

How did they know? Timing the transit on the other side of the world

To compare observations from Tahiti, Siberia and the Cape, astronomers needed two things: accurate times and accurate positions. In the 1760s neither was easy. Pendulum clocks were set up on site and regulated each day by observing the Sun or stars crossing the local meridian, which gives local time very precisely. But comparing local times at different places requires knowing their longitudes, and in the 1760s longitude at sea or in remote places was found by careful astronomy: timing the eclipses of Jupiter's moons, or measuring the Moon's position against the stars, and comparing with tables computed for Paris or Greenwich. [5, 6]

Halley's method was designed around this weakness. Because it compares the duration of the transit at each place, from the first inner contact to the last, each observer only needs a clock that runs steadily for six hours; the longitude hardly matters. The drawback is that the observer must see both the beginning and the end of the transit, so clouds at either moment ruin the measurement. The French astronomer Joseph-Nicolas Delisle proposed a variation that uses the absolute times of a single contact, which allowed many more stations but required the longitudes to be known much better. Both methods were used in 1761 and 1769. [5]

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: was Cook's voyage only about Venus?

The legend: James Cook's first great voyage was a purely scientific expedition to observe the transit of Venus.

The record: the transit was the official purpose, and it was observed successfully. But Cook also carried sealed secret instructions from the Admiralty, to be opened after the transit. They ordered him to sail south in search of the great southern continent, Terra Australis, that geographers believed must exist, and to claim lands for Britain. His search led him to chart the coasts of New Zealand and the east coast of Australia. [8, 6]

The grain of truth: science really was a major motive. The Royal Society asked for the expedition and chose Tahiti for the observation. But astronomy, navigation, trade and empire were intertwined, as they were for most of the transit expeditions.

A second legend: "the transits of Venus gave the precise distance to the Sun." They gave a far better value than ever before, within a few per cent, but the black drop and other problems left a spread of results, and the transits of the 1870s and 1880s did not settle the question either. The decisive improvements came from asteroids and, finally, radar.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: when a measurement becomes a definition

For more than two thousand years, the distance to the Sun was the great unknown of astronomy. Today it cannot be measured at all, in a sense, because it is no longer a measurement: since 2012 the astronomical unit has been defined as a fixed number of metres, just as the metre itself is now defined through the speed of light. What happened?

The answer is that the role of the number changed. Once it was the uncertain bridge between the solar system's shape (known from Kepler) and its size (known from nothing). Now it is simply a convenient unit, and the questions that remain, such as exactly how far the Earth is from the Sun on a given day, are answered by tracking spacecraft and planets with radio, in metres. The story shows a common pattern in science: a quantity that was once the hardest thing to measure becomes, through better methods, a fixed reference against which other things are measured.

The story also shows the difference between a good method and a good measurement. Aristarchus's geometry was perfect, but his angle was wrong by three degrees, and the answer was wrong by a factor of twenty. Halley's method was clever, but nature added a black drop he did not foresee. Progress came not from cleverer ideas alone, but from better instruments, more observers, honest error estimates and the patience to repeat.

Questions to think about: If the AU is now defined, could it ever turn out to be "wrong"? Is a method that cannot be carried out in practice still a good method?
