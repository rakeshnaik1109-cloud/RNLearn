Socrates, Plato and Aristotle: How Should We Ask Questions About the World?

A stonemason’s son who asked questions, an aristocrat who looked beyond the senses, and a doctor’s son who catalogued the living world: three teachers in Athens who set out the two great paths to knowledge.

1. Three Teachers in Athens

In the fourth and fifth centuries BCE, in a single city of perhaps a few hundred thousand people, three thinkers formed a chain of teacher and pupil that shaped how the world would think for the next two thousand years. Socrates taught Plato. Plato taught Aristotle. And Aristotle, for a few years, taught a Macedonian prince who became Alexander the Great.

They asked very different questions in very different ways. Socrates wrote nothing and spent his life questioning people in the marketplace. Plato wrote brilliant dialogues and argued that the true reality lies in perfect, unchanging Forms that only reason can grasp. Aristotle, the most scientific of the three, catalogued animals, invented formal logic and built a physics that lasted until Galileo. This first part of the series asks how these three changed the way we ask questions about the world, and what each got right and wrong. [1, 2]

3. Socrates: The Man Who Asked Questions

Socrates was born in Athens around 470/469 BCE. He served as a foot soldier in the Peloponnesian War, married, had three sons, and spent his days in the streets and gymnasia of the city, talking. He charged no fees and wrote nothing. Almost everything we know about him comes from others: above all from the dialogues of his pupil Plato, from the soldier and writer Xenophon, and from the comic playwright Aristophanes, whose play Clouds of 423 BCE mocked him as a fraud who studied the heavens and taught young men to win arguments by trickery. [3, 4, 5]

This creates the famous Socratic problem: the Socrates of Plato’s dialogues often speaks for Plato’s own ideas, and scholars disagree about where the historical man ends and the literary character begins. [6] Most agree on a core. Socrates was concerned less with the natural world than with how to live: what is justice, courage, piety, virtue? And he was convinced that most people, including himself, did not really know the answers.

According to Plato’s Apology, a friend asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and was told that no one was. Puzzled, Socrates questioned people with a reputation for wisdom and found that they thought they knew things they did not. He concluded that he was wiser only in this: he did not think he knew what he did not know. [3]

4. The Socratic Method

Socrates’ way of testing a claim is called the elenchus (cross-examination), or the Socratic method. It usually goes like this. Someone offers a definition (“courage is endurance”). Socrates asks questions that lead them to agree to other statements (“foolish endurance is not admirable, but courage is”). These turn out to contradict the original definition, which must be revised or abandoned. Often the dialogue ends in aporia, a state of puzzlement, with no definition left standing. [6]

The method is negative: it shows what is wrong rather than proving what is right. Yet it is the ancestor of much that we now value in science: testing claims against counterexamples, refusing to accept an idea because an authority holds it, and admitting ignorance. A hypothesis that survives determined attempts to refute it is more trustworthy than one that has never been tested, an idea Karl Popper would make central to his philosophy of science (Part 12). Philosophers still test definitions in the Socratic way: in 1963 Edmund Gettier produced cases of justified true belief that do not seem to be knowledge, reopening a question Plato had left in aporia. [7]

5. The Trial and Death of Socrates

In 399 BCE, three Athenian citizens accused Socrates of impiety (not recognising the city’s gods and introducing new ones) and of corrupting the young. The background was political: Athens had lost the war with Sparta, and some of Socrates’ associates had been involved in a brutal oligarchic regime. The trial was held before a jury of about five hundred citizens. [8]

Plato’s Apology presents Socrates’ defence. He refuses to beg for mercy and insists that he will go on questioning people, because “the unexamined life is not worth living”. He was found guilty by a small margin; Plato has him remark that if only thirty votes had gone the other way, he would have been acquitted. When asked to propose a penalty, he at first suggested free meals at public expense, as a benefactor of the city. The jury voted for death. [3]

Friends arranged an escape, but Socrates refused, arguing in Plato’s Crito that he must obey the laws of the city that had raised him. Plato’s Phaedo describes his last hours, discussing the immortality of the soul before drinking a cup of hemlock. His death made him a symbol of intellectual integrity: someone who would rather die than stop asking questions.

6. Plato and the World of Forms

Plato was born into a wealthy Athenian family around 428/427 BCE. He was in his late twenties when Socrates died, an event that turned him away from politics. After travelling, he founded a school around 387 BCE in a grove sacred to the hero Academus: the Academy, which in some form lasted for centuries and gave its name to academic life. His writings, around thirty-five dialogues, survive almost complete. [3, 2]

Plato’s central idea is the theory of Forms. The things we see are always changing and always imperfect: no drawn circle is perfectly round, no act perfectly just. Yet we understand perfect circularity and perfect justice. Plato concluded that these perfect, eternal patterns, the Forms, are real, and indeed more real than the objects that imperfectly copy them. They are grasped not by the senses but by reason.

His most famous image is the allegory of the cave, in the Republic. Prisoners chained in a cave since birth see only shadows of objects cast on a wall, and take the shadows for reality. A prisoner who is freed and climbs out into the sunlight is at first dazzled, but eventually sees things as they really are, and the Sun that makes them visible. Returning to tell the others, he is mocked and, Plato hints, might be killed. [3] The senses show us shadows; philosophy leads us out of the cave.

7. Knowledge as Recollection: The Slave Boy and the Square

If knowledge of the Forms does not come from the senses, where does it come from? In the Meno, Plato offers a striking answer: we already have it, from before we were born, and learning is recollection. To illustrate, Socrates questions an enslaved boy who has never studied geometry about how to make a square with twice the area of a square with sides of two feet. [3]

The boy first says confidently: double the side. That gives a square of 16 square feet, four times as large. Then he tries a side of three feet: 9 square feet, still too large. Now he admits that he does not know, and Socrates points out that this confusion is progress. Finally, by drawing the diagonals of four copies of the original square, Socrates leads him to see that the square built on the diagonal has exactly twice the area:

$$\text{side} = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}\ \text{feet}, \qquad \text{area} = 8\ \text{square feet}.$$

Plato drew a philosophical conclusion: the boy was not taught, only asked, so the knowledge must have been in him already. Few philosophers today accept recollection, but the passage raises a question that never went away: how much of our knowledge, especially in mathematics, comes from experience, and how much from reason alone? It also hides a mathematical shock that the Greeks had recently discovered: the length √8 cannot be written as a ratio of whole numbers, so the side of the doubled square is incommensurable with the original. [9]

8. Mathematics as the Language of Reality: The Platonic Solids

Plato placed mathematics at the heart of education, as the training that turns the mind from the changing world towards the eternal Forms. In the Timaeus, his account of how the cosmos was made, he went further: the elements themselves are built from geometry. Fire is made of tetrahedra (sharp, hence its burning), earth of cubes (stable), air of octahedra and water of icosahedra; the fifth solid, the dodecahedron, the craftsman used “for the whole”. [3]

The details are fanciful, but the ambition was prophetic. The idea that the deepest reality is mathematical, that nature is written in the language of geometry, runs from Plato through Kepler, who tried to fit the planetary orbits between nested Platonic solids, and Galileo, to modern physics, where symmetry groups describe the fundamental particles.

9. Aristotle: From Ideas to Observations

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE in Stagira, in northern Greece, the son of a physician at the Macedonian court. At about seventeen he joined Plato’s Academy and stayed for twenty years, until Plato’s death in 347 BCE. He then spent several years on the coast of Asia Minor and the island of Lesbos, became tutor to the young Alexander of Macedon around 343 BCE, and in 335 BCE returned to Athens to found his own school, the Lyceum. His habit of teaching while walking in its covered walkway (the peripatos) gave his followers the name Peripatetics. [10, 11]

His surviving works, mostly lecture notes, cover an astonishing range: logic, physics, astronomy, biology, psychology, ethics, politics, rhetoric and poetry. [12] Although he learned from Plato, he disagreed with him on the most basic point. For Aristotle there is no separate world of Forms: the form of a thing exists in the thing, and we come to know it by studying many particular things through the senses. Knowledge begins with observation. Disagreeing with his teacher, he wrote that although both friends and truth are dear, piety requires us to honour truth first. [12]

10. Logic: The Syllogism

Aristotle invented formal logic, the study of which arguments are valid by their form alone. His central tool is the syllogism: two premises sharing a middle term, and a conclusion that follows from them. [12]

  • All mammals are animals. All whales are mammals. Therefore all whales are animals.
  • No reptiles are mammals. All snakes are reptiles. Therefore no snakes are mammals.

The key insight is that validity depends only on the pattern: “All M are P; all S are M; therefore all S are P” is valid whatever S, M and P stand for. Medieval logicians gave the valid patterns memorable names, such as Barbara and Celarent for the two forms above. Aristotle’s logic remained the core of logic for more than two thousand years, until the work of Boole, Frege and others in the nineteenth century, and its idea of checking validity mechanically lies behind the computers of today (Part 12).

11. Aristotle the Biologist

On Lesbos, beside a great lagoon, Aristotle and his colleague Theophrastus studied living things with a thoroughness no one would match for centuries. In works such as the History of Animals, Parts of Animals and Generation of Animals, Aristotle described hundreds of kinds of animals: their anatomy, behaviour, reproduction and development. He watched chick embryos develop day by day in eggs, classified animals by features such as blood and mode of reproduction, and recognised that dolphins breathe air and suckle their young. He described the specialised arm that male octopuses use in mating, a structure now called the hectocotylus. [12, 13]

He also asked why things are as they are, and answered with his doctrine of the four causes, four kinds of explanation for anything:

  • the material cause: what it is made of (bronze, for a statue);
  • the formal cause: its form or structure (the shape of the statue);
  • the efficient cause: what brought it about (the sculptor);
  • the final cause: what it is for (to honour a god).

In biology, final causes made sense: eyes are for seeing, teeth for chewing. Aristotle applied them to the whole of nature, and here he went astray: a stone falls because its natural place is at the centre of the cosmos. Modern science largely abandoned final causes in physics, though biologists still ask what a structure is for, a question Darwin would reinterpret in terms of natural selection (Part 8).

12. Aristotle’s Physics: Wrong, but Not Foolish

Aristotle taught that every element has a natural place: earth at the centre, then water, air and fire. Bodies move naturally towards their places; heavier bodies, having more earth, fall faster, with a speed proportional to their weight and inversely proportional to the resistance of the medium. A vacuum, he argued, is impossible, because in it bodies would move infinitely fast. [12]

This physics is often ridiculed, because Galileo showed that in air, light and heavy bodies fall almost together (Part 5). But the physicist Carlo Rovelli has pointed out that Aristotle’s rules are a good description of what we actually see in everyday life: motion through a resisting medium, where objects quickly reach a steady terminal speed. In a thick fluid, a heavier body of the same size really does fall faster, roughly in proportion to its weight. [14] Aristotle’s mistake was to take this special case for the general law.

In this simulation the two balls land together in air (about 0.64 s), but in water the iron ball takes 1.2 s against the stone’s 2.3 s, and in honey about 15 s against 87 s. Aristotle’s physics was not stupid; it was a theory built from careful observation of a world full of friction, which turned out not to be the most fundamental description.

13. The Long Shadow

Aristotle left Athens after Alexander’s death in 323 BCE, when anti-Macedonian feeling made it dangerous for him; according to a later anecdote, he said he would not let the Athenians sin twice against philosophy. He died the following year at Chalcis. [11] His works were preserved, edited and studied in the Greek, Arabic and Latin worlds. Philosophers such as Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd (Part 4) wrote major commentaries; to medieval European scholars like Thomas Aquinas he was simply “the Philosopher”. His authority became so great that challenging him required courage, which is why the scientific revolution of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was in large part a revolt against Aristotle.

Plato’s influence ran through a different channel: mathematics and the belief that reason can reach truths beyond experience. Socrates left an attitude: question everything, including yourself. Between them they set out the two great paths to knowledge, reason and experience, whose relationship is the theme of much of this series.

14. Try It Yourself: A Socratic Dialogue and an Aristotelian Notebook

Try it yourself: question like Socrates, observe like Aristotle
  1. Socrates: with a friend, try to define a word everyone thinks they understand: “science”, “fair”, “alive” or “measurement”. One proposes a definition; the other searches for counterexamples. Revise until you are stuck. Did you end in aporia?
  2. Plato: draw a square on squared paper and try to construct a square of twice its area without measuring, using only the diagonals, as in Figure 3.
  3. Aristotle: choose a living thing you can watch for a week: ants, a pigeon, a plant on a windowsill. Keep a notebook of exactly what you see, separating observations from guesses. At the end, try to give all four of Aristotle’s causes for one feature you noticed.

Notice how the three activities feel different: one tests ideas by argument, one by reasoning about perfect shapes, one by patient observation.

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: “Let no one ignorant of geometry enter”

The legend: above the door of Plato’s Academy was carved the inscription “Let no one ignorant of geometry enter”.

The record: no writer of Plato’s time mentions such an inscription, and nor does Plato himself in his surviving works. The earliest sources that report it are commentators on Aristotle writing in the sixth century CE, such as John Philoponus and Elias, around nine hundred years after the Academy was founded. [15] It may have been invented, perhaps modelled on inscriptions at sacred sites forbidding entry to the impure.

The grain of truth: whether or not the words were ever carved, they capture Plato’s real conviction. Mathematics was central to the Academy, several of the leading mathematicians of the age worked there, and in the Republic Plato prescribes years of mathematical study for future rulers before they may approach philosophy. [9, 3]

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: reason or experience?

In Raphael’s fresco The School of Athens (1509–1511), Plato and Aristotle walk together at the centre. Plato, holding his Timaeus, points upward to the heavens; Aristotle, holding his Ethics, stretches his hand out flat towards the earth. The gestures sum up a disagreement that runs through the history of thought. Is true knowledge reached by reason, which grasps unchanging truths, as Plato held? Or does it begin with the senses and the patient study of particular things, as Aristotle taught?

Later philosophers took sides. Descartes and Leibniz were heirs of Plato’s rationalism; Bacon, Locke and Hume developed Aristotle’s starting point into empiricism. Kant tried to combine them (Part 7). Modern science needs both: mathematics, which Plato would have recognised as knowledge from reason, and experiment, which Aristotle would have seen as the extension of careful observation. And Socrates reminds us that both can go wrong if we stop questioning.

Questions to think about: Is a mathematical truth such as “the diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side” discovered or invented? Could we know anything at all about the world without our senses?
