1. Is Heat a Fluid? Count Rumford's Cannons

Around 1800, scientists were proud of having replaced old mystical ideas with things they could measure. But some puzzles were still explained by "imponderable fluids": weightless substances thought to soak through ordinary matter and cause warmth, electricity, magnetism and light. [6, 7]

The most important was caloric, popularised by Lavoisier (Part 7). Caloric was pictured as an invisible fluid that flows from hot things to cold things. When something soaked up caloric, the caloric pushed its particles apart, making it expand, melt or boil. Caloric could be neither made nor destroyed: heat was a substance that could only move around.

The first big problem for caloric came in Munich in 1798, from an American-born adventurer and administrator named Benjamin Thompson, better known by his title Count Rumford (1753–1814). He was in charge of the Bavarian army's workshops, where cannons were made by drilling out solid metal castings. He noticed that the drilling made the metal and the shavings extremely hot, so hot that they had to be cooled with water.

Caloric theory said the heat came from the drill squeezing caloric out of the metal, like water from a sponge, so the shavings should hold less heat than solid metal. Rumford tested this by comparing how much heat the shavings and solid metal could hold. There was no difference: no caloric had been squeezed out.

Then he did a dramatic experiment. He put a cannon in a wooden box with about eighteen pounds (8 kg) of cold water, at 60 °F (16 °C). He pressed a deliberately blunt borer against the metal and had a horse turn it. The blunt borer cut away very little metal, but the water kept getting hotter: after an hour it was at 107 °F (42 °C), after two hours 178 °F (81 °C), and after two and a half hours, to the amazement of the onlookers, it boiled.

Heat had been produced without any fire, burning or loss of material. And it seemed to be endless: as long as the horse kept walking, the friction kept making heat. Something that can be produced without limit can't be a fixed amount of substance stored in the metal. Rumford concluded that heat isn't a fluid but a kind of motion: the rapid jiggling of the tiny particles that make up matter.

2. How Good Can an Engine Be? Sadi Carnot

Meanwhile, steam engines were driving the Industrial Revolution, pumping water out of mines, powering mills and pulling trains. But they were very wasteful: less than 5% of the energy in the coal they burned ended up as useful work. The rest escaped as waste heat. [1]

Engineers improved engines by trial and error: bigger boilers, higher pressures, better valves. Nobody knew whether there was a fundamental limit to how well heat could be turned into work. The person who worked it out was a young French military engineer, Sadi Carnot (1796–1832).

In 1824 Carnot published his only book, Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire. His key step was to forget about real-world problems such as friction and leaks, and imagine a perfect, idealised engine.

He compared a heat engine to a water wheel. A water wheel doesn't use up water; it gets its power from water falling from a higher level to a lower one. Carnot, still thinking in terms of caloric, imagined heat "falling" from a hot boiler to a cold condenser, producing work as it falls.

His ideal engine runs through four steps, now called the Carnot cycle. Here it is in the form later given by Émile Clapeyron, with gas in a cylinder and a frictionless piston:

  1. Expand while hot: the cylinder touches the hot source. The gas expands slowly, taking in heat and pushing the piston, while staying at the same high temperature.
  2. Expand while insulated: the cylinder is now wrapped in insulation. The gas keeps expanding and pushing the piston; with no heat coming in, it cools down to the temperature of the cold sink.
  3. Squeeze while cold: the cylinder touches the cold sink. The piston pushes the gas back in, and the gas gives up waste heat to the cold sink, staying at the low temperature.
  4. Squeeze while insulated: insulated again, the gas is squeezed further and warms back up to the starting temperature. The engine is back where it began.

Carnot proved, by a clever argument, that no engine working between the same two temperatures can beat this ideal one. If one could, you could use it to drive a Carnot engine backwards as a refrigerator, and the pair would pump heat from cold to hot for free, forever, which never happens.

So the best possible efficiency depends only on the temperatures of the hot source and the cold sink, not on the engine's design or what is inside it. Once Kelvin had set up the absolute temperature scale (measured from absolute zero, −273 °C) and Clausius had corrected Carnot's caloric picture, the result took its famous form:

$$\text{maximum efficiency} = 1 - \frac{T_{\text{cold}}}{T_{\text{hot}}}$$

For example, an engine running between 600 K and 300 K can never turn more than half its heat into work. To reach 100% you would need a cold sink at absolute zero or an infinitely hot source, both impossible. Carnot died of cholera in 1832, aged just 36, and many of his papers were burned to stop the disease spreading. But his small book became the foundation of the science of heat, thermodynamics.

3. Work Makes Heat: James Joule

Carnot's water-wheel picture had one flaw: heat isn't conserved. When an engine does work, some of the heat doesn't just "fall" to the cold side; it is actually turned into work. The proof that work and heat can be exchanged at a fixed rate came from Manchester, from the wealthy brewer and amateur physicist James Prescott Joule (1818–1889). [4, 9]

Joule was superb at measuring temperature, a skill helped by brewing, where small temperature differences matter. With specially made thermometers he could read differences of about a two-hundredth of a degree Fahrenheit.

In the 1840s Joule set out to find exactly how much work makes how much heat. His most famous apparatus was a paddle wheel in a tub of water. Inside a well-insulated copper container of water, a vertical axle carried rotating paddles, with fixed vanes on the walls to stop the water simply swirling round.

The axle was turned by cords running over pulleys to two heavy weights outside. As the weights slowly fell through a measured height, they turned the paddles, which churned the water. The churning (friction in the water) turned the motion into heat, warming the water very slightly.

When the weights reached the floor, Joule wound them back up without turning the paddles, and repeated the drop twenty times. From the tiny rise in temperature, after correcting for heat lost to the room and friction in the pulleys, he worked out the mechanical equivalent of heat: how much work produces a given amount of heat.

In his famous paper of 1850 he reported that 772 foot-pounds of work raise the temperature of one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. In modern units, that means about 4.15 joules of work make 1 calorie of heat, within about 1% of today's value of 4.184. (The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him.)

Joule got the same result in completely different ways: with electric currents, by squeezing gases, and by friction in mercury. Movement, electricity, chemical reactions and heat were all forms of one thing that can change from one form to another at fixed rates: energy.

4. The Laws of Energy: Conservation and Entropy

In 1847 a 26-year-old German army doctor, Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894), brought together the work of Joule, Carnot and Julius Robert von Mayer in a paper "On the Conservation of Force". He argued that energy is never lost: if it disappears in one form (motion, say, or chemical energy), it reappears in exactly the same amount in another (heat, electricity or light). [5, 9]

This became the first law of thermodynamics: the total energy of a closed-off system stays the same. Energy can change form, but it can't be created or destroyed.

But this raised a puzzle. If energy is always conserved, why don't things run backwards? When a cup falls and smashes, its energy turns into sound, vibration and a little warmth in the floor. The total energy is the same. So why doesn't a smashed cup ever soak up warmth from the floor, reassemble itself and jump back onto the table? The first law doesn't forbid it, yet it never happens.

The answer came between 1850 and 1865 from the German physicist Rudolf Clausius (1822–1888) and the Scottish physicist William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin (1824–1907). They saw that while work can always be turned completely into heat, heat can never be turned completely back into work. Natural processes have a direction: time has an arrow.

Kelvin described it as energy becoming less useful: the amount stays the same, but it spreads out and becomes less able to do work. Clausius found a way to measure this. In 1865 he named the quantity entropy (from a Greek word meaning "transformation"), written $S$. Roughly speaking, entropy measures how spread out or disordered energy is.

This gave the second law of thermodynamics: in any real process in a closed-off system, entropy goes up (or, in an ideal, perfectly reversible process, stays the same). It never goes down. That's why heat flows from hot to cold, why smashed cups stay smashed, and why no engine can be 100% efficient. Clausius ended his 1865 paper with two famous sentences:

  1. Die Energie der Welt ist constant: the energy of the universe is constant.
  2. Die Entropie der Welt strebt einem Maximum zu: the entropy of the universe tends towards a maximum.

5. Twitching Frogs and the First Battery: Galvani and Volta

Meanwhile, a revolution was happening in electricity. Through the 1700s, scientists could only make static electricity: sparks from rubbing glass, stored in jars called Leyden jars. These sparks were brief and hard to study. [6]

The key to a steady electric current came from an anatomy laboratory in Bologna. Around 1780 the Italian anatomy professor Luigi Galvani (1737–1798) was dissecting frogs' legs. When an assistant touched a nerve in a dead frog's leg with a metal scalpel just as a nearby machine gave off a spark, the leg kicked sharply.

Galvani studied this for years. In one experiment he hung frogs' legs on copper hooks from an iron railing, and found that the legs twitched whenever they touched the iron. He concluded that living tissue produces its own animal electricity, made in the brain and carried by the nerves to the muscles.

In Pavia, the physicist Alessandro Volta (1745–1827) was at first impressed, but then noticed something odd. The legs twitched strongly only when touched by two different metals at once, such as copper and zinc. With two pieces of the same metal, nothing happened. Volta decided the frog's leg wasn't making the electricity; it was just a very sensitive detector of electricity made by the two metals touching through something moist.

To prove it, in 1799 Volta built the first battery, the voltaic pile. He stacked discs of zinc and copper (or silver), with a piece of card or cloth soaked in salt water between each pair: zinc, wet card, copper, zinc, wet card, copper, and so on.

When he connected the top and bottom of the pile with a wire, electricity flowed steadily through it: not a single spark but a continuous current. Volta announced his invention in a letter to the Royal Society in London in March 1800. Within weeks, scientists everywhere were building piles, and electricity became something you could study and use, starting a golden age of discovery (including Davy's new metals, Part 7).

6. Electricity Moves a Compass: Ørsted

For centuries electricity and magnetism seemed to be separate things. Both could attract and repel, but static electricity picked up bits of paper while magnets attracted only iron. [6]

Some thinkers felt that all the forces of nature must be connected. In Copenhagen, the Danish scientist Hans Christian Ørsted (1777–1851), influenced by German philosophers who believed in the unity of nature, had long searched for a link between electricity and magnetism.

The breakthrough came in April 1820, during an evening lecture. Ørsted placed a thin wire connected to a battery just above a compass needle, lined up with it. When he switched on the current, the needle swung round, ending up almost at right angles to the wire.

When he reversed the current, the needle swung the other way. With the wire below the needle instead of above, it reversed again. In July 1820 he published his findings in a four-page pamphlet in Latin, which quickly spread across Europe.

Ørsted had found something new: the magnetic force from a current doesn't pull straight towards or away from the wire; it goes round the wire in circles. Every force known before, such as gravity, acted along a straight line between two objects. This one went round in a circle.

7. Currents Attract and Repel: Ampère

News of Ørsted's discovery reached Paris in September 1820, when François Arago demonstrated it at the Academy of Sciences. In the audience was André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836), a brilliant mathematician whose father had been executed by guillotine during the French Revolution. [6]

Within a week, Ampère had made a series of important discoveries. His reasoning: if a current pushes on a magnet, two currents should push on each other, with no magnet needed.

Using light, movable wire frames connected to batteries, he found that:

  • Two parallel wires carrying currents in the same direction attract each other.
  • Two parallel wires carrying currents in opposite directions repel each other.

To strengthen the effect, Ampère wound wire into a tight coil, which he called a solenoid (from a Greek word for "tube"). With a current flowing, the coil behaved exactly like a bar magnet, with a north and south pole. This is the principle of the electromagnet.

Ampère then made a bold suggestion: perhaps all magnets are really electric. A permanent magnet, he proposed, contains countless tiny electric currents circulating inside its particles. This was remarkably close to the modern explanation, which involves the motion and "spin" of electrons, discovered nearly a century later.

Ampère worked out the mathematics of the forces between currents, and around the same time Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart found a formula for the magnetic field produced by a current. Ampère's great book of 1826 led Maxwell later to call him "the Newton of electricity".

8. Magnets Make Electricity: Faraday

While French mathematicians wrote equations, a largely self-taught blacksmith's son was experimenting in the basement laboratory of the Royal Institution in London: Michael Faraday (1791–1867). Born poor and apprenticed at fourteen to a bookbinder, Faraday educated himself by reading the books he was binding, including popular science books such as Jane Marcet's Conversations on Chemistry. He later became Humphry Davy's assistant. [3, 7]

In 1821, fascinated by Ørsted's circular force, Faraday built the first electric motor: a wire hanging into a dish of mercury with a magnet in the middle. When current flowed, the wire spun round and round the magnet. Electricity had been turned into continuous motion.

If electricity can make magnetism, Faraday asked, can magnetism make electricity? For years people had put wires next to strong magnets and found nothing. Faraday realised what was missing: electricity is produced not by a magnet sitting still, but by change.

On 29 August 1831 he wound two separate coils of wire around an iron ring. When he connected the first coil to a battery, a meter attached to the second coil flicked briefly and then went back to zero. When he disconnected the battery, it flicked again, the other way. A steady current in the first coil did nothing; only switching it on or off, which changes the magnetism, produced a current in the second.

He confirmed this by pushing a bar magnet into a coil of wire connected to a meter. Pushing the magnet in made the needle move one way; pulling it out made it move the other way; holding it still inside the coil did nothing. The faster the magnetism through the coil changes, the stronger the effect. This is electromagnetic induction.

In 1834 the physicist Heinrich Lenz (1804–1865) added an important detail, now called Lenz's law: the induced current always flows in the direction that opposes the change causing it. This follows from conservation of energy: if it helped the change instead, you would get energy for nothing.

In the autumn of 1831 Faraday spun a copper disc between the poles of a big horseshoe magnet and drew a steady current from it: the first generator (dynamo). Every power station today, whether driven by water, steam or wind, works on the same principle: spinning a coil and a magnet relative to each other.

Faraday had little mathematics, so he thought in pictures. Sprinkling iron filings on paper over a magnet, he saw them line up in curves, which he called lines of force. He came to think of the space around magnets and currents not as empty, but as filled with these lines, stretched and pushing on each other. This was the beginning of one of the most important ideas in physics: the field.

9. Maxwell Puts Faraday's Ideas into Equations

To mathematicians trained in the French tradition, Faraday's lines of force looked like a quaint way of picturing things, not real physics. It took the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell (1831–1879) to see that Faraday's pictures were deeper than the old idea of forces acting instantly at a distance. [2, 7]

In 1856 Maxwell published On Faraday's Lines of Force, describing the lines with the mathematics of flowing fluids. In 1861–1862 he went further and built an imaginary mechanical model of space: tiny spinning whirlpools lined up along the magnetic lines, with even tinier "idle wheels" between them, like ball bearings, standing for electric charges.

The whirlpool model was later dropped, but it led Maxwell to a crucial discovery. Ampère's law, which describes how currents make magnetism, didn't work properly when things were changing, for example when charge builds up on the plates of a capacitor, where the current stops at a gap. To fix this, Maxwell added a new idea: a changing electric field also makes a magnetic field, just as a current does, even in completely empty space. He called it the "displacement current".

With this, electricity and magnetism became beautifully balanced:

  1. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field (Faraday's discovery).
  2. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field (Maxwell's addition).

Together with two simpler rules about electric charges and magnetic poles, these make up what we now call Maxwell's equations.

10. Light Turns Out to Be Electromagnetic

In 1865 Maxwell presented his complete theory, A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field, to the Royal Society. (He wrote it as twenty equations; Oliver Heaviside later boiled them down to the four used today.) Maxwell asked: what happens in empty space, with no charges or currents at all? [2]

The two rules above feed each other. A changing magnetic field makes a changing electric field, which makes a changing magnetic field, and so on. Maxwell showed that this chain reaction travels outward through space as a wave, and his equations gave its speed:

$$\text{speed} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \epsilon_0}}$$

Here $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are two numbers that describe how strong electric and magnetic forces are. They had been measured in the laboratory using charged plates and current-carrying wires, experiments that had nothing to do with light.

When Maxwell put in the measured values (from experiments by Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch in 1856), he got a speed of about 310,000 kilometres per second. That matched, within the errors of the time, the measured speed of light: Hippolyte Fizeau had found about 315,000 km/s and Léon Foucault about 298,000 km/s.

Maxwell had already drawn the conclusion in 1862, in one of the most famous passages in physics:

The velocity of transverse undulations in our hypothetical medium, calculated from the electro-magnetic experiments of MM. Kohlrausch and Weber, agrees so nearly with the velocity of light calculated from the optical experiments of M. Fizeau, that we can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena.

In other words, light is an electromagnetic wave: electric and magnetic fields making each other, travelling through space at about 300,000 kilometres per second. Optics, studied since Euclid and Ptolemy (Part 4), turned out to be part of electricity and magnetism.

11. Invisible Waves Across a Room: Hertz

Many physicists, especially in Germany, doubted Maxwell's theory. Maxwell died of cancer in 1879, aged 48, without seeing his waves detected. [8]

The proof came in 1887–1888 from the young German physicist Heinrich Hertz (1857–1894), working in Karlsruhe. Inspired by a prize question set by his former teacher Helmholtz, he set out to test Maxwell's ideas.

Hertz built a transmitter: two metal rods with a small gap between them, powered by a high-voltage coil. When a spark jumped across the gap, electricity surged back and forth along the rods extremely fast, many millions of times a second.

A few metres away, with no wires connecting them, he placed a receiver: a simple loop of wire with a tiny gap in it. When the transmitter sparked, Hertz, peering through a magnifying glass in a darkened room, saw a faint spark jump across the gap in the receiver. Something invisible had crossed the room.

Over the next two years he showed these invisible waves behave just like light. He focused them with large curved metal mirrors, reflected them off metal sheets, bent them with a huge prism made of pitch (weighing about half a tonne), and showed with a grid of wires that they were polarised, like light. By reflecting them off a metal wall to make standing waves, he measured their wavelength, and combined with their frequency, this gave a speed close to the speed of light. (The waves in his mirror and prism experiments were about 66 cm long.)

Asked by his students what his discovery was good for, Hertz is said to have replied that it was of no use at all; it just showed Maxwell was right. Yet within a few years Guglielmo Marconi, Oliver Lodge and Jagadish Chandra Bose were using these waves for wireless signalling. Every radio, phone, Wi-Fi network and GPS signal today uses what we now call radio waves, and the unit of frequency, the hertz, is named after him.

12. Why Time Has a Direction: Boltzmann and Probability

While electromagnetism was revealing the role of fields, another group of physicists was explaining heat in terms of atoms and molecules. In 1859 Maxwell used probability to describe the speeds of the countless molecules in a gas, bouncing off each other at random. He showed that at any temperature there is a typical spread of speeds: a few slow molecules, many medium-speed ones and a few very fast ones (now called the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution). [10]

The Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann (1844–1906) devoted his life to a deep puzzle. Newton's laws work equally well forwards or backwards in time: a film of two billiard balls colliding looks fine either way. So if every molecule obeys Newton's laws, where does the one-way arrow of time in the second law come from?

In 1877 Boltzmann gave his answer, based on counting. A gas with a certain temperature and pressure can be made up of an enormous number of different detailed arrangements of its molecules (where each one is and how fast it is moving). He called these arrangements microstates.

Imagine a box with a divider, with all the gas molecules in the left half. Now remove the divider. The molecules spread out to fill the whole box. Why don't they ever all gather back on the left? Because there are vastly more ways for them to be spread across the whole box than for them all to be on one side. For 100 molecules, there is only 1 way to have all of them on the left, but about 1029 ways to have them split roughly half and half.

For a real amount of gas, with something like 1023 molecules, the odds against all of them returning to one side are so overwhelming that it would never be seen, not even once, in many times the age of the universe.

Boltzmann linked entropy to this counting. In the form Max Planck gave it in 1900, later carved on Boltzmann's gravestone:

$$S = k \log W$$

Here $W$ is the number of microstates and $k$ is a constant now named after Boltzmann. Entropy is a measure of how many ways a situation can happen. Things don't move towards higher entropy because of some special force; they move that way simply because disordered arrangements vastly outnumber ordered ones. The arrow of time isn't an absolute law of mechanics; it is an overwhelming matter of probability.

13. How Did They Know? Joule's Careful Measurements

How did they know? Measuring a tiny temperature rise

How did Joule convince doubtful scientists that falling weights produce a definite amount of heat? [4]

It was hard. The temperature rise in his experiments was tiny, usually less than half a degree Fahrenheit (about a quarter of a degree Celsius). To get a reliable answer, Joule had to deal with four sources of error:

  1. Heat leaking in or out: the experiment took about 45 minutes, and the room's temperature could change. Joule stood the container on a wooden stand that barely touched it, put a screen between it and his body, and measured how fast it warmed or cooled before and after each run, so he could correct for leaks.
  2. Friction in the pulleys and cords: some of the falling weights' energy was used up in the pulleys rather than turning the paddles. Joule measured this separately, by finding how much weight was needed just to overcome the pulley friction, and subtracted it.
  3. The weights hitting the floor: the weights were still moving slightly when they landed, carrying a little energy that never reached the water. He calculated this and subtracted it too.
  4. Thermometer accuracy: thermometers of the time often had uneven bores. Joule had his specially calibrated so that differences of about a two-hundredth of a degree Fahrenheit could be read reliably.

By accounting for every bit of energy going in and out, Joule showed that the ratio of work to heat stayed the same over hundreds of trials, whether the liquid was water, oil or mercury. Careful attention to errors turned a bold idea into an exact law.

14. Legend and Record: Galvani's Frogs and Faraday's Missing Maths

Legend and Record: when being "wrong" was partly right

Some of the great discoveries of the 1800s came from people who were partly wrong, or who lacked what seemed like essential skills. [6, 3]

Galvani versus Volta: who was right? Textbooks often present Volta as the winner who showed that Galvani's "animal electricity" was a mistake. The truth is that both were partly right. Volta was correct that his battery made electricity from two metals, with no animal needed. But Galvani was also correct that living things make their own electricity: our nerves and muscles really do work by electrical signals. In 1794 Galvani showed that touching a frog's nerve directly to a muscle, with no metal at all, could make the muscle twitch, which is real biological electricity.

Faraday's lack of mathematics: some of Faraday's contemporaries looked down on him for his lack of mathematical training; he used hardly any algebra. Yet historians now think this may have helped him. Mathematicians of the time were so used to the idea of forces acting in straight lines at a distance that they found it hard to imagine anything else. Faraday, thinking in pictures, could imagine lines of force filling space, an idea that mathematics only later learned to describe.

15. Philosophy Corner: Is "Empty" Space Really Empty?

Philosophy Corner: from action at a distance to fields

Newton himself was uncomfortable with gravity acting across empty space. In a famous letter of 1693 to Richard Bentley, he wrote that the idea of one lump of matter acting on another at a distance, through a vacuum, with nothing in between, seemed to him so absurd that no one who thought carefully could believe it. [7]

The field theory of the 1800s changed the picture. In Maxwell's physics, the basic ingredients of the world aren't just particles moving through empty space. The field itself is real.

A field isn't empty space; it is a condition of space that carries energy and momentum. When an electric charge is shaken, it doesn't instantly affect a distant charge. It disturbs the field around it, and the disturbance travels outward as a wave at the speed of light. While the wave is on its way, its energy is neither in the sender nor in the receiver; it is in the field. (That is how sunlight carries energy to Earth.)

This idea prepared the way for the physics of the 20th century, relativity and quantum theory (Part 10), in which space and time themselves become active parts of physics rather than an empty stage.
