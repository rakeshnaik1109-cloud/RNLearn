From Alchemy to the Periodic Table: The Making of Chemistry

How human curiosity transformed the occult art of transmutation into the exact science of matter: from medieval Islamic distillation and Boyle’s sceptical corpuscles to Lavoisier’s balance, Dalton’s atomic weights, and Mendeleev’s prophetic table.

1. Craftsmen and Alchemists

Long before chemistry was a school subject full of equations and atomic numbers, there were two separate traditions: the hands-on know-how of craftsmen, and the mystical quest of the alchemists. [3]

The craftsmen worked in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, India and China. They smelted copper with tin to make bronze, got purple dye from sea snails, melted sand with soda to make glass, and soaked fragrant resins in oil to make perfume. They knew a huge amount of practical chemistry: which additives made metal melt more easily, which earths cleaned wool, which vinegars ate into metal. But they had no general theory of what matter is.

In Alexandria in Egypt, in the first centuries CE, this craft knowledge mixed with Greek philosophy and mystical religion to produce alchemy (from the Arabic al-kīmiyā'). Thinkers such as Zosimos of Panopolis (around 300 CE) sought transmutation: turning ordinary metals such as lead, iron and copper into silver and gold. This wasn't as silly as it sounds. In Aristotle's theory, all metals were the same basic stuff with different surface qualities such as colour and hardness. Change the qualities with fire and special substances, and lead might become gold.

In the 8th to 10th centuries, scholars in the Islamic world reshaped alchemy. The great name is Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, traditionally placed in the 8th century, though the large collection of writings under his name was probably written by several authors in the 9th and 10th centuries. These writings taught that all metals form deep in the Earth from two ingredients: "sulfur" (the principle of burning and oily heat) and "mercury" (the principle of liquid flow and metallic shine). Gold was the perfect balance of the two; other metals had too much impure sulfur or too little mercury.

To work with substances, Islamic alchemists improved and spread laboratory equipment, much of it inherited from Alexandria. The most famous is the alembic (al-inbīq): a still-head placed on top of a boiling flask, which catches the vapour and lets it cool and drip into a separate container. The doctor al-Rāzī described laboratory equipment and sorted substances into groups with new clarity. Strong acids (sulfuric acid, nitric acid and "aqua regia", which dissolves gold) are first clearly described later, in Latin books of the 1200s written under the name "Geber", and distilling strong alcohol from wine spread through Europe in the 1100s. The chemistry laboratory was taking shape.

2. Paracelsus: Medicine as Chemistry

In the early 1500s a loud, quarrelsome Swiss doctor who called himself Paracelsus (1493–1541) shook up medicine. He is said to have publicly burned a book by the revered Avicenna at Basel, and he declared that health wasn't about balancing Galen's four body fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile), but about chemistry. [2, 3]

Paracelsus replaced the four Greek elements with three "principles" of his own, the tria prima:

  • Sulfur: what makes things burn; linked with oiliness, warmth and soul.
  • Mercury: what makes things flow and rise as smoke; linked with spirit.
  • Salt: what stays solid, like ash and crystals; linked with the body.

He liked to illustrate this with burning wood: the smoke was mercury, the flame was sulfur and the ash left behind was salt. Everything, he argued, was a mixture of these three.

Paracelsus turned alchemy away from making gold and towards making medicines, a field called iatrochemistry ("chemical medicine"). He saw the body as a kind of chemical works, and illness as a breakdown in separating good nourishment from poisons. Instead of gentle herbal remedies he used carefully measured doses of strong chemicals: mercury for syphilis, antimony and opium tinctures. He also gave us the basic rule of toxicology: "All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; the dose alone makes it not a poison."

3. Robert Boyle: What Is an Element?

By the mid-1600s, chemistry was buried under fancy, symbolic language. Alchemists wrote about "the green lion devouring the Sun" and "the marriage of the Red King and the White Queen". [8, 2]

In 1661 the Anglo-Irish scientist Robert Boyle (1627–1691), a founding member of the Royal Society, published The Sceptical Chymist. Written as a polite conversation between a doubter and supporters of Aristotle and Paracelsus, it attacked both the four elements and the three principles.

Boyle asked awkward questions. How can fire be an element when it seems to be just a kind of motion? Why do so many substances, when heated, give off nothing like sulfur, salt or mercury? Gold can be mixed with other metals, dissolved in acid and brought back again, unchanged. It didn't behave like a mixture of shifting principles.

Boyle described what a true element would have to be, though he did it mainly to doubt that any known substance fitted:

"I mean by elements... certain primitive and simple, or perfectly unmingled bodies; which not being made of any other bodies, or of one another, are the ingredients of which all those called perfectly mixt bodies are immediately compounded, and into which they are ultimately resolved."
— Robert Boyle, The Sceptical Chymist (1661)

Boyle's demand that claims about elements be tested in the laboratory prepared the way for Lavoisier's practical definition a century later: an element is a substance that nobody has yet managed to break down into anything simpler. Boyle also pictured matter as made of tiny particles whose shapes, sizes and movements produce the colours, tastes and textures we notice. Chemistry was starting to become a modern science.

4. What Is Fire? The Phlogiston Theory

One old mystery still puzzled everyone in the 1700s: burning. What really happens when wood burns to ash, a candle disappears into the air, or iron rusts? [1, 2]

Around 1700 the German chemists Johann Joachim Becher and Georg Ernst Stahl came up with a theory that dominated European chemistry for about seventy-five years: the phlogiston theory (from a Greek word meaning "burnt" or "inflammable").

Stahl said that everything that can burn (wood, charcoal, sulfur, oil, metals) contains an invisible fiery substance called phlogiston. When a candle burns, it gives off its phlogiston into the air. When a candle goes out under a glass jar, it's because the air in the jar has soaked up all the phlogiston it can hold.

The theory worked surprisingly well. It explained metal-making neatly: a metal was "calx" (a kind of earthy ash, what we now call an ore or oxide) plus phlogiston. Heat iron ore with charcoal, which is full of phlogiston, and the charcoal passes its phlogiston to the ore, turning it back into iron. It hung together as a system.

But there was a serious problem with weight. When wood burns, the ash weighs much less than the log, which fits the idea that something escapes. But when a metal such as lead, zinc or tin is heated in air until it turns into calx, the calx weighs more than the metal did.

If burning means losing phlogiston, how can something get heavier by losing part of itself? Supporters of the theory tried more and more strained explanations. Some said phlogiston had "negative weight", lifting things up like a float, so losing it made a metal heavier. Others said particles of fire soaked into the calx. The theory was starting to crack.

5. Many Kinds of Air

What finally overturned phlogiston was the study of gases, then called "airs". For centuries people assumed that air was one single substance. In the mid-1700s, a handful of British experimenters showed it was nothing of the sort. [1, 2]

In Edinburgh in 1756 the Scottish doctor Joseph Black (1728–1799) found that when chalk is heated it loses weight and gives off a heavy, colourless gas that puts out candles. Bubbled through clear limewater, this gas turned it milky. Black called it fixed air, because it had been "fixed" (locked up) inside the solid chalk. We call it carbon dioxide.

Ten years later in London, the shy, wealthy scientist Henry Cavendish (1731–1810) poured acid on zinc and iron filings and collected a very light gas that exploded with a pop when lit. He called it inflammable air; we call it hydrogen. In 1781 he found something astonishing: when this gas burned in ordinary air, drops of pure water formed on the sides of the vessel. Water, which Thales had thought was the basic stuff of everything (Part 3), wasn't an element at all; it could be made by burning hydrogen.

In 1774 the English minister and scientist Joseph Priestley (1733–1804) used a large magnifying glass to focus sunlight on a red powder, the calx of mercury (what we call mercury oxide), inside a glass tube. The powder broke down and gave off a new gas. A candle in this gas burned with a dazzling flame, glowing embers burst into flame, and a mouse in a jar of it lived about twice as long as in ordinary air. Breathing it himself, Priestley said it felt "light and easy" in his chest.

The Swedish pharmacist Carl Wilhelm Scheele had made the same gas a year or two earlier (he called it "fire air"), but his book came out only in 1777. Priestley, who stuck with the old theory all his life, called it dephlogisticated air: air with no phlogiston in it, which was why it could soak up phlogiston from burning things so eagerly. We call it oxygen. In October 1774 Priestley visited Paris and told the chemist Antoine Lavoisier about his new gas over dinner. Lavoisier saw at once that it might be the key to the whole puzzle of burning.

6. Lavoisier Balances the Books

Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier (1743–1794) was a member of the French Academy of Sciences, an investor in the private company that collected taxes for the king, and head of the national gunpowder office. In his well-equipped laboratory at the Paris Arsenal he worked with his wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier (1758–1836), who drew the diagrams of the equipment, translated English scientific papers and kept careful records of the experiments. Lavoisier brought an accountant's mind to chemistry. [6, 10]

His key tool was an extremely sensitive balance, made by the instrument maker Nicolas Fortin. His key rule was simple: in every chemical change, the total weight of what you start with must equal the total weight of what you end up with. Nothing appears from nowhere and nothing vanishes.

Combining Priestley's discovery with careful experiments of his own in the 1770s, in which he weighed everything and measured every volume of gas (including his famous twelve-day mercury experiment, section 13), Lavoisier showed that burning is not losing phlogiston but combining with oxygen. When mercury was heated for days in a closed jar of air, it took up about a sixth of the air (about 8 of 50 cubic inches) and turned into a red crust. The gas left behind put out flames and suffocated animals; he called it azote, and we call it nitrogen. The red crust was a compound: mercury plus oxygen. Heated more strongly, it broke down again into mercury and released the same amount of oxygen.

In 1789, the year the French Revolution began, Lavoisier published his great textbook, the Elementary Treatise on Chemistry. In it he stated what we call the law of conservation of mass:

"Nothing is created, either in the operations of art or in nature, and, as a general principle, in every operation an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the operation; the quality and quantity of the constituents remain precisely the same, and what occurs is merely changes and modifications."
— Antoine-Laurent Lavoisier, Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789)

He also included the first modern list of chemical elements: 33 substances that couldn't be broken down further, including oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, carbon and seventeen metals. (It also included light and "caloric", a supposed fluid of heat, which later turned out not to be substances at all.) Phlogiston was gone for good.

7. New Names for a New Chemistry

Lavoisier believed a scientific revolution needs a clear language. In 1787, with Guyton de Morveau, Claude-Louis Berthollet and Antoine François de Fourcroy, he published a new system for naming chemicals. [4]

Before this, chemical names were a jumble left over from alchemy. Substances were named after people (Glauber's salt), after how they looked ("butter of antimony", "flowers of zinc", "liver of sulfur"), after the planets ("lunar caustic" for silver nitrate, because silver was linked with the Moon), or with old medical terms ("spirit of salt" for hydrochloric acid, "oil of vitriol" for sulfuric acid).

Following the philosopher Condillac, who said that a science is really just a well-made language, Lavoisier's team started again. Each name would say what the substance is made of:

  • The new gas was named oxygen, Greek for "acid-maker", because Lavoisier believed (wrongly, as it turned out) that every acid contains it.
  • Compounds of oxygen with metals became oxides: iron oxide, zinc oxide, copper oxide.
  • Acids were named after their main element and how much oxygen they contain: sulfuric acid has more oxygen than sulfurous acid.
  • Salts made from these acids got matching names: sulfuric acid with copper gives copper sulfate; sulfurous acid gives copper sulfite.

Within about ten years this system was used across Europe, and it is the basis of the names chemists still use. Speaking Lavoisier's language made young chemists think in terms of what things are made of, and how much.

8. Atoms Return: John Dalton

Lavoisier had shown that mass is conserved and that there are simple elements, but he didn't explain why elements always combine in fixed proportions by weight. That step was taken in Manchester by a quiet Quaker teacher, John Dalton (1766–1844). [9]

Dalton started out studying the weather and the gases in the air. Around 1803 he revived the ancient Greek idea of atoms (Part 3), but turned it from a philosophical guess into a theory you could test with measurements.

In his book A New System of Chemical Philosophy (1808), Dalton set out his main ideas:

  1. All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms, which can't be split or destroyed.
  2. All atoms of one element are identical, with the same weight; atoms of different elements have different weights.
  3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements join in simple whole-number ratios, such as 1 to 1, 1 to 2 or 2 to 3.
  4. Chemical reactions just rearrange atoms; atoms are never created, destroyed or changed into other kinds of atom.

His strongest evidence was the law of multiple proportions: when two elements can combine in more than one way, the amounts of one that combine with a fixed amount of the other are in simple whole-number ratios.

For example, carbon and oxygen form two gases: carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO₂). For 12 grams of carbon, carbon monoxide contains 16 grams of oxygen and carbon dioxide contains 32 grams, exactly twice as much. There's no compound with 24 grams, or 19.3 grams. Elements combine in whole steps, not in any amount you like, which is just what you would expect if they come in separate units: atoms.

9. A Clue from Gas Volumes: Avogadro

While Dalton was weighing things in Manchester, French chemists were measuring the volumes of gases as they react. In 1808 Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1778–1850) found that when gases react, their volumes (measured at the same temperature and pressure) are always in simple whole-number ratios. [9, 1] For example:

$$\text{2 volumes of hydrogen} + \text{1 volume of oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{2 volumes of steam}$$ $$\text{1 volume of nitrogen} + \text{3 volumes of hydrogen} \longrightarrow \text{2 volumes of ammonia}$$

This looked like great support for atoms. Yet Dalton rejected it. His reasoning: if equal volumes of gas contain equal numbers of atoms, then (with Dalton's formula for water, one hydrogen to one oxygen) a volume of oxygen, say 100 atoms, should make 100 particles of steam, filling only one volume. If it really makes two volumes, 200 particles, then each oxygen atom would have to split in half to share itself between two of them. And atoms couldn't split.

The puzzle was solved in 1811 by the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro (1776–1856) in Turin. He made a crucial distinction between an atom (the smallest piece of an element) and a molecule (the separate particles that actually move around in a gas, which can be made of several atoms joined together).

Avogadro proposed two ideas:

  1. Avogadro's hypothesis: equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of molecules.
  2. Gases like hydrogen and oxygen come in pairs: their molecules are not single atoms but two atoms joined together: H₂, O₂, N₂.

Now everything fits. Two volumes of H₂ molecules react with one volume of O₂ molecules. Each O₂ molecule comes apart into its two atoms, and each oxygen atom joins two hydrogen atoms, making two volumes of water molecules: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. No atom is split; only the pair of oxygen atoms comes apart.

Sadly, Avogadro's paper was ignored or misunderstood for nearly fifty years, and chemists spent decades arguing over atomic weights and formulas.

10. Splitting Compounds with Electricity: Davy and Berzelius

In 1800 physics gave chemistry a powerful new tool: the electric battery, invented by Alessandro Volta. [1, 2]

At the Royal Institution in London, the brilliant chemist Humphry Davy (1778–1829) realised electricity could pull apart compounds that nothing else could. In October 1807 he built a huge battery from hundreds of copper and zinc plates and passed its current through a slightly damp lump of caustic potash (potassium hydroxide).

As the current flowed, bubbles of oxygen appeared at one wire, and at the other, tiny shiny beads of metal formed. Some burst into flame and skittered across the surface with a lilac glow. According to his cousin, Davy danced around the room with delight. He had discovered the element potassium. A few days later he did the same with caustic soda and found sodium. In 1808 he freed four more metals: calcium, magnesium, strontium and barium.

Davy's work suggested that whatever holds compounds together is electrical. In Sweden, Jöns Jacob Berzelius (1779–1848) built this into a theory: every compound is made of a positively charged part joined to a negatively charged part, such as a metal joined to oxygen.

Berzelius also gave chemistry its modern symbols. Instead of Dalton's circles with dots and lines, he used the first letter of each element's Latin name, adding a second letter when two names clashed: H, C, N, O, Fe (iron, ferrum), Cu (copper, cuprum), Ag (silver, argentum), Au (gold, aurum). Over twenty years he analysed some 2,000 compounds and measured the atomic weights of more than forty elements remarkably accurately.

11. Sorting Out the Confusion: The Karlsruhe Meeting

By the 1850s chemistry was in a muddle. Chemists couldn't agree on how heavy each atom was, so different laboratories wrote different formulas for the same substances. [9, 7]

For water, Dalton wrote HO (taking oxygen as 8 times as heavy as hydrogen), Berzelius wrote H₂O (oxygen 16), and others wrote other things. The chemist August Kekulé found nineteen different formulas for acetic acid (the acid in vinegar) in use. A student in Paris might not understand a chemistry book from Heidelberg or London.

To end the confusion, Kekulé and others organised the first international meeting of chemists: the Karlsruhe Congress, held in Germany in September 1860. More than 140 chemists came from all over Europe.

After three days of argument, the meeting seemed to have failed. Then, as people were leaving, the Italian chemist Stanislao Cannizzaro (1826–1910) had a friend hand out copies of a short paper he had written explaining his chemistry course.

In it, Cannizzaro brought back Avogadro's forgotten idea from 1811. He showed that if you weigh equal volumes of different gases, Avogadro's hypothesis gives you a clear, reliable way to work out the true weights of molecules and atoms, with no guesswork. Carbon was 12, oxygen 16, hydrogen 1, and water was definitely H₂O.

Delegates read it on the way home. The German chemist Lothar Meyer later wrote: "It was as though a veil fell from my eyes; doubts vanished, and a feeling of quiet certainty took their place." With reliable atomic weights at last, the periodic table could be built.

12. Mendeleev's Periodic Table

With trustworthy atomic weights, several chemists noticed patterns. In England, John Newlands found that if you list the elements in order of weight, every eighth one seems similar, like the notes of a musical scale (his "law of octaves", 1865). But one member of the Chemical Society asked him sarcastically whether he had tried listing them alphabetically. [7]

The breakthrough came in St Petersburg in February 1869, from the Russian professor Dmitri Mendeleev (1834–1907). While writing a chemistry textbook, he was looking for a sensible order in which to teach the 63 elements then known.

Mendeleev wrote each element's name, atomic weight and properties on a separate card. (A popular legend says he saw the final arrangement in a dream.) He found that when the elements are put in order of atomic weight, their properties repeat at regular intervals. "Periodic" means "repeating".

He laid the cards out in rows (periods) and columns (groups). The reactive soft metals lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium and caesium fell in one column; fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine in another. What made Mendeleev stand out was his boldness:

  • He trusted the pattern over the measurements. Where an element's measured weight put it in the wrong family, Mendeleev moved it anyway. He put tellurium (weight 128) before iodine (weight 127), so that iodine sat with the similar elements chlorine and bromine. He suspected tellurium's weight had been measured wrongly. It hadn't: tellurium really is heavier. But his order was right, and the reason was found in 1913 when Henry Moseley showed that the true order is by atomic number (the number of protons in the nucleus), not by weight.
  • He left gaps. Where no known element fitted, he left a space, saying an element there simply hadn't been found yet.
  • He made predictions. In 1871 he predicted the atomic weights, densities and other properties of three missing elements, which he called eka-boron, eka-aluminium and eka-silicon ("eka" is Sanskrit for "one": one place below boron, aluminium and silicon).

He was proved right within fifteen years:

  1. In 1875 the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered gallium. It matched eka-aluminium remarkably well: predicted weight 68, actual 69.7; predicted density 5.9, actual 5.91; predicted to melt easily, and it actually melts at 29.8 °C, in the warmth of your hand.
  2. In 1879 Lars Fredrik Nilson in Sweden discovered scandium (eka-boron).
  3. In 1886 Clemens Winkler in Germany discovered germanium (eka-silicon).

The periodic table turned chemistry from a huge collection of separate facts into an organised science that could predict things. The elements weren't a random collection; they formed a family with a clear pattern.

13. How Did They Know? Lavoisier's Twelve-Day Experiment

How did they know? Lavoisier's closed-jar experiment with mercury

How did Lavoisier show that burning means combining with part of the air, rather than giving off phlogiston? [10, 6]

He set up his equipment so that nothing could get in or out:

  1. The flask: he put 4 ounces of pure mercury in a glass flask whose long, curved neck ran under a jar standing upside down in a dish of mercury.
  2. The trapped air: the total amount of air shut inside the flask and the jar was carefully measured: 50 cubic inches.
  3. Twelve days of heat: the flask sat on a furnace, kept just below the boiling point of mercury (about 350 °C), for twelve days and nights. At first nothing seemed to happen. On the second day small red specks appeared on the mercury, and over the following days they grew into a red crust.
  4. The air shrinks: as the red crust grew, the mercury in the upside-down jar crept up, showing that some of the trapped air was being used up. When it stopped and everything had cooled, the air had shrunk from 50 to 42 cubic inches, a loss of 8, about a sixth. (Air is actually about a fifth oxygen; not all of it was used up.)
  5. Testing what was left: the remaining 42 cubic inches put out candles and suffocated animals. It was mostly nitrogen.
  6. Running it backwards: Lavoisier collected the red crust, 45 grains of it, and heated it more strongly in a small flask. It broke down into shiny mercury and gave off about 8 cubic inches of oxygen, in which things burned brightly and animals could breathe.

The two halves matched: 50 − 8 = 42 on the way in, and 42 + 8 = 50 on the way back. Nothing was lost; burning was a combination with oxygen.

14. Legend and Record: Was Alchemy Just Fraud and Magic?

Legend and Record: what alchemists really did

Popular culture often shows alchemy as pure trickery: con men faking gold to fool kings, or sorcerers chanting over bubbling pots. [3, 8]

What the evidence shows: Historians of science such as Lawrence Principe and William Newman have shown that alchemy (or "chymistry", as it was often spelled before 1700) was a serious practical pursuit:

  • Alchemists built the first laboratories. They developed furnaces that could hold steady high temperatures, the water bath (still called a bain-marie in kitchens), stills, flasks and crucibles, and ways of purifying substances by crystallising them. Modern chemistry inherited its laboratory from them.
  • Great scientists were alchemists. Robert Boyle, author of The Sceptical Chymist, spent decades on secret experiments trying to transmute metals, and in 1689 persuaded Parliament to repeal an old law against "multiplying" gold. Isaac Newton spent years on alchemical experiments in Cambridge and left about a million words of notes on the subject.
  • Transmutation made sense at the time. If all matter is made of the same basic particles in different arrangements, rearranging lead into gold seemed no stranger than turning ice into water. And in the 20th century, nuclear physics showed that turning one element into another really is possible: tiny amounts of gold have been made from mercury in nuclear reactors and from bismuth in particle accelerators, though at a cost far greater than the gold is worth.

15. Philosophy Corner: Is Chemistry Just Physics?

Philosophy Corner: can chemistry be reduced to physics?

In 1929 the British physicist Paul Dirac made a famous claim that philosophers still argue about: [7]

"The underlying physical laws necessary for the mathematical theory of a large part of physics and the whole of chemistry are thus completely known, and the difficulty is only that the exact application of these laws leads to equations much too complicated to be soluble."

This is reductionism: the idea that chemistry is really just complicated physics. In principle, every chemical reaction and every molecule is described by the equations of quantum physics; it's only that the sums are too hard.

But many chemists and philosophers of chemistry, such as Eric Scerri and Roald Hoffmann, push back:

  • Some properties only appear in groups. A single water molecule isn't wet. Wetness only appears when huge numbers of water molecules interact. You won't find the smell of vanilla or the poisonousness of cyanide by looking at one electron. Properties like these are called emergent.
  • The chemical bond is a useful idea, even if physics blurs it. In quantum physics, electrons are spread out in fuzzy clouds; there are no little sticks joining atoms. Yet the chemist's picture of bonds between atoms (developed by G. N. Lewis and Linus Pauling) is one of the most useful tools in science. Without ideas like bonds and molecular shape, modern medicine and biology couldn't work.

On this view, chemistry is a science in its own right: a bridge between the strange quantum world of electrons and the everyday world of materials and living things.
