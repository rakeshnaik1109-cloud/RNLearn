Marie Curie and Ernst Mach: Are Atoms Real?

One of the century’s great physicists doubted that atoms exist; a Polish woman in a leaking Paris shed found atoms that change into other atoms. Within fifteen years, the invisible had become countable.

1. Are Atoms Real?

At the end of the nineteenth century, chemists used atoms every day to explain how elements combine, and physicists such as Ludwig Boltzmann explained heat as the motion of molecules. Yet no one had seen an atom, and a powerful school of scientists and philosophers argued that atoms were at best a convenient fiction. Its most influential voice was the Austrian physicist Ernst Mach, who insisted that science should describe only what can be observed. [1]

At the same time, in a leaking shed in Paris, Marie Curie was discovering that some atoms are not eternal at all: they emit energy and transform into other elements. Within about fifteen years, radioactivity and the measurement of Brownian motion turned atoms from a hypothesis into a fact. This part pairs the sceptic and the discoverer to ask when we should believe in things we cannot see. [2]

3. Ernst Mach: Physicist and Philosopher

Ernst Mach was born in 1838 in Moravia, then part of the Austrian Empire, and became a professor of physics at Graz, then for nearly thirty years at Prague, where he was rector of the university, and finally a professor of philosophy at Vienna. He worked on acoustics, optics, the physiology of the senses (he discovered the visual illusion now called Mach bands) and the history of science. [1] His philosophy, which influenced the Vienna Circle and young Einstein, held that science is an economical description of the relations among our sensations. Concepts that go beyond possible experience, like Newton’s absolute space or invisible atoms, should be treated with suspicion.

4. Mach’s Critique of Mechanics

In The Science of Mechanics (1883), Mach traced the historical development of mechanics and attacked its metaphysical baggage. [3] Newton had argued for absolute space with his spinning bucket: the water’s concave surface reveals real rotation (Part 6). Mach replied that we have only ever observed rotation relative to the mass of the rest of the universe, the “fixed stars”. Perhaps if the bucket’s walls were several miles thick, they would make the water curve; perhaps inertia itself is produced by distant matter. Einstein, who read Mach as a student, called this idea Mach’s principle and was inspired by it in creating general relativity (Part 10), though his final theory did not fully embody it. [4]

Mach applied the same scepticism to atoms. Physics should relate measurable quantities such as pressure, temperature and volume; assuming invisible particles added nothing that could be checked. When others spoke of atoms, he is said to have asked: “Have you seen one?”

5. Making the Invisible Visible: Shock Waves

Ironically, one of Mach’s greatest experimental achievements was to photograph something no one had seen. With Peter Salcher in 1887, he used an electric spark, triggered by the bullet itself, to photograph a supersonic bullet, revealing the cone of shock waves trailing behind it. [5] They showed that a shock forms only when the bullet travels faster than sound, and that the cone’s angle depends on the ratio of the two speeds. That ratio is now called the Mach number.

6. Maria Skłodowska: From Warsaw to Paris

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, in a part of Poland ruled by Russia, where higher education for women was forbidden. Her parents were teachers impoverished by their patriotism. She studied secretly at the clandestine “Flying University”, worked for years as a governess to support her sister’s medical studies in Paris, and in 1891 followed her to the Sorbonne, living in a freezing attic and sometimes fainting from hunger. She graduated first in physics in 1893 and second in mathematics in 1894. In 1895 she married Pierre Curie, a gifted physicist who had discovered piezoelectricity with his brother Jacques. [2]

7. Radioactivity and Two New Elements

In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium salts emit invisible rays that fog photographic plates. [6] For her doctoral research, Marie Curie measured these rays precisely, using the weak electric current they produced in air and an electrometer built by Pierre and Jacques. She found that the intensity depended only on the amount of uranium present, regardless of its chemical compound, its temperature or whether it was lit. The radiation, she concluded, was a property of the atoms themselves. She found that thorium also emits it, and coined the word radioactivity. [7]

Then came the decisive clue. Pitchblende, a uranium ore, was several times more radioactive than its uranium content could explain. Something much more active must be hidden in it. Pierre abandoned his own research to join her, and in 1898 they announced two new elements: polonium, named after her occupied homeland, in July, and radium in December. [8, 9] Chemists would believe in new elements only when they could be isolated and weighed. Working in a converted shed, Marie processed tonnes of pitchblende residue, stirring boiling vats with an iron rod, and by 1902 had isolated a tenth of a gram of radium chloride and measured radium’s atomic weight. [2]

8. A Nobel Prize, and a Tragedy

In 1903 the Nobel Prize in Physics was shared by Becquerel and the Curies for their work on radioactivity; the nomination had at first left out Marie, and Pierre insisted that she be included. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize. Meanwhile Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy, in Montreal, showed that radioactive atoms transform into atoms of other elements at characteristic rates, each with its own half-life, overturning the ancient idea that elements are unchangeable. [10] Radioactivity also released enormous energy: a gram of radium gave out heat continuously, enough to warm itself above its surroundings. This solved Kelvin’s objection to Darwin (Part 8): the Earth was kept warm from within, and radioactive decay soon provided a clock for dating rocks billions of years old.

In April 1906 Pierre was killed when he slipped in the street and was run over by a horse-drawn wagon. Marie took over his professorship, becoming the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. [2]

9. Marie Curie’s Later Life

In 1911 Marie Curie won a second Nobel Prize, in chemistry, for the discovery of radium and polonium, the only person to win in two different sciences. The same year, a press scandal over her relationship with the physicist Paul Langevin, a married man, led a member of the Nobel committee to suggest that she stay away from the ceremony; she went anyway, saying the prize was for her science, not her private life. During the First World War she organised mobile X-ray units, the “petites Curies”, and trained operators, helping surgeons find bullets and shrapnel in perhaps a million wounded soldiers. [2] She founded the Radium Institute in Paris, where her daughter Irène and son-in-law Frédéric Joliot discovered artificial radioactivity, winning their own Nobel Prize in 1935. She died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, almost certainly caused by radiation; her notebooks are still too radioactive to handle without protection.

10. Counting Molecules: Einstein and Perrin

Radioactivity showed atoms transforming and, by counting individual flashes of alpha particles on a screen, let Rutherford count them one by one. But the decisive argument for most sceptics came from a humbler phenomenon. Particles of pollen debris or dust suspended in water jiggle endlessly, as the botanist Robert Brown had observed in 1827. In 1905 Einstein showed that if water consists of molecules in thermal motion, such particles should be knocked about so that the average square of their displacement grows in proportion to time, at a rate that depends on Avogadro’s number, the number of molecules in a mole. [11]

From 1908 the French physicist Jean Perrin tested this with exquisite care, preparing emulsions of resin grains of uniform, measured size. He tracked their wandering under a microscope, and he counted how their concentration decreased with height as they settled, a miniature atmosphere. Both gave the same Avogadro’s number, about 6–7 × 10²³, and so did a dozen other methods, from the blue of the sky to radioactivity. In his book Les Atomes (1913), Perrin argued that this agreement of independent methods could not be a coincidence. [12, 13] He received the Nobel Prize in 1926. Even Wilhelm Ostwald, a leading opponent of atoms, announced in 1909 that he was convinced.

11. Boltzmann, Mach and the Atomic Debate

The debate was not only scientific. Ludwig Boltzmann, who had built the statistical theory of heat on atoms, felt increasingly isolated against the “energeticists” and Mach’s followers, though historians argue about how much this contributed to his depression. He took his own life in 1906, just before Perrin’s experiments vindicated him. [14] Mach himself never fully accepted atoms. Yet his scepticism was not foolish: it demanded that hypotheses earn their place by connecting to observation, and that demand was met, not by philosophy, but by experiments that made the atom’s effects countable.

12. Two Legacies

Mach died in 1916. His positivism shaped the logical empiricism of the Vienna Circle in the 1920s, which named its society after him, and his insistence that every concept be tied to measurement helped Einstein rethink simultaneity (Part 10) and the young Heisenberg build quantum mechanics from observable quantities. Marie Curie’s discoveries opened nuclear physics, radiotherapy for cancer and, ultimately, nuclear energy and weapons. Her example also mattered: in an age that barred women from most of science, she became the most famous scientist in the world.

13. When Should We Believe in the Unseen?

Atoms became real for scientists not because someone finally saw one (that came only with the scanning tunnelling microscope in the 1980s) but because many independent lines of evidence, each relying on different assumptions, gave the same numbers. Philosophers call such reasoning inference to the best explanation or an argument from convergence. It is how we come to accept many unobservable entities, from electrons and genes to dark matter, and why sceptics like Mach are useful: they force the evidence to become overwhelming.

14. Try It Yourself: Seeing the Invisible

Try it yourself: chance, jiggling and shock waves
  1. Decay with dice: roll 60 dice. Remove every six (“decayed”) and roll the rest again. Plot the number left after each roll. After about how many rolls is half gone? (Theory: log 2 / log(6/5) ≈ 3.8.)
  2. Brownian motion: put a drop of diluted milk on a slide under a cheap microscope or a phone macro lens. Watch the fat droplets jiggle. Warm the slide: what changes?
  3. Shock waves: watch a boat’s wake from a bridge. Its V shape is a close cousin of the Mach cone.
  4. Convergence: look up two independent methods for measuring something you cannot see directly (say, the age of the Earth). Why does their agreement increase your confidence?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: “Have you seen one?”

The legend: whenever someone mentioned atoms, Mach would ask, in Viennese dialect, “Ham’s schon g’sehn?” (“Have you seen one?”), and he went to his grave denying that atoms exist.

The record: the remark was reported many years later by the physicist Stefan Meyer and others recalling Boltzmann’s lectures, and its exact wording and occasion are uncertain. [1] Mach did consistently treat atoms as at most a useful model, not a reality, and late in life showed no sign of conversion, though a famous story that he was persuaded by watching the scintillations of alpha particles is also poorly documented.

The grain of truth: Mach’s position was principled: he held that science should admit only what experience can connect to. The irony is that the experiments that made atoms observable, in the ways that counted for physicists, were exactly the kind of measurement he championed.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: realism and anti-realism

Scientific realists hold that our best theories describe a real world, including unobservable parts such as atoms and electrons; otherwise the success of science would be a miracle. Anti-realists, in the tradition of Mach, reply that theories need only be empirically adequate: they should save the phenomena (Part 5), and belief in unobservables goes beyond the evidence. They point out that past theories were successful yet postulated things we now reject, such as the luminiferous ether (Part 10).

Perrin’s argument from many independent methods is often cited as a model of how realists can answer the sceptic: if atoms did not exist, why would a dozen unrelated phenomena all point to the same number of them?

Questions to think about: Do you believe in electrons? What would it take to persuade a determined sceptic? Is an atom imaged by a microscope “seen”?
