3. Log–Log and Semi-Log Plots

Logarithmic axes (Part 1) are not just a way to fit large ranges onto a page; they are a test of the law behind the data. On a semi-log plot, with a logarithmic vertical axis, exponential growth or decay becomes a straight line. On a log–log plot, with both axes logarithmic, a power law y = a·xᵇ becomes a straight line whose slope is the exponent b. Vilfredo Pareto used a log–log plot in 1896 to show that the number of people with incomes above a given level followed a power law. [1]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(1, 100, 200)
power = 2 * x**1.5                                   # power law: y = a x^b
expo = 2 * np.exp(0.08 * x)                          # exponential: y = a e^(kx)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 3.8))
for ax, (xs, ys, title) in zip(axes, [("linear", "linear", "Linear axes"),
                                      ("linear", "log", "Semi-log (log y)"),
                                      ("log", "log", "Log–log")]):
    ax.plot(x, power, label="power law  2x^1.5")
    ax.plot(x, expo, label="exponential  2e^(0.08x)")
    ax.set_xscale(xs)
    ax.set_yscale(ys)
    ax.set_title(title)
axes[0].legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
# On semi-log axes the exponential is straight; on log–log axes the power law is straight,
# with slope equal to its exponent: fit it with np.polyfit(np.log(x), np.log(y), 1).

A caution: almost any smooth curve looks fairly straight on log–log axes over a small range, and claims of power laws in data have often been overstated. Aaron Clauset, Cosma Shalizi and Mark Newman showed that many published power laws do not survive careful statistical tests. [2]

4. Contour and Surface Plots

A quantity that varies over two dimensions, such as height over a landscape, temperature over a region or a function of two parameters, can be shown as a contour plot: lines joining points of equal value. Edmond Halley’s 1701 chart of the Atlantic, with lines of equal magnetic compass variation, was one of the first to show a measured quantity this way. [3]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200)
y = np.linspace(-2, 2, 150)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
Z = np.exp(-((X - 1) ** 2 + Y**2)) + 0.8 * np.exp(-((X + 1.2) ** 2 + (Y - 0.5) ** 2) / 0.5)  # two "hills"

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12, 4))
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(1, 3, 1)
cs = ax1.contour(X, Y, Z, levels=8, colors="black", linewidths=0.8)
ax1.clabel(cs, fontsize=7)                          # label the contour lines
ax1.set_title("Contour lines")
ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 3, 2)
cf = ax2.contourf(X, Y, Z, levels=15, cmap="viridis")
fig.colorbar(cf, ax=ax2)
ax2.set_title("Filled contours")
ax3 = fig.add_subplot(1, 3, 3, projection="3d")
ax3.plot_surface(X, Y, Z, cmap="viridis", linewidth=0)
ax3.set_title("Surface (hard to read exactly)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

5. Polar Plots and Spectrograms

Some data are naturally circular: wind directions, compass bearings, times of day, months of the year. A polar plot puts angle around a circle, so that north and the end of the year join up with their neighbours; a wind rose is a polar bar chart of how often the wind blows from each direction. Polar plots share the weaknesses of pie and radar charts (Parts 2 and 8): areas grow with the square of the radius, and values at different angles are hard to compare.

A spectrogram shows how the frequencies in a signal, such as speech, music, bird song or vibration, change over time: time runs along the horizontal axis, frequency up the vertical axis, and colour shows the strength of each frequency at each moment. The sound spectrograph was described by engineers at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1946. [4] SciPy computes spectrograms directly: [5]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import signal

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12, 4.5))

# 1. Polar bar chart (a simple wind rose): direction is naturally circular
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 1, projection="polar")
directions = np.deg2rad(np.arange(0, 360, 30))
counts = np.array([5, 8, 12, 20, 26, 18, 10, 6, 4, 3, 3, 4])      # synthetic wind counts
ax1.bar(directions, counts, width=np.deg2rad(28), color="#0072B2", alpha=0.8)
ax1.set_theta_zero_location("N")                    # north at the top
ax1.set_theta_direction(-1)                         # clockwise, like a compass
ax1.set_title("Wind rose: where the wind comes from")

# 2. Spectrogram: frequency content of a signal over time
fs = 2000
t = np.arange(0, 3, 1 / fs)
chirp = signal.chirp(t, f0=50, f1=600, t1=3) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=t.size)   # rising tone + noise
f, tt, S = signal.spectrogram(chirp, fs=fs, nperseg=256)
ax2 = fig.add_subplot(1, 2, 2)
ax2.pcolormesh(tt, f, 10 * np.log10(S + 1e-12), cmap="magma", shading="auto")
ax2.set_ylim(0, 800)
ax2.set_xlabel("Time (s)")
ax2.set_ylabel("Frequency (Hz)")
ax2.set_title("Spectrogram of a rising tone")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Survival Curves

In medical studies and reliability engineering, the question is often how long until something happens: death, relapse, the failure of a component. Some subjects are censored: the study ends, or they leave it, before the event. Simply ignoring them, or treating their last contact as an event, biases the result. In 1958 Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier published a method that uses each subject’s information for as long as they were observed, producing the staircase-shaped Kaplan–Meier curve. [6]

How to read a survival curve
  1. Each step down is an event; tick marks are censored subjects.
  2. The median survival time is where the curve crosses 50%.
  3. The right-hand end rests on few subjects and is very uncertain; look for the number still at risk, often printed below the plot.
  4. Curves that cross suggest a treatment that helps some patients or periods and not others.

The lifelines library provides Kaplan–Meier estimates with confidence bands; the method itself is short enough to write by hand:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def kaplan_meier(time, event):
    """Return times and survival probabilities; event=1 means the event happened, 0 means censored."""
    order = np.argsort(time)
    time, event = time[order], event[order]
    at_risk, surv = len(time), 1.0
    ts, ss = [0.0], [1.0]
    for t in np.unique(time):
        d = np.sum((time == t) & (event == 1))          # events at this time
        if d:
            surv *= 1 - d / at_risk
            ts.append(t); ss.append(surv)
        at_risk -= np.sum(time == t)                     # events and censored leave the risk set
    return np.array(ts), np.array(ss)

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
for name, scale, col in [("Standard treatment", 10, "#D55E00"), ("New treatment", 24, "#0072B2")]:
    t_event = rng.exponential(scale, 100)               # synthetic months to relapse
    t_censor = rng.uniform(6, 36, 100)                   # end of follow-up
    time = np.minimum(t_event, t_censor)
    event = (t_event <= t_censor).astype(int)
    ts, ss = kaplan_meier(time, event)
    ax.step(ts, ss, where="post", color=col, label=name)
ax.set_xlabel("Months since treatment")
ax.set_ylabel("Proportion still relapse-free")
ax.set_ylim(0, 1.02)
ax.legend()
ax.set_title("Kaplan–Meier survival curves")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Control Charts

On 16 May 1924 Walter Shewhart, an engineer at Bell Telephone Laboratories, sketched the first control chart in a short memo, and he set out the method fully in 1931. [7] A control chart plots a process measurement over time, with a centre line and control limits, usually three standard deviations either side. Variation within the limits is the normal noise of the process; a point outside them, or a suspicious pattern, signals a cause worth finding.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
weights = rng.normal(500, 2, 60)                        # synthetic fill weights (g)
weights[40:] += 3                                        # the machine drifts after sample 40

baseline = weights[:20]                                  # limits from an in-control period
centre, sigma = baseline.mean(), baseline.std(ddof=1)
ucl, lcl = centre + 3 * sigma, centre - 3 * sigma

out = (weights > ucl) | (weights < lcl)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 4))
ax.plot(weights, "o-", color="#0072B2", ms=4)
ax.plot(np.where(out)[0], weights[out], "o", color="#D55E00", ms=8, label="outside limits")
for level, style in [(centre, "-"), (ucl, "--"), (lcl, "--")]:
    ax.axhline(level, color="gray", ls=style)
ax.set_xlabel("Sample number")
ax.set_ylabel("Fill weight (g)")
ax.set_title("Shewhart control chart: centre line and ±3σ limits")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Choosing the Right Chart

Across this series, one principle keeps returning: start from the question, not from the chart type. What comparison should the reader make first? Then choose the visual encoding the eye reads most accurately for that comparison: position on a common scale where possible, then length, and only then angle, area or colour (Part 1). Leland Wilkinson’s grammar of graphics, which describes any chart as data mapped to marks, scales and coordinates, underlies modern plotting tools and makes this way of thinking natural. [8]

9. How Charts Mislead: A Checklist

Before you trust a chart, check…
  1. The axes. Do bars start at zero? Is the scale linear or logarithmic? Are panels on the same scale? (Part 1)
  2. The window. Why does the time axis start and end where it does? (Part 3)
  3. Two y-axes. Could the scales have been chosen to make lines look related? (Part 3)
  4. The bins and smoothing. Would another bin width or bandwidth tell a different story? (Part 4)
  5. The summary. Do bars of means or box plots hide clusters, outliers or tiny samples? (Parts 4 and 5)
  6. The error bars. Are they SD, SE or CI, and what is n? (Part 7)
  7. Hidden groups. Could a lurking variable reverse the pattern? (Part 6)
  8. Areas and colours. Are bubbles scaled by area? Is the colour map uniform and centred sensibly? (Parts 6 and 8)
  9. 3D and decoration. Do perspective or ornaments distort the values? (Part 1)
  10. The denominator. Counts or rates? Per person, per year, per test? (Part 2)

10. Figures for Publication

A figure in a paper or report must survive being shrunk to a column width, printed in greyscale and read by someone in a hurry. Set the figure size to its final printed size (a single journal column is about 8–9 cm, or 3.5 inches, wide) so that fonts appear at their intended size; use fonts no smaller than about 7 points; save line art in a vector format (PDF or SVG) and photographs at 300 dots per inch or more; use colour-blind-safe palettes; and write a caption that says what is plotted, what the error bars are, and how many observations there are. [9] Matplotlib’s rcParams set these defaults once for a whole project:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.rcParams.update({                     # set once at the top of a script or notebook
    "font.size": 9,                       # match the journal's body text
    "axes.spines.top": False,
    "axes.spines.right": False,
    "savefig.dpi": 300,
    "savefig.bbox": "tight",
})

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = np.linspace(0, 10, 30)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(3.5, 2.6))   # 3.5 inches ≈ one journal column wide
ax.errorbar(x, np.sin(x) + rng.normal(0, 0.1, x.size), yerr=0.1, fmt="o", ms=3, capsize=2,
            color="black", label="measured (mean ± SD, n = 5)")
ax.plot(x, np.sin(x), color="#0072B2", label="model")
ax.set_xlabel("Time (s)")
ax.set_ylabel("Signal (mV)")
ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=7, loc="lower center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, 1.0), ncol=2)
fig.savefig("figure1.pdf")                  # vector format for print: PDF or SVG
fig.savefig("figure1.png")                  # raster at 300 dpi for slides and web
plt.show()

11. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: a one-page memo

The legend: quality control began with complex statistical theory.

The record: it began with a picture. Shewhart’s memo of May 1924 to his manager at the Western Electric Company was less than a page long, and about a third of it was a diagram of what became the control chart. The insight was to separate “common causes” of variation, which are built into a process and should be left alone, from “assignable causes” that can be found and fixed, and to let a simple chart tell them apart. His colleague W. Edwards Deming later carried these ideas to Japanese industry after the Second World War. [7]

A second record: Halley’s 1701 magnetic chart was made for navigation, not science: sailors could estimate their longitude by comparing the compass variation they measured with the lines on the chart. It is one of the earliest maps of a measured quantity drawn with isolines. [3]

12. The Series in One Picture

Over twelve parts, this series has moved from the anatomy of a chart to the plots of machine learning. The same handful of ideas run through every part: read the frame before the data; choose encodings the eye reads well; show the data, not just summaries; make uncertainty visible; beware of scales, windows and hidden groups chosen by someone else; and check your own analyses with diagnostic plots before trusting them. Every plot here can be made with a few lines of Python. The best way to learn is to take a data set you care about and try several of them: the plot that surprises you is usually the one worth keeping.
