Can Machines Think? Turing, the First Computers and the Birth of AI

In just twenty-five years, electronic computers went from an idea on paper to machines that played games, learned from experience and proved theorems, and a new science got its name: artificial intelligence.

1. A New Kind of Machine

Part 1 ended in 1937 with three ideas waiting to come together: machines that follow a stored program, reasoning as calculation, and logic written as 1s and 0s. In the twenty-five years after that, they did. Electronic computers were built, and almost at once people began asking whether these new machines could think. [18, 19]

This part covers roughly 1936 to 1961. In that short time:

  • Alan Turing worked out what a computer is, before one existed, and later asked the famous question that gives this part its title.
  • Scientists built model brain cells out of logic, and robots out of a few valves and a light sensor.
  • The first programs played games, learned from experience and proved mathematical theorems.
  • A group of researchers gave the new field its name: artificial intelligence.

It was a time of huge excitement and bold predictions. Many of those predictions turned out to be wrong, and Part 3 tells what happened next. But almost every basic idea in today's AI, from neural networks to learning by trial and error, first appeared in these years.

3. Turing, War and the First Computers

In 1936 a 24-year-old Cambridge mathematician, Alan Turing (1912–1954), published a paper about a question in pure mathematics: is there a mechanical method for deciding every mathematical statement? To answer it (the answer was no), he first had to pin down exactly what a "mechanical method" is. So he imagined the simplest possible calculating machine: a long tape of squares, a head that reads and writes one symbol at a time, and a short table of rules. [1, 3]

Turing showed that one such machine, a universal machine, could imitate any other, if it was given the other machine's rules written on its tape. In other words, one machine plus the right instructions can do any job that can be done by following rules. That is exactly what a modern computer is. (The mathematics is told in Part 11 of How We Came to Know, "What Computers Can't Do".)

Three years later war broke out, and Turing joined the British codebreakers at Bletchley Park. He designed the "bombe", an electro-mechanical machine that from 1940 helped break messages sent with the German Enigma cipher machine. For a harder German cipher, used by the high command, the Post Office engineer Tommy Flowers built Colossus. Working from early 1944, it used about 1,600 vacuum tubes (valves), electronic switches with no moving parts, and was thousands of times faster than anything mechanical. Colossus was kept secret for decades. [21]

In the United States, ENIAC was completed at the University of Pennsylvania in 1945 to calculate artillery tables. It filled a large room and used about 18,000 valves. But Colossus and ENIAC had to be "programmed" by setting switches and plugging cables, which could take days. In 1945 the mathematician John von Neumann wrote a report describing a better design: keep the program in the computer's own memory, alongside the numbers it works on, so a new program can be loaded as easily as new data. [22, 23]

The first machine to actually do this was a small experimental computer in Manchester, nicknamed the Baby. On 21 June 1948 it ran a 17-instruction program, stored in its own memory, that found the highest factor of a number. Every computer since, including the one you are reading this on, works the same way. Turing himself moved to Manchester later that year, to work on programming its successors. [22]

4. Brains Made of Switches

If a computer is a network of switches, is a brain one too? In 1943 the neuroscientist Warren McCulloch and a brilliant, largely self-taught young logician, Walter Pitts, published a paper suggesting that it could be thought of that way. [4]

A real brain cell, or neuron, receives signals from other cells. If enough signals arrive together, it "fires", sending a signal of its own. McCulloch and Pitts simplified this into a model neuron:

  • Each input is either on (1) or off (0).
  • Some inputs excite the neuron (push it towards firing); others inhibit it (hold it back).
  • The neuron adds up its inputs. If the total reaches a set level, its threshold, it fires (outputs 1); otherwise it stays silent (0).

Their key result was that networks of these simple units could carry out any logical rule, the same AND, OR and NOT that Boole had described (Part 1). The brain, they suggested, might be a kind of logic machine. It was a huge simplification of real neurons, but it planted the seed of every artificial neural network since.

McCulloch and Pitts's neurons were wired up in fixed ways: they could not learn. In 1949 the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb suggested how real brains might. When one cell repeatedly helps to make another fire, he proposed, the connection between them grows stronger. This is often summed up as "cells that fire together wire together". Hebb's idea, that learning means changing the strength of connections, is still at the heart of how neural networks are trained today. [5]

5. Feedback and Robot Tortoises

During the war, the American mathematician Norbert Wiener worked on anti-aircraft guns that would aim at where a plane was going to be. This got him thinking about feedback: a system that measures the result of its own actions and corrects itself. A thermostat does it, and so does your hand reaching for a cup, constantly adjusting as it goes. [6]

In 1948 Wiener published Cybernetics, a book arguing that animals and machines both control themselves through feedback and information, so the same science could study both. The word comes from the Greek for "steersman". For a few years cybernetics was the most exciting field around, bringing together engineers, biologists and psychologists.

The most charming result came from Bristol, England. The neuroscientist William Grey Walter built two small robots in 1948–49, nicknamed Elmer and Elsie. Each had a shell, three wheels, a light sensor, a touch switch and a "brain" of just two valves. Their behaviour came from a few simple rules: [7]

  • If you see no light, keep turning your steering while you creep forward, which makes you scan around.
  • If you see a moderate light, stop turning and head for it.
  • If the light is very bright, turn away.
  • If you bump into something, back off and turn.

The tortoises seemed almost alive. They explored, circled lights, and wove complicated paths around obstacles. Later versions had a light inside their charging hutch: when their batteries ran low, they became less dazzled by bright light and so found their way home to "feed". Grey Walter's point was that complex, purposeful-looking behaviour can come from very simple machinery interacting with the world. That idea would return decades later in robotics. [7]

6. Shannon: Bits and Chess

We met Claude Shannon in Part 1, showing that switches can do logic. In 1948, working at Bell Labs, he published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication". It showed how to measure information itself, in units he called bits (binary digits): one bit is the information in the answer to a single yes/no question. Every text, photo, song and AI model today is stored and sent as bits. [8]

Shannon also loved games and gadgets. In 1950 he wrote a paper on how a computer could be programmed to play chess. A chess program, he explained, should do two things: [9]

  • Look ahead: imagine its possible moves, the opponent's possible replies, its replies to those, and so on, building a "tree" of possibilities.
  • Score positions: since it can't look ahead to the end of the game, judge each imagined position with a rule of thumb, for example by counting material (a queen is worth about nine pawns) and adding points for things like mobility.

Then pick the move that leads to the best position, assuming the opponent also plays their best. Shannon estimated that the number of possible chess games is around 10120, far more than the number of atoms in the observable universe, so no machine could ever simply check them all. It would have to be selective, as human players are. That combination of look-ahead and scoring was used by chess programs for the rest of the century. Shannon also built "Theseus" (1950), a mechanical mouse that found its way through a maze by trial and error and then remembered the route.

7. The Imitation Game

In October 1950, Turing published a paper in the philosophy journal Mind called "Computing Machinery and Intelligence". It begins: "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" [10]

But Turing immediately pointed out a problem. Words like "machine" and "think" are too vague to argue about usefully. So instead he replaced the question with a game. In the original version, a judge exchanges typed messages with two hidden players, a man and a woman, and tries to work out which is which. Then, Turing asked, what if one player is replaced by a machine? Can the judge tell the machine from the person? This is the imitation game, now known as the Turing test.

The beauty of the test is that it ignores what the machine is made of and how it works. Judge it the way we judge each other: by what it says. If it can talk about anything as well as a person can, Turing suggested, we would have as much reason to say it thinks as we have for other people.

Turing also made a prediction. He thought that in about fifty years, computers with a memory of about 109 bits (roughly 125 megabytes) would play the game so well that "an average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning". And he expected that by the end of the century, people would be able to speak of machines thinking "without expecting to be contradicted". How those predictions turned out is part of the story of Parts 6 and 7.

8. Turing Answers His Critics

Most of Turing's paper is spent answering objections to the idea of thinking machines. He listed nine, and his replies are still worth reading. Here are five of them in plain words: [10, 2]

  • "The consequences would be too dreadful." Turing called this the "heads in the sand" objection: wishing something weren't true is no argument that it isn't.
  • "Machines can't be conscious." But, Turing replied, we can't be sure anyone else is conscious either; we only see how they behave. If we refuse to judge machines by behaviour, we must doubt each other too. Most people adopt the "polite convention" that others think.
  • "Gödel proved machines have limits." Gödel's theorem (Part 11 of How We Came to Know) shows any machine has questions it can't answer. True, said Turing, but there is no proof that humans are free of such limits.
  • "Machines can never do X": be kind, fall in love, enjoy strawberries and cream, make mistakes, learn from experience. Turing noted that these claims were mostly based on the machines people had seen so far, not on any argument.
  • Lady Lovelace's objection: a machine can only do what we tell it (Part 1). Turing replied that machines surprised him all the time. And it could be built to learn, so that its behaviour came from experience rather than from its programmer.

That last point led to the most forward-looking idea in the paper. Instead of trying to program an adult mind directly, Turing wrote, "why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's?" Build a simple learning machine, then educate it, rewarding and punishing it as we teach children. That is very close to how today's AI systems are made: they are trained rather than programmed.

9. Machines That Learned to Play

Games were the perfect testing ground: clear rules, a clear goal, and a clear way to keep score. By 1952, Christopher Strachey had a program playing complete games of draughts (checkers) on the Ferranti Mark 1 computer in Manchester. [2]

At IBM in the United States, Arthur Samuel wrote a draughts program for IBM's first commercial computers and kept improving it for years. Its real novelty was that it learned. It used Shannon's look-ahead and scoring idea, but it adjusted its own scoring rule based on experience, including thousands of games against copies of itself. In time it played better than Samuel himself. His 1959 paper about it helped make the phrase machine learning popular. [11]

The simplest learning machine of all contained no electronics. In 1961 the British researcher Donald Michie, who had worked alongside Turing at Bletchley Park, built MENACE (the Machine Educable Noughts And Crosses Engine) out of 304 matchboxes. Each box stood for one position on the board and held coloured beads, one colour for each possible move. To play, you took the box for the current position, shook out a bead at random, and made that move. [12]

After each game, MENACE was rewarded or punished. If it won, three extra beads of the right colour went into each box it had used; for a draw, one; if it lost, the beads it had played were taken away. Moves that led to wins became more likely; moves that led to losses gradually disappeared. In a tournament of a couple of hundred games against Michie himself, MENACE steadily improved until it was hard to beat, without anyone telling it a single rule of strategy.

This idea, learning by reward and punishment, is now called reinforcement learning. Fifty-five years later, a far more powerful version of it would beat the world's best player at Go (Part 5).

10. Naming the Field: Dartmouth, 1956

By the mid-1950s, researchers were trying to make machines think in many different ways, under many different names: cybernetics, automata studies, complex information processing. A young mathematician at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire, John McCarthy, wanted to bring them together. [13]

In August 1955, with Marvin Minsky of Harvard, Nathaniel Rochester of IBM and Claude Shannon, McCarthy wrote a proposal to the Rockefeller Foundation for a two-month summer workshop. It was here that the name artificial intelligence first appeared. The proposal's central claim was breathtakingly confident:

The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.

It listed topics to study: how to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems then reserved for humans, and improve themselves. It also predicted that "a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer."

The workshop took place in the summer of 1956. Among those who came, for at least part of it, were McCarthy, Minsky, Shannon, Rochester, Arthur Samuel, Oliver Selfridge, Ray Solomonoff, and two researchers from Pittsburgh, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon. No great breakthrough happened that summer. But the people there went on to found the main AI laboratories, at MIT, Stanford and Carnegie Mellon, and to lead the field for the next twenty years.

11. A Program That Proved Theorems

Newell and Simon arrived at Dartmouth with something nobody else had: a working program. Together with the programmer Cliff Shaw at the RAND Corporation, they had built the Logic Theorist, a program that proved theorems in logic. In January 1956 Simon told his students: "Over Christmas, Allen Newell and I invented a thinking machine." Before the program ran on a computer, Simon tested it by having his family and students act out its parts by hand, each holding cards with instructions. [14, 18]

The Logic Theorist worked on theorems from Principia Mathematica, Whitehead and Russell's great attempt to build mathematics from logic. It searched for proofs by working backwards from the statement to be proved, trying to reach things it already knew. Because the number of possible paths explodes, it used rules of thumb, called heuristics, to try the most promising steps first, much as a human mathematician does.

It proved 38 of the first 52 theorems in chapter 2 of Principia. For one of them, its proof was shorter and more elegant than Whitehead and Russell's. Simon wrote to Russell, who was reportedly delighted. Newell and Simon tried to publish the new proof in a logic journal with the Logic Theorist as a co-author; the journal declined.

The team followed it with the General Problem Solver (from 1957), which tried to solve any problem that could be described as getting from a starting situation to a goal, by repeatedly reducing the difference between where it was and where it wanted to be. Meanwhile McCarthy invented LISP (1958), a programming language for manipulating symbols that was used for AI research for decades.

Success bred confidence. In 1958 Simon and Newell predicted that within ten years a computer would be the world chess champion, unless the rules barred it, and would discover and prove an important new mathematical theorem. Both things did happen eventually: a computer beat the world chess champion in 1997, and in 1996 a program proved a long-open problem in algebra (the Robbins conjecture). But it took nearly forty years, not ten. [15]

12. The Perceptron: A Machine That Learned to See

Newell, Simon and McCarthy believed intelligence was a matter of manipulating symbols by rules. A psychologist at Cornell, Frank Rosenblatt (1928–1971), took the other road, the one McCulloch, Pitts and Hebb had pointed to: build something like a brain, and let it learn. [16]

His perceptron (1957–58) was a network of artificial neurons with one crucial addition: the strengths of its connections, called weights, could change. It was shown examples, such as cards marked on the left or the right, and told the right answer each time. Whenever it got one wrong, each weight was nudged slightly in the direction that would have given the right answer. Over many examples, the mistakes became rarer.

It was soon proved that if the two kinds of example can be separated by a straight line (or, with more inputs, a flat cut), this learning rule is guaranteed to find a separating line eventually. In 1958 he built a machine version, the Mark I Perceptron: a camera of 400 light sensors arranged in a 20 × 20 grid, wired to artificial neurons whose weights were adjusted by electric motors turning dials. It learned to recognise simple shapes and letters.

The press went wild. After a demonstration in July 1958, the New York Times reported that the US Navy, which funded the work, expected the device to be the "embryo of an electronic computer" that "will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence". Rosenblatt himself made bold claims. [17]

So by about 1960, two rival approaches to AI were each promising the world. The symbolic road, of logic and rules, had the Logic Theorist. The neural road, of learning networks, had the perceptron. As Figure 5 hints, the perceptron had a weakness: it could only learn patterns that a straight line can split. Part 3 tells how that weakness, and a lot of broken promises, plunged AI into its first winter.

13. How Did They Know? How Samuel's Program Learned

How did they know? Teaching a program to judge a position

Saying a program "learns" sounds mysterious. Samuel's 1959 paper explained exactly what his draughts program did, and it can be followed step by step. [11]

  1. Describe a position with numbers. Samuel chose features a player might notice: how many pieces each side has, how many kings, how many moves are available, how much of the centre is controlled, and so on.
  2. Score it with a weighted sum. The program's judgement of a position was a total: each feature multiplied by a weight saying how important it is. At first the weights were guesses.
  3. Look ahead. To choose a move, it imagined the possible moves and replies several turns ahead (Shannon's tree) and used the scoring rule on the positions at the end.
  4. Compare the quick judgement with the deeper one. A position's score after looking ahead is usually more accurate than its score at a glance. So after each move, the program adjusted its weights so that its quick, at-a-glance scores agreed better with the scores it got by looking ahead.
  5. Practise against itself. One version of the program played another; the one that learned better rules kept them.

Step 4 is the clever part. Without anyone telling it which features mattered, the program discovered, for example, that some features it had been given were nearly useless and that others mattered a lot. The general idea, improving your estimates by making them consistent with slightly better later estimates, became an important technique in modern reinforcement learning.

14. Legend and Record: Was AI Born at Dartmouth?

Legend and Record: the Dartmouth workshop

The legend: in the summer of 1956, the founders of AI gathered at Dartmouth for two months, worked side by side, and launched a new science. [13, 18, 19]

The record: the truth is messier. The proposal asked for ten people for two months, but attendance was patchy: many came for a few days or weeks, and only a few stayed for the whole summer. There was no shared project. Newell and Simon, the only ones with a working program, were reportedly not very interested in the others' ideas, and McCarthy later said the meeting fell short of what he had hoped. Many of the key ideas came from earlier work done elsewhere: McCulloch and Pitts, Turing, Shannon, and the cyberneticists.

What really happened: the workshop's lasting achievement was the name, and the network of people it brought together. McCarthy said he chose "artificial intelligence" partly to set the field apart from cybernetics, which was dominated by Norbert Wiener. The name stuck, though many people, then and now, have found it misleading or overblown.

15. Philosophy Corner: Is Passing the Test Enough?

Philosophy Corner: the Turing test and the Chinese Room

Turing's test says, in effect: if it talks like a thinker, treat it as a thinker. Not everyone agrees that this is enough. [10, 20]

The most famous challenge came in 1980 from the American philosopher John Searle, with a thought experiment called the Chinese Room. Imagine a person who speaks no Chinese, locked in a room with a huge rule book. Chinese questions are passed in through a slot. By following the rules, which say things like "when you see these symbols, write those symbols", the person sends back perfect Chinese answers. To people outside, the room understands Chinese. But the person inside understands nothing. They are just shuffling symbols.

A computer running a program, Searle argued, is in the same position: it follows rules for manipulating symbols without understanding what they mean. So passing the Turing test would not prove a machine understands anything.

Critics replied in several ways. Perhaps the person doesn't understand Chinese, but the whole system (person, rule book and room together) does. Or perhaps understanding is nothing more than having the right patterns of activity, whatever they are made of. Searle answered that adding more symbol-shuffling never adds meaning.

The argument has never been settled, and it matters more today than ever. Modern AI chatbots produce fluent answers by learning patterns from enormous amounts of text. Are they the Chinese Room, or something more? Keep the question in mind through Parts 6 to 8.
