Beyond Silicon: Chiplets, Photonics, Neuromorphic and Quantum Computing

Shrinking transistors can no longer carry progress alone. The next gains will come from stacking chips, computing with light and spikes, harnessing quantum mechanics, and smarter use of every level of the machine.

1. The Road Ahead

For fifty years the recipe for better computers was simple: shrink the transistor. Dennard scaling ended around 2005 (Part 10), the cost per transistor now falls slowly, and the most important limit is energy, especially the energy of moving data (Part 13). Computing has not stopped improving, but the gains now come from many directions at once: better software and algorithms, specialised hardware, new ways of packaging chips, and entirely new physical principles. Charles Leiserson and colleagues summed this up in 2020 by arguing that “there’s plenty of room at the top” of the computing stack, even as room at the bottom runs out. [1, 2]

3. Chiplets and 3D Integration

Instead of one huge die, a processor can be built from several smaller chiplets in one package. Small dies yield far better (Part 10), each can be tested before assembly, and each can be made in the process that suits it: compute cores on the newest node, input/output and memory interfaces on older, cheaper ones. AMD’s server processors from 2017 onwards made the approach mainstream. [3] Industry groups have since agreed common die-to-die interface standards so that chiplets from different makers could be combined.

Packaging is going vertical too. High-bandwidth memory stacks DRAM dies connected by thousands of through-silicon vias, placed beside the processor on a silicon interposer (Part 13). Hybrid bonding joins dies face to face with connections a few micrometres apart, allowing, for example, extra cache stacked directly on top of processor cores. The challenges are heat, since stacked dies are harder to cool, testing, and the cost of the packages themselves.

4. Domain-Specific Accelerators

A general-purpose core spends most of its energy not on arithmetic but on fetching and decoding instructions, predicting branches, renaming registers and moving data through caches. Hardware tailored to one class of problems can strip most of that away. In their 2018 Turing lecture, published in 2019, John Hennessy and David Patterson argued that this makes the coming decade “a new golden age for computer architecture”, with domain-specific architectures, programmed through domain-specific languages, delivering large gains in performance per watt. [4] Examples are everywhere: GPUs and tensor accelerators for AI (Part 13), video codecs and image processors in phones, cryptographic engines, network processors, and FPGAs, reconfigurable chips whose lookup tables (Part 2) can be rewired after manufacture.

5. Light Instead of Electrons: Silicon Photonics

Electrical wires are capacitors that must be charged for every bit, so their energy grows with length (Part 4). Light in a waveguide or optical fibre travels with little loss, and many wavelengths can share one fibre, so long links have been optical for decades. Silicon photonics builds waveguides, modulators and detectors with the same factories that make chips. A milestone came in 2004, when Intel researchers demonstrated a silicon modulator switching faster than 1 GHz. [5] David Miller has analysed how optics could eventually compete even for short links inside computers if its energy per bit falls to the femtojoule level. [6] Today, optical transceivers connect the racks of every data centre, and “co-packaged optics” places them right beside switch and processor chips.

Light can also compute. A mesh of interferometers performs matrix multiplication as light passes through it; in 2017 Yichen Shen and colleagues demonstrated a simple neural network running on such a programmable nanophotonic processor. [7] Whether optical computing can beat electronics in practice, given the cost of converting between electrical and optical signals, is still being explored.

6. Brain-Inspired Computing

A human brain performs remarkable feats of perception on about 20 watts. Its neurons are slow, but there are tens of billions of them, they compute where their memory is stored, and they communicate with sparse electrical spikes. In 1990 Carver Mead proposed building neuromorphic electronic systems that copy these principles. [8] Digital neuromorphic chips followed: IBM’s TrueNorth (2014) packed a million spiking neurons and 256 million synapses into a chip using tens of milliwatts, and Intel’s Loihi (2018) added on-chip learning. [9, 10]

Another approach computes inside memory. Leon Chua predicted in 1971 a fourth basic circuit element, the memristor, whose resistance depends on the history of current through it; [11] in 2008 HP Labs reported a titanium dioxide device behaving this way. [12] In a crossbar of such devices, applying voltages to the rows produces currents in the columns equal to a matrix–vector product, by Ohm’s and Kirchhoff’s laws (Part 4), computed in place with no data movement. Device variability, limited precision and wear remain obstacles.

7. Quantum Computing

In 1982 Richard Feynman argued that simulating quantum systems efficiently would need a computer that is itself quantum mechanical. [13] A quantum computer stores information in qubits, which can be in superpositions of 0 and 1 and can be entangled with each other, so n qubits are described by 2n complex amplitudes. A quantum algorithm arranges for the amplitudes of wrong answers to cancel by interference and those of right answers to add up. In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could factor large numbers efficiently, which would break the public-key encryption used across the internet and has driven the adoption of new, “post-quantum” cryptography. [14]

Building one is extraordinarily hard: qubits lose their quantum state through tiny interactions with their surroundings. In 2019 Google reported that its 53-qubit superconducting processor performed a random-sampling task far faster than it estimated the best classical supercomputer could, though improved classical methods later narrowed the gap. [15] The decisive step is quantum error correction, which spreads one reliable “logical” qubit across many physical ones. In results published in 2025, Google showed logical qubits whose error rates fell as the code was enlarged, the threshold behaviour needed for large-scale machines. [16] Useful, fault-tolerant quantum computers will probably need hundreds of thousands to millions of physical qubits, and they will accelerate specific problems such as simulating molecules and materials, not everyday computing. [17]

8. Looking Back: From Switch to Supercomputer

This series climbed a ladder of abstraction, each rung built on the one below:

  • Quantum mechanics explains why electrons in crystals form bands, and why silicon is a semiconductor whose conductivity can be controlled (Part 5).
  • Junctions and fields turn that control into devices: diodes, bipolar transistors and MOSFETs (Parts 6–8).
  • Circuits pair transistors into CMOS gates that restore signals and waste almost no power at rest (Parts 4 and 9).
  • Manufacturing prints billions of them at once, and scaling made them cheaper and faster for fifty years (Parts 10 and 11).
  • Logic combines gates into adders, multiplexers, flip-flops and state machines (Parts 1–3).
  • Architecture organises those into processors with pipelines, caches and prediction, and into massively parallel GPUs (Parts 12 and 13).

At every level, a clean abstraction hides messy physics: a “1” is really a voltage, a switch is really an exponential, and an instruction sequence is really hundreds of operations in flight. Understanding several rungs at once is what lets engineers keep improving computers when one rung reaches its limits.

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): monolithic versus chiplets

A 600 mm² design is made either as one die or as four 150 mm² chiplets (ignore interface overhead). With 0.1 defects per cm², compare the yields and the silicon cost per working product.

Solution

Monolithic: Y = e−6 × 0.1 ≈ 55%, so each working product costs 600/0.55 ≈ 1,090 mm² of silicon. Chiplets: Y = e−1.5 × 0.1 ≈ 86% each, and since chiplets are tested first, a product costs 4 × 150/0.86 ≈ 700 mm²: about 36% less silicon. Interface area and assembly losses eat into this, as Figure 3 shows.

Problem 2 (easy): link power

A processor sends 10 Tb/s of data off-package. Compare the power at 5 pJ/bit (electrical) and 1 pJ/bit (co-packaged optics).

Solution

P = bit rate × energy per bit: 1013 × 5 × 10−12 = 50 W electrically, versus 10 W optically. At the bandwidths of AI systems, interconnect energy becomes a significant share of the power budget.

Problem 3 (medium): firing rate of a model neuron

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron has τ = 20 ms, a threshold 15 mV above rest, and a constant drive R·I = 20 mV. Find the time to reach threshold from rest and the firing rate (ignore the refractory period). What happens at R·I = 14 mV?

Solution

V(t) = RI(1 − e−t/τ) reaches θ when t = τ ln(RI/(RI − θ)) = 20 × ln(20/5) ≈ 27.7 ms, so the rate is about 36 spikes per second. At 14 mV the voltage approaches 14 mV but never reaches 15 mV: the neuron never fires. This threshold behaviour makes spiking neurons natural detectors of strong inputs.

Problem 4 (medium): simulating qubits classically

Storing the full state of n qubits takes 2n complex numbers of 16 bytes each. How much memory is needed for 30, 50 and 60 qubits?

Solution

30 qubits: 230 × 16 B ≈ 17 GB, fine for a workstation. 50 qubits: 250 × 16 B ≈ 18 PB, beyond any single machine’s memory. 60 qubits: ≈ 18 EB. Each extra qubit doubles the requirement, which is why simulating even modest quantum computers exactly is so hard (though clever methods can handle special cases).

Problem 5 (harder): how far from the limit?

A logic node in a modern chip has a capacitance of about 0.1 fF and switches at 0.7 V. Compare the energy ½CV² dissipated in one transition with Landauer’s limit kT ln 2 at 300 K (Part 1). What does this suggest about the future of computing energy?

Solution

½CV² = 0.5 × 10−16 × 0.49 ≈ 2.5 × 10−17 J. kT ln 2 = 1.38 × 10−23 × 300 × 0.693 ≈ 2.9 × 10−21 J. The switching energy is about 8,500 times the limit, and a whole operation, including wires and memory access, costs far more. Physics leaves enormous room for more efficient computing; the obstacles are thermal noise (which demands voltages well above kT/q), the 60 mV/decade switching limit, and above all the energy of moving data. Better devices, shorter wires, computing in memory and smarter algorithms all attack that gap.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: what will computers be made of in 2050?

Nobody knows which technologies will dominate. Silicon CMOS has outlasted many predicted successors, and its ecosystem of factories, tools and designers is a formidable advantage. Will stacking and chiplets keep density growing? Will photonics move from linking chips to computing inside them? Will neuromorphic or in-memory computing find a mass market beyond niches? Will error-corrected quantum computers become practical, and for which problems? And will software and algorithms, where Leiserson and colleagues see large untapped gains, matter more than hardware? These are some of the most consequential open questions in engineering. [1]

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “quantum computers will replace ordinary computers”

The myth: quantum computers are simply much faster computers that will one day run everything.

The reality: quantum computers offer big speedups only for particular problems with special structure, such as factoring, simulating quantum systems and some optimisation and search tasks, and even then only once they are large and error-corrected. For word processing, web browsing or most of machine learning, classical computers will remain better and cheaper. Future systems will likely use quantum processors as accelerators attached to classical machines, much as GPUs are today.

Why it matters: every new computing technology so far has joined the toolbox rather than replacing it.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Chiplets and 3D stacking improve yield, mix processes and bring memory closer, at a cost in interfaces, heat and packaging.
  • Domain-specific accelerators trade generality for large gains in performance per watt.
  • Light beats wires beyond a crossover distance; silicon photonics is bringing optics ever closer to chips.
  • Neuromorphic and in-memory computing copy the brain’s event-driven, compute-where-stored style.
  • Quantum computers exploit superposition and interference for specific problems and depend on error correction.
  • From quantum physics to architecture, computers are a ladder of abstractions; progress now comes from every rung.
