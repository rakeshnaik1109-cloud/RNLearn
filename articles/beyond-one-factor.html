Beyond One Factor: Interactions, Blocks, Repeated Measures and Mixed Models

Real experiments vary several things at once, run on different days, and measure the same samples again and again. Here is how to analyse experiments with structure, and the traps that await those who ignore it.

1. One Factor Is Rarely Enough

Real experiments seldom vary just one thing. A chemist wants the best combination of catalyst and temperature. A biologist tests a drug in both male and female animals. Measurements are made on different days, by different people, on the same samples at several time points, or on many cells from a few animals. Each of these situations has a structure, and the one-way ANOVA of Part 5 ignores it.

This part extends the analysis of variance to that structure: factorial designs with two or more factors and their interactions, blocking to remove nuisance variation, repeated measures on the same units, analysis of covariance to adjust for a continuous variable, random effects and the trap of pseudoreplication, and the modern, flexible framework of mixed models. Getting the structure right is often more important than any choice of test. [1, 2]

3. Factorial Designs: Asking Several Questions at Once

The traditional advice is to change one thing at a time while holding everything else constant. In 1926 Ronald Fisher disagreed sharply: “No aphorism is more frequently repeated in connection with field trials, than that we must ask Nature few questions, or, ideally, one question, at a time. The writer is convinced that this view is wholly mistaken.” [3] Nature, he argued, responds best to a carefully thought-out set of questions asked together.

In a factorial design, every level of each factor is combined with every level of the others. Two catalysts at two temperatures make a 2 × 2 design with four combinations; adding three pressures makes 2 × 2 × 3 = 12. Factorial designs have two great advantages over changing one factor at a time: [4, 5]

  • Efficiency. Every run contributes to the estimate of every factor. In a 2 × 2 design with four runs per combination, the catalyst effect is estimated from all 16 runs (8 against 8), and so is the temperature effect. Studying the two factors in two separate experiments would need twice as many runs for the same precision.
  • Interactions. Only a factorial design can reveal that the effect of one factor depends on the level of another. One-factor-at-a-time experiments can miss the best conditions entirely when such interactions exist, as the worked example in Section 13 shows.

4. Main Effects and Interactions

With two factors, A and B, there are three questions:

  • The main effect of A: averaged over the levels of B, does A make a difference?
  • The main effect of B: averaged over the levels of A, does B make a difference?
  • The interaction A × B: does the effect of A depend on the level of B?

The clearest way to see an interaction is an interaction plot: the mean response for each combination, with one line for each level of one factor. Parallel lines mean no interaction; the effect of one factor is the same whatever the other does. Lines that diverge, converge or cross indicate an interaction.

When an interaction is present, main effects must be interpreted with care. A main effect is an average over the levels of the other factor, and an average of a large effect and no effect describes neither. Report the simple effects instead: the effect of each factor at each level of the other. In a crossover interaction, where the lines cross, a main effect can be close to zero even though each factor matters a great deal.

5. The Two-Way ANOVA Table

A two-way ANOVA splits the total sum of squares into four parts: factor A, factor B, the A × B interaction and the residual (within-cell) variation. With a levels of A, b levels of B and n replicates per combination, the degrees of freedom are a − 1, b − 1, (a − 1)(b − 1) and ab(n − 1). Each effect is tested with F = MSeffect/MSresidual. For the catalyst–temperature experiment of Section 13:

SourceSum of squaresdfFpPartial η²
Catalyst26.01151.81.1 × 10⁻⁵0.81
Temperature92.161183.71.2 × 10⁻⁸0.94
Catalyst × temperature19.36138.64.5 × 10⁻⁵0.76
Residual6.0212

With several effects in one model, partial η² = SSeffect/(SSeffect + SSresidual) is the usual effect size. All three effects here are significant, but the interaction tells the main story: the catalyst matters at 80 °C but not at 60 °C.

When the design is balanced (the same number of replicates in every cell), the sums of squares add up exactly and the order of the factors does not matter. With unbalanced data that is no longer true (Section 11).

6. Blocking: Removing Nuisance Variation

Some sources of variation are not of interest but cannot be avoided: different days, batches of reagent, operators, instruments, plots of land. If they are large, they swamp the effects you care about. The solution is blocking: group the experimental runs into blocks that are as similar as possible inside, and run every treatment within each block. The analysis then includes the block as a factor, which removes the block-to-block variation from the error term. [6]

The simplest version is the randomised complete block design: every treatment once in each block, in random order within the block. It was devised for field trials, where neighbouring plots share similar soil, and it is just as useful in the laboratory, where runs on the same day share the same conditions. The experimental designer’s rule of thumb is: block what you can, randomise what you cannot. [2]

In this example the treatment means are 20.55, 21.37 and 21.78. Ignoring days, the one-way ANOVA gives p = 0.53: nothing to see. With day as a block, p = 0.005. The data are identical; the blocked analysis simply recognises that measurements on the same day belong together.

7. Repeated Measures and Paired Designs

When the same experimental units are measured more than once, under different conditions or at several times, the design has repeated measures. The simplest case is the paired design of Part 4: before and after on the same samples. Each unit acts as its own control, so differences between units cancel out. Repeated-measures designs can be far more powerful than comparing separate groups, as Figure 4 shows.

For three or more conditions, a repeated-measures ANOVA treats the unit as a block. It adds one assumption: sphericity, that the differences between every pair of conditions have the same variance. It was the subject of a test published in 1940 by John Mauchly, better known as a co-designer of the ENIAC computer. [7] Sphericity often fails in practice, for example when measurements close in time are more alike than distant ones. The Greenhouse–Geisser and Huynh–Feldt corrections then reduce the degrees of freedom by a factor ε (between 1/(k − 1) and 1) to keep the test honest. [8, 9] Many statisticians now prefer mixed models (Section 10) for repeated measures, because they also cope with missing time points.

The order of conditions matters too. If every unit receives the conditions in the same order, the effect of a condition cannot be separated from learning, fatigue or drift. Randomise or counterbalance the order.

8. ANCOVA: Adjusting for a Covariate

Sometimes a continuous variable affects the response but is not of interest itself: the starting weight of an animal, the initial height of a plant, a baseline measurement before treatment, the room temperature. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) includes it as a covariate. [10] It fits a regression line for each group with a common slope and compares the groups at the same value of the covariate.

ANCOVA has two benefits. It removes the variation explained by the covariate from the error, so the test is more powerful; and it corrects for chance imbalance, when one group happens to start higher than another. For randomised experiments with a baseline measurement, ANCOVA on the final value is usually more powerful than analysing the change from baseline. [11]

Two cautions. ANCOVA assumes the slopes are similar in all groups; if they are not, the treatment effect depends on the covariate, which is itself an interaction worth reporting. And the covariate must not be affected by the treatment: adjusting for something the treatment changes can remove part of the very effect you want to measure.

9. Fixed and Random Effects, and Pseudoreplication

The factors so far have been fixed effects: their levels were chosen deliberately, and conclusions apply to those levels only. Some factors are different. The six days in Figure 3, eight patients, three batches of reagent or five laboratories are samples from a larger population of possible days, patients, batches or laboratories. Treated as random effects, they contribute a source of variation, and the conclusions can be generalised to that population. Churchill Eisenhart set out this distinction in 1947. [12]

A crucial question in every experiment is: what is the experimental unit? It is the smallest unit to which a treatment is independently applied. If a drug is added to three tanks of fish and a placebo to three others, the tank is the experimental unit, and there are three replicates per treatment, not thirty, however many fish are measured. Treating the fish as independent replicates is called pseudoreplication. Stuart Hurlbert showed in 1984 that it was widespread in ecological field experiments; [13] it remains common in laboratory science, where many cells, slices or readings are taken from a few animals or cultures. [14]

Even when tanks account for only 10% of the variation, analysing fish as independent gives a false-positive rate of about 16% instead of 5%; at 30%, about one in three. The simplest valid analysis averages within each tank first. Better still is a mixed model with tank as a random effect.

10. Mixed Models

A linear mixed model contains both fixed effects (the treatments you are comparing) and random effects (the grouping structure: animals, tanks, days, batches, subjects). [15] For the fish, a mixed model would estimate the treatment effect as a fixed effect and the tank-to-tank variation as a variance component, giving correct standard errors while using every fish.

The ideas grew from animal breeding, where Charles Henderson developed methods for estimating variance components in the 1950s. [16] Restricted maximum likelihood (REML), introduced in 1971, became the standard way to estimate them, [17] and Nan Laird and James Ware popularised mixed models for repeated measurements over time in 1982. [18] Widely available software has since made them routine. [19]

Mixed models have important advantages over classical repeated-measures ANOVA. They cope with missing measurements and unequal spacing in time, handle several crossed or nested random factors at once (say, cells within animals within litters), and allow covariates that change over time. They also shrink estimates for individual groups towards the overall mean, more strongly for groups with little data, which tends to make the estimates more accurate. The price is more modelling decisions to make and report, and assumptions (normal random effects, correct structure) that still need checking.

11. Unbalanced Data and Types of Sums of Squares

When cells have different numbers of replicates, perhaps because a run failed, the factors are no longer independent, and the variation they explain overlaps. Software then offers different types of sums of squares: [1]

  • Type I (sequential): each term is tested after those listed before it, so the order of the factors changes the answer.
  • Type II: each main effect is tested after the other main effects, assuming no interaction. A good choice when the interaction is negligible.
  • Type III: each term is tested after all the others, including interactions. Common in some fields, but its main-effect tests are hard to interpret when an interaction is present, and it requires a particular coding of the factors.

For balanced designs all three agree. For unbalanced ones, state which type you used. Better still, design for balance, and look at the interaction first.

12. Reporting

  • Describe the design fully: the factors and their levels, blocks, which factors are within or between units, the experimental unit, and the randomisation.
  • Show an interaction plot or the cell means with intervals, and the individual data where possible.
  • Report each F with both degrees of freedom, its p-value and an effect size such as partial η². For repeated measures, state any sphericity correction; for mixed models, the random effects and the estimation method.
  • If there is an interaction, report simple effects with confidence intervals rather than relying on main effects.
  • Make the number of true replicates clear. “n = 60 fish from 6 tanks” is honest; “n = 60” is not.

13. Worked Example: Catalyst and Temperature

A reaction is run with two catalysts (X and Y) at two temperatures (60 and 80 °C), four runs for each combination in random order. Mean yields (%), with SDs:

60 °C80 °C
Catalyst X70.60 (0.75): 70.2, 71.5, 69.8, 70.973.20 (0.66): 73.1, 72.4, 74.0, 73.3
Catalyst Y70.95 (0.64): 71.0, 70.3, 71.8, 70.777.95 (0.78): 78.2, 77.1, 78.9, 77.6
  1. Plot. The interaction plot shows lines that diverge: raising the temperature helps both catalysts, but Y much more.
  2. Two-way ANOVA (table in Section 5): interaction F(1, 12) = 38.6, p < 0.001; residual mean square 0.50, so the run-to-run SD is about 0.71 percentage points.
  3. Simple effects. At 60 °C, Y − X = 0.35 (95% CI −0.74 to 1.44, p = 0.50): no detectable difference. At 80 °C, Y − X = 4.75 (95% CI 3.66 to 5.84, p < 0.001).
  4. Conclusion. Use catalyst Y at 80 °C, which gives about 78% yield. The catalyst only matters at the higher temperature.
  5. What one-factor-at-a-time would have done. Starting at X and 60 °C, a chemist tries Y at 60 °C, sees no gain (70.95 versus 70.60), and keeps X. Then they raise the temperature and reach 73.2%. They stop there, never testing Y at 80 °C, and miss nearly 5 percentage points of yield.
Try it in Python: two-way ANOVA, blocking and a mixed model 
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

cells = {("X", 60): [70.2, 71.5, 69.8, 70.9], ("X", 80): [73.1, 72.4, 74.0, 73.3],
         ("Y", 60): [71.0, 70.3, 71.8, 70.7], ("Y", 80): [78.2, 77.1, 78.9, 77.6]}
df = pd.DataFrame([(c, t, y) for (c, t), ys in cells.items() for y in ys],
                  columns=["catalyst", "temp", "yield_"])
fit = smf.ols("yield_ ~ C(catalyst) * C(temp)", data=df).fit()
print(sm.stats.anova_lm(fit, typ=2))      # interaction F = 38.6

14. Try It Yourself: A Factorial Helicopter Experiment

Try it yourself: two factors, blocks and an interaction
  1. Using the paper helicopters from Part 5, choose two factors with two levels each: rotor length (short, long) and weight (one paper clip, two). That makes four designs.
  2. Recruit two or three people to drop and time. Each person is a block: every person times every design, three times each, in a random order.
  3. Draw the interaction plot. Does adding weight shorten the flight more for long rotors than short ones?
  4. Run a two-way ANOVA with person as a block: time ~ C(length) * C(weight) + C(person). How much variation do the people account for? What happens to the p-values if you leave person out?

Bonus: if each design is built once and dropped many times, what is the experimental unit? How would you change the experiment so that differences between individual helicopters are part of the replication?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: should a good experiment change one thing at a time?

The legend: the scientific method means changing one variable at a time while keeping everything else the same.

The record: Fisher argued in 1926 that this advice, endlessly repeated, was “wholly mistaken”, and his factorial designs, worked out at Rothamsted with Frank Yates, became standard in agriculture and industry. [3, 4] Factorial experiments use every run to estimate every factor and can reveal interactions, which one-factor-at-a-time experiments cannot. Industrial case studies have repeatedly found that designed experiments reach better conditions with fewer runs. [5]

The grain of truth: the legend contains a real principle, control: anything that is not being studied should be held constant, blocked or randomised, so that it cannot be confused with the factors of interest. What it gets wrong is the idea that the factors you are studying should be varied separately.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: what does an experiment generalise to?

Suppose you test a new protocol on cells from three mice, measuring a thousand cells from each. Is your sample size three or three thousand? The answer depends on the question. If you want to know about these three mice, the cells are replicates. If you want to know about mice in general, the mice are the replicates, and three is a small sample of mice, however many cells you measure.

The distinction between fixed and random effects is, at heart, a statement about what you want to generalise to. Treat three reagent batches as fixed, and your conclusions apply only to those three batches; treat them as random, and you claim something about batches in general, at the cost of wider uncertainty. Hurlbert’s pseudoreplication and the debates about mixed models are really debates about this question: what population does my experiment represent? [13, 15]

Questions to think about: In your own field, what is usually the experimental unit? When an experiment is repeated in another laboratory, with different people, equipment and suppliers, which of those differences should count as noise, and which as part of what the result claims to generalise over?
