Counting Words Together: How Language Models Began with Probabilities

Every answer a large language model gives is built one word at a time, each chosen from a list of probabilities. The story starts with the simplest way to get those probabilities: counting which words follow which.

At a glance

The puzzle: a large language model writes an answer one word at a time, each time choosing a likely next word. How can a machine that only predicts words produce answers that are fluent, relevant and often correct? This six-part series takes the machine apart.

In this part: language as a game of prediction; next-word probabilities from counting word pairs; n-gram models and the Markov assumption; generating text; why counting runs out of data; smoothing and perplexity; and what counting can never do.

Try: four interactive figures built on a small toy world of 800 sentences, including a text generator whose output you can watch turn from word salad into fluent sentences, and then into copies.

1. A Puzzle About Words

Ask a chatbot “What is the capital of Australia?” and it answers “Canberra”. Underneath, the model did not look the answer up. It was given the words of the question and computed, for every word (or piece of a word) it knows, a probability that it comes next. It picked one, added it to the text, and repeated the process until it decided to stop. Every answer, poem and program a large language model (LLM) produces is built this way: one word at a time.

That raises two questions this series answers. First, how does a model turn the patterns of words that occur together into probabilities? Second, why does choosing likely words so often produce true and useful answers, and why does it sometimes produce confident nonsense? We begin with the simplest possible language model: counting. [1, 2]

Running example

Throughout the series we use a toy world small enough to see completely: 1,000 short sentences generated from a simple grammar, about cats, dogs, birds and horses, girls, boys, teachers and farmers, what they eat and drink and where they sleep and play. It has just 39 distinct words. Cats prefer fish and milk, horses live in fields and never drink in the training text. The first 800 sentences are for training, the last 200 for testing. Real models learn from trillions of words, but the principles are the same.

3. Language as a Game of Prediction

Language is highly predictable. Read “the cat drinks the …” and you expect “milk” or “water”, not “garden”. In 1913 the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov counted how vowels and consonants follow each other in 20,000 letters of Pushkin’s Eugene Onegin, the first statistical study of a chain of dependent events, now called a Markov chain. [3] In 1951 Claude Shannon turned prediction into a game: people guessed a text one letter at a time, and from their success he estimated that English carries only about one bit of information per letter, far less than the nearly five bits needed to pick among 27 symbols blindly. [4]

A language model plays this game with numbers. For any text so far, it gives a probability to every possible next word. Multiplying those probabilities gives the probability of a whole sentence, by the chain rule of probability:

$$P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n) = P(w_1)\,P(w_2 \mid w_1)\,P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2)\cdots P(w_n \mid w_1, \dots, w_{n-1}).$$

This is not an approximation: any probability for sentences can be written this way. The whole difficulty lies in estimating each factor, the probability of the next word given everything before it.

4. Counting Word Pairs

The simplest estimate looks only at the previous word. Count how often each pair of words, each bigram, occurs in a large text, and divide:

$$P(\text{next} \mid \text{previous}) \approx \frac{\text{count}(\text{previous},\ \text{next})}{\text{count}(\text{previous})}.$$

In the toy world, “cat” occurs 110 times in the training text, followed by “eats” 31 times, so P(eats | cat) = 31/110 = 0.28. Looking back two words (a trigram) or three (a 4-gram) gives sharper predictions, because more of the context is used.

After “the”, almost any noun or adjective may follow, and the most likely word, “bread”, has a probability of only 0.09. After “eats the”, only foods remain. After “cat drinks the”, a 4-gram model knows that cats drink only milk and water. Notice that the model has no idea what a cat or milk is. It knows only which words have followed which.

5. Longer Memories: n-Gram Models

A model that uses the previous n − 1 words is an n-gram model. It makes the Markov assumption: the next word depends only on that short window, and everything earlier is forgotten. With this assumption, a model can also write: start a sentence, draw the next word at random in proportion to its probability, append it, and repeat. Shannon did exactly this by hand in 1948, producing text that grew more English-like as he used longer contexts. [2]

With no context (n = 1), words appear at their overall frequencies in random order. With n = 2, every adjacent pair is plausible but the sentence as a whole may not be: “the teacher eats the garden”. With n = 3 the sentences are mostly sensible, and 31% are copies of training sentences. With n = 4, 65% are copies, and with n = 6, 85%. A long-context count model becomes fluent by memorising: its contexts are so specific that only the continuation it has seen remains. That is the first hint of a tension that runs through this series, between reproducing the training data and generalising from it.

6. Running Out of Data

Why not simply use long contexts and lots of text? Because the number of possible word sequences explodes. With a vocabulary of V words there are Vn possible n-grams: for V = 50,000, there are 2.5 billion possible bigrams and 1.25 × 1014 trigrams. Most perfectly good sentences contain word combinations that never appeared in any training text, and a count model gives them probability zero. This is data sparsity.

Even in this tiny, repetitive world, a model trained on 100 sentences has never seen 10.5% of the trigrams in the test sentences, and 33.8% of the 6-grams. More data helps, but only slowly: language keeps producing new combinations. In 2007 Thorsten Brants and colleagues at Google trained n-gram models on up to two trillion words for machine translation, calling them “large language models”, and found that translation kept improving as the data grew. [5] Counting can be pushed far, but it never escapes sparsity.

7. Smoothing and Perplexity

The classic fix is smoothing: take a little probability from seen events and give it to unseen ones. The simplest version, add-k smoothing, pretends every possible pair was seen k extra times. Better methods back off to shorter contexts when a long one is unseen, as Slava Katz proposed in 1987, [6] or blend contexts of all lengths; Reinhard Kneser and Hermann Ney’s 1995 method, which asks how many different contexts a word has appeared in, became the best of these. [7, 8] Such models powered speech recognition at IBM from the 1970s, in Frederick Jelinek’s group, and machine translation for decades. [9]

To compare models we need a score. Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-probability that a model gives to each word of a test text. It can be read as the number of equally likely words the model is choosing between at each step: lower is better.

Ignoring the previous word altogether (a unigram model) gives a perplexity of 17.8 on the toy test sentences. A bigram model trained on 800 sentences reaches 3.6. Trained on only 50 sentences, it needs smoothing: with k = 0.0001 its perplexity is 8.0, with k = 0.01 it improves to 5.6, and with k = 10 it worsens to 23, because every word has become almost equally likely. Choosing the smoothing is choosing how much to trust the counts.

The maths: perplexity

For a test text of N words,

$$\text{PP} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln P(w_i \mid w_1, \dots, w_{i-1})\Big).$$

This is the exponential of the cross-entropy loss of Inside the Machine Part 7. A model that spreads probability evenly over V words has perplexity V; a model that always gives the true next word probability 1 has perplexity 1.

In code: a bigram model in a few lines of Python 
import math, random
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

train = ["the cat drinks the milk .", "the dog eats the bread .",
         "the cat eats the fish .", "the horse sleeps in the field ."]
pairs = defaultdict(Counter)
for s in train:
    w = ["<s>"] + s.split()
    for a, b in zip(w, w[1:]):
        pairs[a][b] += 1

def prob(a, b, k=0.01, V=20):           # add-k smoothing
    return (pairs[a][b] + k) / (sum(pairs[a].values()) + k * V)

def generate():
    w, out = "<s>", []
    while w != ".":
        w = random.choices(list(pairs[w]), weights=pairs[w].values())[0]
        out.append(w)
    return " ".join(out)

print(prob("cat", "drinks"), generate())

8. What Counting Cannot Do

Counting models hit three walls:

  • No notion of similarity. To a count model, “cat” and “dog” are as different as “cat” and “tea”. Seeing “the dog drinks the water” a thousand times tells it nothing about “the horse drinks the water”, which in our toy world never appears in training.
  • Short memory. In “the keys that the old man left on the table are …”, choosing “are” rather than “is” depends on “keys”, eight words back, far beyond any practical n.
  • Sparsity. Most long contexts have never been seen, so the model falls back to short ones and loses the meaning.

The breakthrough, described in Part 2, was to stop treating words as unrelated symbols. In 2003 Yoshua Bengio and colleagues gave each word a learned vector of numbers and let a neural network predict the next word from those vectors, so that what is learned about cats can transfer to horses. [10] Part 3 then shows how attention lets a model look back over thousands of words at once.

9. Open Questions

Open questions: how predictable is language?

Shannon estimated that English carries between about 0.6 and 1.3 bits of information per letter, depending on how much context his human guessers used. [4] The true value is still unknown, because it would require a perfect predictor. Every improvement in language models lowers the upper bound, and the gap between today’s models and that unknown limit is one way to ask how much of language, and of the knowledge expressed in it, they have yet to capture.

10. Worked Example: The Probability of a Sentence

Using the bigram counts of the toy world, without smoothing, score “the hungry cat eats the fish .”:

  1. Every sentence starts with “the”: P(the | start) = 800/800 = 1.
  2. P(hungry | the) = 51/1,773 = 0.029.
  3. P(cat | hungry) = 6/51 = 0.118.
  4. P(eats | cat) = 31/110 = 0.282, and P(the | eats) = 347/347 = 1.
  5. P(fish | the) = 14/1,773 = 0.008, and P(. | fish) = 12/14 = 0.857.
  6. Multiplying: P(sentence) ≈ 6.5 × 10−6. Even a perfectly ordinary sentence has a tiny probability, because probability is shared among an enormous number of possible sentences. That is why language models work with logarithms of probabilities.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “language models just paste together sentences they have seen”

The myth: an LLM is a giant lookup table that retrieves and stitches together text from its training data.

The reality: that describes a long-context n-gram model, which, as Figure 4 shows, becomes fluent mainly by copying. Modern models are different. R. Thomas McCoy and colleagues found that text generated by neural language models is largely novel: most of its longer word sequences do not appear in the training data, and the models often use words in new grammatical combinations, though they do occasionally copy long passages. [11] Nicholas Carlini and colleagues showed that models can memorise and regurgitate some rare training text word for word, such as contact details that appear only a few times, which matters for privacy. [12]

Why it matters: LLMs both generalise and memorise. Which one they are doing in a given answer is not visible from the outside, a theme Part 6 returns to.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • A language model assigns a probability to every possible next word; by the chain rule, these give the probability of any text.
  • n-gram models estimate those probabilities by counting how often words follow the previous n − 1 words.
  • Generating text means repeatedly drawing a next word from those probabilities.
  • Longer contexts give more fluent text but need exponentially more data, and generation drifts into copying.
  • Smoothing and back-off share probability with unseen sequences; perplexity measures how well a model predicts new text.
  • Counting has no notion of similarity and a short memory: the problems that word vectors (Part 2) and attention (Part 3) solve.
