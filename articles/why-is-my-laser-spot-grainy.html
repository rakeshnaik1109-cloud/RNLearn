Why Is My Laser Spot Grainy? Speckle, Stray Fringes and Other Gremlins of the Optics Lab

Speckle, unwanted fringes, ghost spots and rings around dust: most of the gremlins of the optics lab share one cause. Here is what it is, how to recognise each problem, and how to make it go away.

1. The Grainy Spot

Shine a laser pointer at a plain white wall and look closely at the spot. It is not a smooth red disc. It is a restless field of bright and dark grains that seems to sparkle and shimmer, and it changes when you move your head. Now shine a torch at the same wall. The patch of light is perfectly smooth. Same wall, same eyes: so why does the laser look grainy?

The grains are called speckle, and they are one of the first surprises anyone meets in a laser laboratory. They are also a good introduction to a whole family of problems that make optical experiments harder than they look: unwanted stripes that drift across an image, ghostly second spots, rings around specks of dust, lasers that become noisy when you add a lens, and fringes that vanish when someone walks past the table.

This tutorial explains where these "gremlins" come from and how to get rid of them. The surprising thing is how many of them share a single cause. The property that makes laser light so useful, its ability to interfere with itself, is the same property that creates most of these problems. Once you understand that, you can usually work out what is going on in your own experiment, and which trick will cure it. We will finish with the hurdles that have other causes, a troubleshooting guide, and the clever ways scientists have turned some of these "noises" into useful signals.

3. Coherence in Five Minutes

Light is a wave. When two light waves meet, they add up. If their crests arrive together, they reinforce each other and make a bright spot; if a crest meets a trough, they cancel and make a dark spot. This is interference, and the pattern of bright and dark bands it produces is called a set of fringes. [2, 3]

But interference only shows up if the two waves keep a steady relationship with each other. Light from a light bulb is a jumble of countless short wave trains, each lasting a tiny fraction of a nanosecond, with random starting points. Two parts of such a beam keep in step only if they have travelled almost exactly the same distance. If one part has taken a longer route, it meets a different, unrelated wave train, the bright and dark spots shift around randomly billions of times a second, and all we see is the average: no fringes at all. The property of keeping in step is called coherence.

There are two kinds. Temporal coherence asks how much extra path one wave can travel and still interfere with the other. This distance is the coherence length, and it depends on how pure the colour is. A source with wavelength λ and spectral width Δλ has a coherence length of roughly Lc ≈ λ²/Δλ. White light, spread over hundreds of nanometres, has a coherence length of about a micrometre. A single-frequency laser can have a coherence length of hundreds of metres. Spatial coherence asks whether light from different points across the beam keeps in step. A laser beam, coming from a tiny "point" source, is spatially coherent; light from a frosted bulb, coming from a large glowing area, is not. [3, 28]

Hold on to this figure, because it explains most of what follows. Every unwanted pattern in this tutorial needs two (or more) waves that have travelled different distances, and a source whose coherence length is longer than that difference. That gives us two general strategies: stop the second wave from existing (or from reaching the detector), or make sure it is no longer coherent with the first.

4. Speckle: Light Interfering with Itself

A wall, a sheet of paper or a painted surface looks smooth to our fingers, but on the scale of a light wave (about half a micrometre) it is a mountain range. Every tiny patch of the surface scatters a little of the laser light towards your eye. Each patch is at a slightly different height, so the waves from different patches arrive with essentially random phases. At any point on your retina, hundreds of these waves add together. [1, 7]

Adding waves with random phases is like a random walk. Imagine each wave as an arrow, all the same length, pointing in a random direction. Lay them head to tail. Sometimes, by chance, many arrows point roughly the same way, and the total is long: a bright grain. Often they mostly cancel, and the total is short: a dark grain. A tiny step to one side on your retina, the arrows are slightly different, and so is the total. The result is a random pattern of bright and dark grains.

The statistics of this random walk were worked out in detail by Joseph Goodman, and they have some striking consequences. [7, 1]

  • Dark is the most common brightness. In "fully developed" speckle, the brightness follows a negative exponential distribution: the darkest values are the most likely, and very bright grains are rare.
  • The contrast is 100 per cent. The speckle contrast, defined as the spread of the brightness divided by its average, equals 1. Speckle is not a faint blemish on the image; it is as strong as the image itself.
  • The grain size is set by the optics, not by the surface. Speckle formed in the space in front of a surface ("objective" speckle) has grains of about λz/D, where D is the size of the illuminated spot and z the viewing distance. Speckle seen through a lens or eye ("subjective" speckle) has grains about the size of the smallest spot the lens can resolve, roughly the wavelength divided by the lens’s numerical aperture. Closing down a camera's aperture makes the grains bigger.

Grainy patterns of this kind were studied long before the laser: physicists such as Karl Exner and Max von Laue described them in light scattered by many particles. [1] But with ordinary light they were faint and fleeting. Within two years of the first laser in 1960, everyone was seeing them. In 1962 J. D. Rigden and E. I. Gordon at Bell Telephone Laboratories described the "granularity of scattered optical maser light", and Bernard Oliver explained the "sparkling spots" as random diffraction the following year. [4, 5, 6]

Speckle is not only about walls. It appears whenever coherent light passes through or reflects from anything random: ground glass, a cloudy liquid, biological tissue, the inside of a multimode optical fibre, or even the dust on a lens. It is the bane of laser projectors, holograms and laser-illuminated microscopes, and it limits how precisely a laser beam's position can be measured on a rough target.

5. Try It Yourself: Speckle and Your Eyesight

Try it yourself: does the speckle move with you?

Shine a low-power laser pointer at a matte (not shiny) wall a few metres away. Without glasses, look at the spot and move your head slowly from side to side.

  • If the speckle pattern seems to move the opposite way to your head, you are probably short-sighted.
  • If it moves the same way as your head, you are probably long-sighted.
  • If it stays still, your eye is focused on the wall.

Now put on your glasses, if you wear them: the motion should mostly stop. The speckle is formed in the space in front of your eye, so it is not "on" the wall. Your eye focuses on one plane, and when that plane is in front of or behind the wall, moving your head produces a parallax, just as a nearby lamp post shifts against distant hills when you walk past. The effect has been used to make simple eye tests. [8]

Safety: use only a low-power pointer (class 1 or 2), point it at a matte wall, and never look into the beam or at its reflection from a mirror, glass or a shiny surface. This is a demonstration, not a substitute for an eye examination.

6. How to Get Rid of Speckle

A rough surface always gives a speckle pattern with coherent light; we cannot stop the waves interfering. What we can do is add up many different speckle patterns, so that the bright grains of one fall on the dark grains of another. If we average N patterns that are truly independent of each other, the contrast falls as 1/√N. [1, 9] That square root is the catch: halving the speckle takes four patterns, but cutting it to a tenth takes a hundred. Every method of reducing speckle is a way of producing many independent patterns within one exposure of the camera or the eye:

  • Move something. A rotating or vibrating diffuser in the beam, a vibrating multimode fibre, or a moving projection screen gives a new speckle pattern every few microseconds, and a camera exposure averages them. This is the most common laboratory cure. Commercial "speckle reducers" are often a diffuser on a wobbling mount.
  • Use several wavelengths. A different colour gives a different pattern, if the surface roughness changes the paths by more than the new coherence length. Broad-spectrum sources such as LEDs and superluminescent diodes produce very little speckle for exactly this reason (see Figure 2).
  • Use two polarisations. Light polarised one way and light polarised the other way do not interfere, so they make two independent patterns: contrast falls to 1/√2, about 0.71.
  • Use several angles. Light arriving from different directions makes different patterns. Laser projectors combine angle, polarisation and wavelength diversity. [9]
  • Average in software. If you can take many images while changing the speckle (for example, by slightly moving the sample or the diffuser), averaging them afterwards works too.
  • Use bigger pixels or a bigger aperture. If many speckle grains fall within one pixel, each pixel averages them. This is why speckle is less obvious with a wide-open camera lens.
The trade-off

Every one of these cures works by destroying coherence, or by averaging over it. That is fine for illumination and imaging, but fatal for experiments that need coherence, such as interferometry, holography or coupling light into a single-mode fibre. A rotating diffuser also scatters light into a wider cone, so some power is lost. Choose the cure that removes the coherence you do not need while keeping the coherence you do.

7. Unwanted Fringes: The Accidental Etalon

Look at a laser beam on a camera, and you will often see faint stripes or rings that drift slowly across the image as the room warms or cools. These are usually caused by an accidental etalon.

Whenever light passes through a piece of glass, a little is reflected at each surface: about 4 per cent at each uncoated glass–air surface. A small part of the light therefore bounces back from the second surface, back again from the first, and finally goes forward with the main beam. It has travelled an extra distance of twice the glass thickness times its refractive index, 2nd. If that extra distance is shorter than the coherence length, the twice-reflected wave interferes with the main beam. Isaac Newton saw the same effect in the coloured rings between a lens and a flat plate, and in 1899 Charles Fabry and Alfred Pérot turned two parallel, partly reflecting surfaces into a precise instrument now called a Fabry–Pérot etalon. [10, 2] Every window, filter, cover slip, beamsplitter, lens and camera cover glass in your setup is a weak etalon, whether you want it or not.

Two features make these fringes so troublesome. First, they are surprisingly strong: two uncoated surfaces with 4 per cent reflection give a ripple of about 15–16 per cent peak to peak, because interference adds amplitudes, not brightnesses, and the square root of 4 per cent is 20 per cent. Second, they drift. One full fringe passes whenever the optical thickness changes by half a wavelength, a few hundred nanometres. For a window 1 cm thick, a few degrees of warming is enough; so is a slight tilt. A slowly drifting stripe pattern across your data is very often an etalon.

The cures, roughly in order of preference:

  • Anti-reflection (AR) coatings. Thin layers designed to cancel the reflection at one wavelength or over a band. A single magnesium fluoride layer reduces the reflection to about 1–1.5 per cent; a multilayer "V-coat" optimised for one laser wavelength can reach below 0.25 per cent. Because the fringe strength depends on the amplitude, cutting the reflection by 16 times cuts the ripple by 16 times. [11]
  • Wedged optics. Windows and beamsplitters whose two faces are not parallel, typically by about half a degree. The twice-reflected beam leaves at an angle, separates from the main beam, and can no longer interfere with it.
  • A small tilt. Tilting a window or a filter by a few degrees sends the reflections off sideways. (Be careful: tilting an interference filter shifts its colour.)
  • Brewster windows. At Brewster’s angle, about 56° for glass, light polarised in the plane of incidence is not reflected at all. Gas lasers have used Brewster windows for decades. [12]
  • Index matching. A drop of fluid with the same refractive index as the glass, between a sample and a cover slip for example, removes the reflection at that surface entirely.
  • Shorter coherence length. If you do not need a long coherence length, use a source that does not have one. A thick window gives no fringes with a superluminescent diode.
  • Keep the temperature steady. If an etalon cannot be removed, at least stop it from drifting.

8. Ghosts and Back-Reflections

The same small surface reflections cause two more problems.

Ghosts. A double reflection inside a lens or window can form a faint second image or spot, slightly displaced from the real one. In a camera this appears as a ghost or a lens flare; in a laser setup it appears as a weak second beam that can hit a detector, interfere with the main beam, or make a stray spot that looks like a signal. A ghost usually moves differently from the real image when you tilt the guilty optic, which is how you find it. The cures are the same as for etalons: AR coatings, wedges and tilts. Unwanted stray beams should be caught in a beam dump, a black, light-absorbing trap, rather than left to bounce around the room.

Back-reflections into the laser. Lasers, especially semiconductor diode lasers, are extremely sensitive to light reflected back into them. Even a fraction of a per cent of returning light can make the laser jump between frequencies ("mode hops"), increase its intensity noise, broaden or narrow its linewidth unpredictably, or in extreme cases become chaotic. [14, 15] A classic sign is that the laser becomes noisy as soon as you put a lens or fibre in front of it.

The cures:

  • An optical isolator. This is a one-way valve for light, based on an effect Michael Faraday discovered in 1845: a magnetic field along a piece of special glass rotates the polarisation of light passing through it. [13] The clever part is that the rotation is in the same direction whichever way the light travels. Light going forward through a polariser is rotated by 45° and passes a second polariser set at 45°. Reflected light coming back is rotated by another 45°, arrives at 90° to the first polariser, and is blocked. A single isolator typically cuts the returning light by a factor of a thousand to ten thousand (30–40 decibels).
  • Angled fibre connectors. The flat end of an ordinary fibre connector reflects light straight back. "APC" connectors are polished at an angle of 8°, so the reflected light leaves the fibre core at an angle and is lost. They are usually coloured green.
  • Tilt everything slightly. No surface should be exactly perpendicular to the beam unless it must be.

9. Dust Rings and the Spatial Filter

Look carefully at an expanded laser beam on a white card and you will often see sets of concentric rings, like little targets, scattered over it. Each one is caused by a speck of dust, a scratch or a fingerprint on one of the optics. The speck scatters a small wave that spreads out in all directions and interferes with the main beam, drawing a "bullseye" of bright and dark rings. It is the same diffraction that produces the famous bright spot in the centre of a round shadow, predicted by Fresnel's wave theory and found by Arago in 1818. [2]

The first cure is to clean the optics, gently and with the right materials (dry blowing first, then lens tissue and solvent, never a dry wipe that grinds grit into a coating). But no beam is perfectly clean, so a second tool is used constantly: the spatial filter.

The idea is that a lens sorts light by direction. A smooth, parallel beam is focused to a tiny spot in the lens’s focal plane. Light that has been scattered by dust is travelling at slight angles, so it is focused to points away from the centre. A pinhole placed exactly at the focus passes the clean beam and blocks the scattered light. A second lens then makes the beam parallel again. A pinhole about one and a half to two times the diameter of the focused spot passes almost all the power while removing most of the noise. [3, 2] Spatial filters are delicate to align: the pinhole must be positioned to within a few micrometres. But the clean, smooth beam they produce is worth the effort for holography, interferometry and imaging.

10. Gremlins in Optical Fibres

Optical fibres are wonderfully convenient, but they bring their own problems.

  • Modal noise. A multimode fibre has a large core in which light can travel along many different paths ("modes"), each with a slightly different length. At the far end these paths interfere, producing a speckle pattern. If the fibre is bent, warmed or touched, the path lengths change and the speckle pattern shifts. Anything that does not capture all the light equally, such as a small detector, a connector with a slight offset, or a polariser, then sees the power fluctuate. Richard Epworth named this modal noise in 1978. [17] The cures are a single-mode fibre, a broadband source (whose coherence is too short for the paths to interfere), capturing all the light, or a deliberately vibrated fibre that averages the speckle.
  • Polarisation changes. An ordinary single-mode fibre does not keep light's polarisation. Bends, twists, stress and temperature changes turn it in unpredictable ways, so the light that leaves the fibre can drift between polarisation states as the fibre moves. After a polariser or a polarisation-sensitive component, this appears as a slowly drifting power. The cures are polarisation-maintaining fibre, which has built-in stress that holds one polarisation, taping fibres down, and controlling the temperature.
  • Back-reflections at connectors. Flat connectors reflect light back towards the laser (section 8); angled APC connectors avoid this.
  • Cladding light. Light launched into a fibre's cladding instead of its core can travel some distance and then leak out, adding a hazy background. A short loop of tightly coiled fibre, or a "cladding mode stripper", removes it.

11. Shaking, Heat and Stray Light

Not every gremlin comes from coherence. Some come from the plain fact that optical experiments measure distances to a fraction of a wavelength.

Vibration and air currents. In an interferometer, moving a mirror by half a wavelength, about 300 nanometres, shifts the fringe pattern by one whole fringe. That is about a hundredth of the width of a human hair. Footsteps, traffic, pumps, fans and air conditioning shake ordinary benches by far more than that. If the fringes move during a camera exposure, they blur and the contrast drops; if they move between measurements, the data jump around. Moving air is almost as bad, because warm and cool air bend light by slightly different amounts. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley floated their interferometer on a massive stone slab resting on a bath of mercury, to isolate it from the vibrations of the building. [18] Today the same idea is built into the optical table: a heavy, stiff, damped honeycomb table resting on air-filled legs that soak up vibrations from the floor.

The cures are a good optical table, short and rigid mounts close to the table surface, covers or tubes around the beam paths to stop air currents, keeping people and fans away during measurements, and short exposures or fast measurements where possible.

Temperature drift. Metal mounts expand and contract with temperature, so alignment slowly "walks" over hours. Lasers change power and wavelength as they warm up, and etalon fringes drift. A good rule is to switch the laser on well before measuring, keep the room temperature steady, and record a reference measurement (for example, a small part of the beam split off to a second detector) so that you can correct for power drift. In high-power lasers the beam itself heats the optics, making them act as weak lenses that change the focus as the power changes: this is called thermal lensing. [29]

Stray light. Room lights, computer screens, indicator LEDs and scattered laser light can all reach a sensitive detector. Turning off the lights helps, but the professional answers are black baffles and tubes, filters that pass only the laser wavelength, and lock-in detection: switching the laser on and off at a known frequency and measuring only the part of the signal that follows it.

Polarisation surprises. Mirrors used at an angle change the polarisation of light slightly, and stressed glass in lenses and windows acts as a weak wave plate. If an experiment depends on polarisation, check it at several points along the beam, not just at the laser.

12. The Detector Lies Too

Finally, the camera or detector at the end of the experiment can create its own artifacts.

  • Saturation. Every detector has a maximum signal. Above it, peaks are flattened, and an intensity measurement is simply wrong. In some cameras, an over-exposed pixel spills charge into its neighbours, producing streaks called blooming. A simple test: halve the light with a filter. If the signal does not halve, the detector was saturated.
  • Nonlinearity. Even below saturation, a detector's response may not be exactly proportional to the light. Check it with calibrated filters before trusting ratios of brightness.
  • Hot pixels, dark current and fixed patterns. Some pixels always read high, and every pixel adds a little signal even in the dark. Taking a "dark frame" with the light blocked, and subtracting it, removes most of this.
  • Aliasing and moiré. When an image contains detail finer than the pixel spacing, the camera records false, wavy patterns. The rule is that the finest detail you want to see must cover at least two pixels.
  • Etalons inside the camera. A camera's protective cover glass is an etalon (section 7). Some thin, back-illuminated sensors also show fringes in the near infrared, where the silicon becomes partly transparent and the sensor itself acts as an etalon.
  • Single-photon detectors. Detectors that count individual photons, used in quantum optics and very faint-light experiments, register occasional "dark counts" with no light at all, and some produce false "afterpulses" shortly after a real detection. Both must be measured and accounted for, and stray light must be controlled with extreme care, since a single stray photon is a signal. [19]

13. When Noise Becomes the Signal

One scientist's noise is another's data. Several important techniques work precisely because of speckle and coherence.

  • Speckle interferometry in astronomy. The atmosphere breaks a star's image into a cloud of speckles that changes many times a second. In 1970 Antoine Labeyrie showed that by taking many very short exposures and analysing the speckle statistics mathematically, astronomers could recover detail down to the telescope's full theoretical resolution. In 1971 the method was used on the 200-inch telescope at Mount Palomar to study bright stars at the telescope’s full resolution. [20, 21]
  • Electronic speckle pattern interferometry (ESPI). Compare the speckle pattern of an object before and after it is stressed, and the differences reveal deformations smaller than a micrometre. Developed at Loughborough by J. N. Butters and J. A. Leendertz around 1971, ESPI is used to test car parts, aircraft panels and works of art. [22]
  • Seeing blood flow. When laser light falls on living tissue, moving blood cells make the speckle pattern flicker. During a camera exposure, the flicker blurs the speckle, so its contrast drops where blood flows fastest. Fercher and Briers used this in 1981 to map blood flow in the retina, and today laser speckle contrast imaging is used to watch blood flow in the brain and skin. [23, 24]
  • Seeing through fog. Light passing through a layer of white paint becomes a speckle pattern, not a focus. In 2007 Ivo Vellekoop and Allard Mosk shaped the incoming laser light with a programmable device so that all the scattered waves arrived in step at one point behind the layer, turning the speckle into a single bright focus. This "wavefront shaping" is now an active field of research aimed at imaging deep inside biological tissue. [25]

And, of course, the very same interference that creates unwanted etalon fringes is the basis of precise interferometers, laser cavities and optical filters. The physics is the same; what matters is whether you designed it in or let it sneak in.

14. What’s That Artifact? A Troubleshooting Guide

When something odd appears in your data, choose the symptom below for its likely cause, a quick test and the usual fixes.

A few general habits prevent most problems before they start:

  1. Ask "what interferes with what?" For every pattern, find the two waves and their path difference, then compare it with the coherence length of your source.
  2. Change one thing at a time: tap, tilt, block or warm one component and watch what happens. An artifact that follows a component belongs to that component.
  3. Use the least coherent source that does the job, and AR-coated, wedged optics wherever possible.
  4. Tilt surfaces slightly, dump every stray beam, and isolate lasers from back-reflections.
  5. Keep it clean, stiff, short and covered: clean optics, rigid mounts, short beam paths and enclosures against air currents.
  6. Measure the background: take dark frames, block the beam, and check that the detector responds linearly.
Stray beams are a safety problem too

An unexpected reflection is not only bad for data: from a powerful laser it can blind. Keep beams at a fixed height well below eye level, never bend down to the level of the beam, remove watches and rings, block every stray reflection with a beam dump, and wear eye protection rated for your laser's wavelength and power. Follow your laboratory's laser safety rules and the international standard for laser classes. [27]

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: is speckle a sign of dirty optics or a bad laser?

The legend: speckle means your optics are dirty or your laser is poor, and a better, "cleaner" laser will make it go away.

The record: it is the other way round. Speckle appears with perfectly clean optics, because it comes from the roughness of the surface being illuminated, and it is strongest with the best lasers. A single-frequency laser with a long coherence length produces speckle of the full 100 per cent contrast. Cheaper multimode lasers, which emit several wavelengths at once, can produce slightly less, if the surface is rough enough for the different wavelengths to make different patterns. [1]

The grain of truth: dust and scratches really do add patterns of their own: the ring patterns of section 9. They are a different effect from speckle, with a different cure.

A second legend: "the speckle is on the wall". It is not. It is formed in the space in front of the wall and depends on where you look from, which is why it moves when you move your head (section 5), and why two people see different speckle patterns on the same spot.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: artifact or discovery?

Every measuring instrument adds something of its own to what it measures. The difficult question is always: is this pattern telling me about the world, or about my apparatus?

History has painful examples. In 1990 the newly launched Hubble Space Telescope turned out to have a mirror ground very precisely to the wrong shape. The investigation traced it to a test instrument that had been set up using a laser reflection: the laser was supposed to reflect from the end of a measuring rod, but it reflected from a worn spot in the black paint on a cap over the rod instead. A lens in the test instrument ended up about 1.3 mm out of position. Other tests hinted at the problem, but they were set aside because the main instrument was trusted. [16] In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos apparently travelling faster than light. Among the faults later identified was a badly connected optical fibre cable in the timing system, and the corrected measurement agreed with the speed of light. [26]

Both stories show the same lesson: an artifact is most dangerous when it gives the answer you expect, or a very exciting one. Good experimenters therefore try to break their own results: they change one component, repeat with a different method, and ask what else could produce the same pattern. The speckle story adds a twist: the same pattern can be noise in one experiment and the measurement itself in another. Whether something is "signal" or "noise" is not a property of the light. It depends on the question you are asking.

Questions to think about: If every instrument adds artifacts, how can we ever be sure we are seeing the world rather than our tools? Is it possible to know you have found all the artifacts in an experiment?
