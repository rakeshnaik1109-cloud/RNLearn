Weighing the Earth: Pendulums, a Scottish Mountain and Two Lead Balls

You cannot put the Earth on a set of scales. Yet a mountain in Scotland and two lead balls in a locked room revealed how heavy it is, and with it the mass of the Sun and every planet.

1. Weighing the Unweighable

How heavy is the Earth? You cannot put it on a set of scales: there is nothing to stand the scales on, and nothing to compare it with. Yet today we can say that the Earth's mass is about 5.97 × 1024 kilograms, six million billion billion kilograms, and that its average density is 5.51 times that of water.

This part of the series tells how that was done. It is a story of falling objects and swinging pendulums, of an astronomer camping for four months on a Scottish mountain, of a shy English aristocrat watching two lead balls through a telescope from outside a locked room, and of a number, the gravitational constant G, that is still the most stubbornly uncertain constant in physics. It is also the story of how one measurement in a basement weighed not just the Earth, but the Sun, the Moon and every planet.

3. Falling Faster and Faster: Galileo

The first step was to understand falling itself. Around 1604, using balls rolling down gentle slopes to slow the motion enough to time it, Galileo found that a falling object does not move at a steady speed: it speeds up steadily, so that the distance covered grows with the square of the time. In twice the time it falls four times as far. He also argued that, apart from air resistance, heavy and light objects fall together. [1]

The steady increase in speed is called the acceleration due to gravity, g. Near the Earth's surface it is about 9.8 metres per second per second: every second, a falling object gains about 9.8 m/s (35 km/h) of speed. The most accurate way to measure g for the next three centuries was a pendulum, whose swing depends only on its length and on g.

4. Newton's Law and a Missing Number

In 1687 Isaac Newton published his law of universal gravitation: every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. In modern notation, F = GMm/r². In Part 3 we saw how Newton tested it by comparing the fall of an apple with the "fall" of the Moon. [2]

The law seems to offer a way to weigh the Earth. At the surface, g = GM/R², where R is the Earth's radius (known from Part 2) and g is measured with pendulums. But there is a catch. Every measurement of falling or orbiting gives only the product G × M. To separate them, we need to know G, the strength of gravity between two ordinary masses, and that requires measuring the gravitational pull of something whose mass we already know. The trouble is that gravity between ordinary objects is incredibly weak. Two people standing a metre apart attract each other with a force less than the weight of a grain of sand.

Newton thought such a measurement impossible. Instead, he made an inspired guess. Rocks at the surface are about twice as dense as water, and rocks from deep mines are denser still, so, he reasoned, the whole Earth is probably "five or six times" as dense as water. [2] The modern value is 5.51. It would take more than a century to show that the guess was right.

5. A Mountain in Scotland: Schiehallion

Newton had pointed out that a large mountain should pull a plumb line slightly towards it. If you could measure that deflection, and estimate the mountain's mass from its size and rock density, you could compare the mountain's pull with the whole Earth's, and so find the Earth's density. A French expedition to the Andes in the 1730s had seen hints of the effect, but under very difficult conditions.

In 1774 the Astronomer Royal, Nevil Maskelyne, chose Schiehallion, a mountain in the Scottish Highlands with a neat, symmetrical ridge. He set up observatories on its north and south slopes and spent about four months there, in miserable weather, measuring the direction of the vertical at each station against the stars. At the same time, surveyors measured the true distance between the stations. Comparing the two showed that the plumb lines on either side were pulled towards the mountain by a combined angle of about 11.6 seconds of arc. [3, 6]

The hard part was calculating the mountain's attraction. The mathematician Charles Hutton divided the mountain into thousands of small blocks and added up the pull of each one. To organise the survey heights, he joined points of equal height with lines, inventing the contour lines now found on every map. In 1778 he concluded that the Earth's average density is about 1.8 times the mountain's; assuming rock of density 2.5, that gave about 4.5 times the density of water. [4] Since surface rocks are much lighter than this, the Earth must have a dense interior, probably metallic. (A modern reanalysis of Maskelyne's data, with better knowledge of the mountain's rocks, gives about 5.5. [18])

6. Cavendish and the Lead Balls

The mountain method depended on guessing the density of the mountain's rocks. A better approach was to measure the attraction between masses that could be weighed exactly. The Yorkshire clergyman and natural philosopher John Michell designed an apparatus for this, but he died in 1793 before he could use it. It passed to Henry Cavendish, a wealthy, painfully shy scientist who rebuilt it and, in 1797–98, carried out one of the most famous experiments in physics. [5, 6]

The heart of the apparatus was a torsion balance: a wooden rod 1.8 m long with a small lead ball (0.73 kg) at each end, hung horizontally from a thin wire. Two large lead balls, each about 158 kg, could be swung close to the small ones from outside. Their gravitational pull drew the small balls towards them and twisted the wire very slightly. By timing the slow natural swing of the rod, Cavendish could work out how easily the wire twisted; from the size of the twist, he could then calculate the force between the balls, and compare it with the force of the whole Earth on the same small balls (their weight).

The forces were tiny, about one fifty-millionth of the weight of the small balls, so small that a slight difference in temperature would set up air currents strong enough to swamp them. Cavendish therefore enclosed the balance in a sealed room, moved the large balls with ropes and pulleys from outside, and read the position of the rod through telescopes set into the walls, using a lamp to light a fine scale. He published his result in 1798: the Earth's average density is 5.48 times that of water. A small slip in his arithmetic, noticed later, means that his own data actually give about 5.45. Either way, it is within about 1 per cent of the modern value of 5.51. [5, 19] Newton's guess had been right.

7. From Density to G

Cavendish's paper was called "Experiments to Determine the Density of the Earth", and that is what he gave: a density, not a value of G. In his day, scientists did not usually write the law of gravity with a separate constant at all. Only later in the nineteenth century, as physicists adopted consistent units for force and mass, did it become natural to express the result as the gravitational constant. Calculated from Cavendish's data, it is about 6.74 × 10−11 in modern units, about 1 per cent high. [19]

The Victorians improved the measurement in different ways. In 1891 John Henry Poynting used an ordinary but very sensitive beam balance, measuring the tiny extra weight of a mass when a large lead sphere was placed beneath it; he found an Earth density of about 5.49. In 1895 Charles Vernon Boys built a miniature torsion balance, hanging tiny gold balls from a fibre of quartz so thin that it could twist with extraordinary sensitivity, and measured G as 6.658 × 10−11. [8, 7] Boys had drawn his quartz fibres by attaching molten quartz to an arrow and firing it from a crossbow.

8. The Most Stubborn Constant

You might expect that, after more than two centuries, G would be known to many decimal places, like the speed of light. It is not. The internationally recommended value today is G = 6.67430 × 10−11 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², with an uncertainty of about 22 parts per million, by far the largest of any fundamental constant. [9]

The trouble is that the best experiments disagree with each other by much more than their own error estimates. Over the past few decades, careful measurements with torsion balances, beam balances and pendulums have spread over a range of several hundred parts per million. In 2014 a team in Florence measured G in an entirely new way, by dropping clouds of ultra-cold atoms past heavy tungsten masses and measuring their fall with atom interferometry; they got 6.67191 × 10−11, noticeably lower than most torsion-balance results. [10] In 2018 a group at Huazhong University of Science and Technology in Wuhan, China, used two different torsion-balance methods and obtained 6.674184 and 6.674484 × 10−11, each with an uncertainty of about 12 parts per million, yet the two disagree slightly with each other. [11]

Why is it so hard? Gravity is so weak that the force between laboratory masses is easily disturbed by the gravity of nearby objects (including the experimenters), by temperature changes, by electric charges, by imperfections in the fibre, and by the difficulty of knowing exactly where the mass in a lead or tungsten ball is located. The spread probably reflects hidden errors in some experiments, but nobody yet knows which. [12]

9. Gravity Maps: From the Himalaya to Satellites

Gravity is not the same everywhere on Earth. It is about 0.5 per cent stronger at the poles than at the equator (Part 2), weaker on mountain tops, and slightly different above dense ores or light sediments. These small differences turned out to reveal what lies beneath our feet.

In nineteenth-century India, the Great Trigonometrical Survey, led by George Everest, compared positions measured by surveying with positions measured from the stars. Between the stations of Kaliana, near the Himalaya, and Kalianpur in central India, the two methods disagreed by about 5.2 seconds of arc, presumably because the mountains pulled the plumb lines northwards. But when Archdeacon John Pratt of Calcutta calculated the pull of the Himalaya and the Tibetan plateau in 1855, he found it should have been about three times larger. [13] The same year, the Astronomer Royal George Airy proposed the explanation: mountains have deep "roots" of lighter rock that float in denser rock beneath, like icebergs in water, so much of their extra mass is cancelled. Pratt proposed a slightly different version. The idea, later named isostasy, became a foundation of geology. [14, 13]

Today gravity is measured from space. From 2002, the twin GRACE satellites, chasing each other around the Earth about 220 km apart, measured tiny changes in their separation as they passed over regions of stronger or weaker gravity. Their monthly maps of the Earth's gravity have revealed ice melting in Greenland and Antarctica, groundwater being pumped from aquifers, including beneath northern India, and water moving through the seasons. [15] A successor mission, GRACE-FO, continues the work.

10. Weighing the Solar System

The greatest reward of measuring G was that it weighed far more than the Earth. Astronomers had long been able to measure G × M for any body with a moon or a planet circling it, from the size and period of the orbit (Kepler's third law, as extended by Newton). From the Earth's orbit, for example, they knew G times the mass of the Sun. Once G was known, all of these products became masses in kilograms.

The results were revealing. The Sun turned out to be about 333,000 times as massive as the Earth, but only a quarter as dense on average: about 1.4 times the density of water, too light to be solid rock. Jupiter and Saturn are giants but light; Saturn is less dense than water. The Earth is the densest planet of all.

12. Try It Yourself: Measure Gravity with a Pendulum

Try it yourself: find g with a string and a weight
  1. Tie a small heavy object (a nut, a fishing weight or a key) to a thin string, and hang it from a fixed point such as a door frame.
  2. Measure the length from the point of suspension to the centre of the weight. One metre is convenient.
  3. Pull the weight aside by a small angle, no more than about 10°, and let it go.
  4. With a stopwatch (a phone works), time 10 complete swings (there and back). Divide by 10 to get the period T. Repeat a few times and average.
  5. Calculate g = 4π² × length ÷ T². With a 1 m pendulum, T should be about 2.0 seconds and g about 9.8 m/s².

Use Figure 2 to check your numbers. With care you can get within 1 per cent, about the precision of the best pendulum measurements of the early 1700s. Now take the next step that took scientists a century: with the Earth's radius from Part 2 (6,371 km), your value of g gives G × M = gR² ≈ 4 × 1014 m³/s². To turn that into the Earth's mass, you would need Cavendish.

13. How Did They Know? Measuring a Force Smaller than a Grain of Sand

How did they know? Using a slow swing to weigh a tiny force

In Cavendish's apparatus, the pull of each large ball on a small one was about 1.5 × 10−7 newtons, roughly the weight of a fine grain of sand. No spring balance of the time could measure anything like that. Cavendish's solution was to let the apparatus measure itself. [5, 20]

A torsion balance left alone swings slowly back and forth; in Cavendish’s first experiments each swing took about 15 minutes. The time of that swing depends on how stiff the wire is and on how the mass is spread along the rod, both of which can be calculated or measured. So by simply timing the swing, Cavendish found the wire's stiffness, without ever applying a known force to it. Then, with the large balls in place, he measured the centre point of the swing, which shifted by only about 4 millimetres at the ends of the rod. He read the position on finely divided scales to about a quarter of a millimetre. Because the rod was always swinging, he could not wait for it to settle; instead he recorded the extreme points of each swing and calculated the middle.

The same idea, turning a force into a slow, easily timed oscillation, is used in many of the most sensitive instruments ever built, from modern torsion balances to the suspended mirrors of gravitational-wave detectors.

14. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did Cavendish measure G?

The legend: in 1798 Henry Cavendish measured the gravitational constant G.

The record: Cavendish set out to find, and reported, the density of the Earth. His paper never mentions G, which was not yet part of the way physicists wrote the law of gravity. The value of G was calculated from his results much later. [19, 5]

The grain of truth: the two are equivalent. Once you know the Earth's density (and so its mass), G follows from g = GM/R². In that sense, Cavendish's experiment really was the first measurement of G, even though he never called it that.

A second legend: "Cavendish invented the torsion balance." The design came from John Michell, and similar balances had been used by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in the 1780s to measure electric forces.

15. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: is the constant of gravity really constant?

We call G a "constant of nature". But how do we know it has the same value everywhere and always? In 1937 the physicist Paul Dirac noticed some striking coincidences between very large numbers in physics, and suggested that they might be explained if the strength of gravity slowly weakens as the universe ages. [16]

The idea can be tested. If G were changing, the orbits of the planets and of the Moon would slowly change too. The laser ranging to the Moon described in Part 3, which tracks the Moon's distance to millimetres over decades, shows that G changes, if at all, by less than about one part in ten million million per year. [17] Dirac's bold idea in its original form has been ruled out, but the question was a good one: it turned an assumption into something we could measure.

This is a general pattern. Many things we take for granted in science, that the laws of physics are the same in distant galaxies and in the distant past, are assumptions that can be, and are being, tested. Yet we cannot test everything at once: each test relies on other assumptions. Science advances by testing its assumptions one at a time, while trusting the rest for now.

Questions to think about: If a constant of nature did turn out to change, would it still be a "law" of nature? And why is it that the weakest of the forces, gravity, is the one that shapes planets, stars and galaxies?
