Error Bars and Uncertainty: SD, SE, Confidence Intervals and Forest Plots

The same data can carry error bars three times longer or shorter, overlapping bars can still mean a real difference, and 1 in 20 confidence intervals misses. How to read and draw uncertainty.

1. How Sure Are We?

Every measurement and every average comes with uncertainty. A chart that shows only the averages, without any sign of how much they might change if the experiment were repeated, invites readers to over-interpret small differences. Error bars, confidence intervals and uncertainty bands are the standard ways of showing that uncertainty, and they are among the most misread marks in science.

This part explains the three common kinds of error bar, what overlapping bars do and do not tell you, how confidence intervals behave across repeated experiments, how forest plots combine many studies, how to show change with dumbbell and slope charts, and how to draw uncertainty around a fitted line.

3. What Does This Error Bar Mean?

The same symbol, a line with caps through a point, is used for at least three different things. [1]

  • Standard deviation (SD): the typical spread of individual values around the mean. It describes the data, and does not get smaller with more observations.
  • Standard error (SE): the SD divided by the square root of the sample size. It describes how precisely the mean is known, and shrinks as the sample grows. William Gosset, writing as “Student” in 1908, worked out how to use it properly for small samples. [2]
  • Confidence interval (CI): usually 95%, roughly the mean ± 2 SE (a little more for small samples). It is a range produced by a method that captures the true mean in 95% of repeated experiments. [3]
How to read error bars
  1. Find out what the bars are: SD, SE, CI, or something else. If the caption does not say, the figure cannot be interpreted.
  2. Find the sample size, n. SE and CI bars depend on it.
  3. Check whether the groups are independent or the same subjects measured twice; error bars on paired data can hide a consistent effect.
  4. Prefer figures that show the individual data points as well as the bars (Part 5).
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
groups = {"Control": rng.normal(50, 10, 20), "Treated": rng.normal(58, 10, 20)}   # synthetic

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 4), sharey=True)
for ax, kind in zip(axes, ["SD", "SE", "95% CI"]):
    for i, (name, y) in enumerate(groups.items()):
        n, m, sd = y.size, y.mean(), y.std(ddof=1)
        se = sd / np.sqrt(n)
        half = {"SD": sd, "SE": se, "95% CI": stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * se}[kind]
        ax.scatter(np.full(n, i) + rng.uniform(-0.08, 0.08, n), y, s=10, color="lightgray")
        ax.errorbar(i, m, yerr=half, fmt="o", color="#0072B2", capsize=8, lw=2, ms=7)
    ax.set_xticks([0, 1], groups.keys())
    ax.set_xlim(-0.6, 1.6)
    ax.set_title(f"Mean ± {kind}")
axes[0].set_ylabel("Measurement")
plt.suptitle("The same data, three different error bars: always say which one you show")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. When Do Error Bars Overlap?

A common shortcut is to judge whether two groups differ by checking whether their error bars overlap. In 2005 Sarah Belia, Fiona Fidler, Jennifer Williams and Geoff Cumming asked hundreds of authors of articles in psychology, behavioural neuroscience and medical journals to adjust two means until they were just significantly different, and found that many misjudged how much overlap was allowed. [4] The rules depend on the kind of bar.

For two independent groups of similar size: 95% CIs that overlap by no more than about half of one arm’s length correspond to p < 0.05, and CIs that just touch to about p ≈ 0.006. SE bars must be separated by a gap of about one SE for p ≈ 0.05. [1] For paired data, none of these rules apply.

5. The Dance of Confidence Intervals

A single confidence interval looks solid on a chart, but if the same experiment were repeated, the interval would move. Geoff Cumming popularised the phrase “the dance of the confidence intervals” for this variability. [5]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
true_mean, sd, n, reps = 50, 10, 15, 25
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 5))
missed = 0
for k in range(reps):                                   # 25 repeats of the same experiment
    y = rng.normal(true_mean, sd, n)
    m, se = y.mean(), y.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    half = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1) * se
    hit = m - half <= true_mean <= m + half
    missed += not hit
    ax.errorbar(m, k, xerr=half, fmt="o", color="#0072B2" if hit else "#D55E00", capsize=3)
ax.axvline(true_mean, color="black", ls="--")
ax.set_xlabel("Sample mean with 95% confidence interval")
ax.set_ylabel("Repeat of the experiment")
ax.set_title(f"{missed} of {reps} intervals miss the true mean (expected about 5%)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Forest Plots

A forest plot displays the results of several studies of the same question, each as a point estimate with its confidence interval, together with a pooled estimate from a meta-analysis. It is the standard figure of systematic reviews in medicine. [6]

How to read a forest plot
  1. Find the line of no effect: 1 for ratios, 0 for differences. Which side favours the treatment?
  2. Each square is a study’s estimate; its size shows the study’s weight. Each line is its confidence interval.
  3. The diamond is the pooled estimate; if it does not cross the line of no effect, the combined evidence points one way.
  4. Look at the spread of the studies: if they disagree more than their intervals suggest, the pooled estimate may hide real differences (heterogeneity).
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# synthetic studies: log risk ratio and its standard error
names = ["Study A", "Study B", "Study C", "Study D", "Study E", "Study F"]
log_rr = np.array([-0.35, -0.10, -0.45, 0.05, -0.25, -0.30])
se = np.array([0.20, 0.15, 0.30, 0.25, 0.10, 0.18])

w = 1 / se**2                                           # inverse-variance weights (fixed effect)
pooled = np.sum(w * log_rr) / np.sum(w)
pooled_se = 1 / np.sqrt(np.sum(w))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
y = np.arange(len(names))[::-1] + 2
for yi, est, s, wi in zip(y, log_rr, se, w):
    ax.plot(np.exp([est - 1.96 * s, est + 1.96 * s]), [yi, yi], color="black")
    ax.plot(np.exp(est), yi, "s", color="#0072B2", ms=4 + 10 * wi / w.max())   # square size ∝ weight
lo, hi = np.exp(pooled - 1.96 * pooled_se), np.exp(pooled + 1.96 * pooled_se)
ax.fill([lo, np.exp(pooled), hi, np.exp(pooled)], [0.8, 1.15, 0.8, 0.45], color="#D55E00")  # diamond
ax.axvline(1, color="gray", ls="--")                    # no effect
ax.set_xscale("log")
ticks = [0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1, 1.25, 1.6]
ax.set_xticks(ticks, [str(t) for t in ticks])
ax.minorticks_off()
ax.set_yticks(list(y) + [0.8], names + ["Pooled"])
ax.set_xlabel("Risk ratio (log scale): left of 1 favours treatment")
ax.set_title(f"Forest plot: pooled RR = {np.exp(pooled):.2f} ({lo:.2f} to {hi:.2f})")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Before and After: Dumbbells and Slopes

When the story is a change between two moments or two conditions, show the change itself. A dumbbell chart joins the two values for each item with a line. A slope chart (or slopegraph), a design Edward Tufte showed in 1983, puts the two moments in two columns and joins each item’s values, so that rises, falls and changes in rank are obvious. [7]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

schools = ["Ash", "Birch", "Cedar", "Elm", "Oak", "Pine"]
before = np.array([62, 55, 71, 48, 66, 58])            # synthetic pass rates (%)
after = np.array([70, 54, 78, 63, 67, 72])

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
order = np.argsort(after - before)
for k, i in enumerate(order):                           # dumbbell: one row per school
    ax1.plot([before[i], after[i]], [k, k], color="lightgray", lw=3)
    ax1.plot(before[i], k, "o", color="#999999")
    ax1.plot(after[i], k, "o", color="#0072B2")
ax1.set_yticks(range(len(schools)), np.array(schools)[order])
ax1.set_xlabel("Pass rate (%)  grey: before, blue: after")
ax1.set_title("Dumbbell chart, sorted by change")

for i, s in enumerate(schools):                         # slope chart
    col = "#0072B2" if after[i] > before[i] else "#D55E00"
    ax2.plot([0, 1], [before[i], after[i]], color=col, marker="o")
    ax2.text(1.05, after[i], s, va="center", fontsize=9)
ax2.set_xticks([0, 1], ["Before", "After"])
ax2.set_xlim(-0.2, 1.35)
ax2.set_title("Slope chart")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Uncertainty Around a Line

A fitted line has two kinds of uncertainty band. The confidence band shows where the true average line might be; it is narrowest near the middle of the data. The prediction band shows where a new individual observation is likely to fall, and is much wider because it includes the scatter of individual points. Confusing the two makes predictions look far more certain than they are.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 10, 60))
y = 2 + 0.8 * x + rng.normal(0, 1.5, x.size)

X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
beta, res, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
n, p = X.shape
s = np.sqrt(res[0] / (n - p))                           # residual standard deviation
grid = np.linspace(0, 10, 100)
G = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(grid), grid])
XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)
se_mean = s * np.sqrt(np.sum(G @ XtX_inv * G, axis=1))  # uncertainty of the fitted line
se_pred = np.sqrt(se_mean**2 + s**2)                     # ...plus the scatter of new points
fit = G @ beta
t = 2.0                                                  # about the 95% t value for n = 60

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
ax.fill_between(grid, fit - t * se_pred, fit + t * se_pred, color="#56B4E9", alpha=0.2,
                label="95% prediction band (new observations)")
ax.fill_between(grid, fit - t * se_mean, fit + t * se_mean, color="#0072B2", alpha=0.4,
                label="95% confidence band (the line)")
ax.plot(grid, fit, color="#0072B2")
ax.scatter(x, y, s=12, color="black")
ax.legend(loc="upper left")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Seaborn’s regplot draws the confidence band by default. Always say which band you show, and at what level.

9. Showing Uncertainty to Everyone

Error bars are a specialist’s tool. For general audiences, other designs work better. Since 1996 the Bank of England has published its inflation forecasts as fan charts, with darker bands for more likely outcomes fading into lighter ones. [8] Matthew Kay and colleagues found that quantile dot plots, which show, say, twenty equally likely outcomes as twenty dots, helped people decide when to leave for a bus better than a density curve or an interval did. [9] Animations that show many possible outcomes one after another, called hypothetical outcome plots, make the same point: uncertainty is easier to grasp as a set of possibilities than as a bar.

10. Common Misreadings

  • Not knowing what the bars are. SD, SE and CI bars for the same data differ several-fold in length.
  • “The bars overlap, so there is no difference.” 95% CIs can overlap and still indicate a significant difference.
  • “The bars don’t overlap, so the difference is significant.” SE bars that just fail to overlap mean p ≈ 0.16.
  • Reading a 95% CI as containing 95% of the data. That is roughly ±2 SD, not ±2 SE.
  • Saying “95% probability the true value is in this interval”. Strictly, the 95% describes the method across repeated experiments.
  • Using independent-group bars for paired data. A consistent improvement in every subject can look like nothing.
  • Treating a confidence band as a prediction band. Individual outcomes are far more variable than the average line.

11. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: forests and logos

The legend: the forest plot is named after a researcher called Forrest.

The record: Steff Lewis and Mike Clarke traced the history of the name in 2001. Plots of this kind appeared in the 1970s and 1980s, and the name “forest plot” was in use by the early 1990s; they noted the story about a researcher named Forrest but found no evidence for it, and the obvious explanation is the resemblance of the many lines to a forest of trees. [6]

A second record: the logo of the Cochrane Collaboration, founded in 1993 to produce systematic reviews of medical evidence, is a forest plot. It shows trials of giving corticosteroids to women about to give birth prematurely. Each trial alone was inconclusive, but together they showed that the treatment reduced deaths of premature babies, a result that took years to be widely adopted because nobody had combined the evidence. [6]
