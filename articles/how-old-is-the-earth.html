How Old Is the Earth? Salt, Cooling Rocks and Radioactive Clocks

Six thousand years, seventy-five thousand, a hundred million, billions: the age of the Earth has had many answers. The right one came from atoms that tick like clocks, and a meteorite that fell in Arizona.

1. A Question with Many Answers

The distances in the earlier parts of this series could at least be pictured: a journey around the Earth, a beam of light to the Moon. The age of the Earth is different. Nobody was there at the beginning, and no record goes back that far. Yet today we can say, with an uncertainty of about 1 per cent, that the Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago.

Getting there took three centuries and produced some spectacularly different answers: six thousand years from adding up the generations of the Bible, seventy-five thousand from cooling iron balls, a hundred million from the salt in the sea and from the heat of the Earth, and finally billions of years from atoms that tick like clocks. This part of the series follows that journey. It is also the story of one of the most famous scientific arguments of the nineteenth century, between physicists and geologists, and of why the physicists, for once, turned out to be wrong.

3. Counting Generations

For most of history, the age of the world was a question for religion and history. Different traditions gave very different answers. In Hindu cosmology, time runs in vast cycles: a single "day of Brahma", or kalpa, lasts 4.32 billion years, a strikingly large figure, though it describes a repeating cosmic cycle rather than the measured age of a planet. [23]

In Christian Europe, scholars treated the Bible as a historical record and added up the ages of the patriarchs, the reigns of kings and the dates of known events. The most famous result came in 1650 from James Ussher, Archbishop of Armagh, who dated the creation to the night before 23 October 4004 BC. Many others reached similar figures, all around six thousand years. [1] These were careful pieces of scholarship by the standards of their time, but they rested entirely on written records. The scientific question was whether the Earth itself contained a record of its age.

4. Reading the Rocks

Geologists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries found that the rocks did tell a story, and a very long one. Layers of sediment, kilometres thick, are laid down today at a rate of millimetres a year. At Siccar Point in Scotland, James Hutton saw tilted layers of ancient rock worn flat and covered by younger, horizontal layers: the remains of at least two cycles of rock being laid down, lifted, tilted and eroded. In 1788 he concluded that in the rocks he could find "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end". [2]

Charles Lyell and the geologists who followed him argued that the Earth's history must stretch over many millions of years, and Charles Darwin needed even more time for evolution. In the first edition of On the Origin of Species (1859), Darwin estimated that the erosion of the Weald, a valley in south-east England, had taken about 300 million years. [7] (The story of deep time and evolution is told in more detail in our history series, in "The Book of Life".) But geology could give only rough, relative ages: this layer is older than that one. To put a number on it, people turned to physics.

5. Buffon's Cooling Iron Balls

The first experimental attempt came from the French naturalist Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon. He imagined that the Earth had begun as a molten ball, perhaps torn from the Sun, and had been cooling ever since. To find how long that would take, he heated iron balls of different sizes until they glowed, and timed how long each took to cool until it could be touched. Scaling his results up to a ball the size of the Earth, in The Epochs of Nature (1778) he arrived at about 75,000 years. [3, 16]

That was more than ten times longer than the biblical chronologies, and controversial for that reason. But the most important thing about Buffon's work was its method: he treated the age of the Earth as a physical problem that could be solved by experiment and calculation.

6. Salt in the Sea

In 1715 Edmond Halley, whom we met in Part 4, proposed a different clock. Rivers carry small amounts of dissolved salt into the sea. Water evaporates, but the salt stays behind, so the sea should become steadily saltier. If we could measure how much salt the rivers bring each year, and how much is in the sea, dividing one by the other would give the age of the oceans. [4]

The Irish physicist John Joly carried out this calculation in 1899, using sodium, the main element in sea salt, and found that the oceans were about 90 to 100 million years old. [5]

The flaw, recognised in the twentieth century, is that salt does not simply accumulate. It is removed from the sea by being trapped in sediments, deposited in salt beds when shallow seas dry up, carried into the Earth's interior with the sea floor, and blown ashore as spray. The ocean is roughly in balance: salt leaves about as fast as it arrives. The salt clock therefore measures not the age of the sea, but the average time that sodium stays in it, which is indeed of the order of a hundred million years.

7. Kelvin's Cooling Earth

The most powerful argument for a "young" Earth came from the greatest physicist in Britain, William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin. Miners had long known that the deeper you go, the warmer the rock: today we know it warms by about 25–30 °C for each kilometre. Heat is therefore flowing out of the Earth's interior. Kelvin reasoned, like Buffon, that the Earth began molten and has been cooling ever since. The longer it has been cooling, the deeper the cold has penetrated, and the gentler the increase in temperature with depth. From the measured gradient, the temperature of molten rock and how quickly heat moves through rock, he could calculate how long the Earth had been cooling. [6, 10]

In 1862 Kelvin concluded that the Earth had probably been cooling for about 100 million years, and certainly between 20 and 400 million. Later, with better data on the melting of rock, he narrowed it to 20–40 million years. He also argued that the Sun, if it was shining by slowly contracting under its own gravity, could not be more than a few tens of millions of years old. [6, 10] To geologists and biologists this was deeply troubling: Darwin called Kelvin's views "one of my sorest troubles", and removed his estimate for the Weald from later editions of the Origin. But Kelvin's physics seemed impeccable, and his mathematical authority was enormous.

The first serious crack came in 1895, from one of Kelvin's former assistants, the engineer John Perry. He pointed out that Kelvin had assumed the Earth was a solid ball through which heat could move only by conduction. If the deep interior could flow, very slowly, like a thick liquid, it would carry heat upwards far more efficiently, keeping the rocks just below the surface hot for much longer. With that change, the Earth could be billions of years old. [8, 9] Perry was right, but his argument made little impression at the time, partly because it relied on a guess about the inside of the Earth. Within a year, something far more dramatic had been discovered.

8. Radioactivity Changes Everything

In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that uranium salts give off invisible rays that fog photographic plates. [20] Marie and Pierre Curie discovered new, intensely radioactive elements, polonium and radium, and in 1903 Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde showed that radium continually gives off heat, enough to melt its own weight of ice every hour. [21] Here was a source of heat inside the Earth that Kelvin had not known about.

Ernest Rutherford and Frederick Soddy then showed that radioactivity is the transformation of one element into another, at a steady rate that cannot be changed by heat, pressure or chemistry. In 1904 Rutherford lectured on the subject at the Royal Institution in London, with the elderly Kelvin in the audience. He later recalled that he was nervous about contradicting the great man, until he noticed that Kelvin had fallen asleep; when Kelvin woke up, Rutherford diplomatically pointed out that Kelvin himself had said his estimate would hold "provided no new source of heat was discovered", and the old man "beamed upon me". [10, 14]

Radioactivity did two things. It added a source of heat, which seemed to rescue the geologists. And far more importantly, it provided a clock.

9. Radioactive Clocks

Every radioactive element decays at its own fixed rate, described by its half-life: the time it takes for half of the atoms in a sample to decay. After one half-life, half are left; after two, a quarter; after three, an eighth. The atoms they turn into, called daughter atoms, build up in the rock. So if a mineral started with uranium but no lead, the ratio of lead to uranium in it today tells you how long ago it formed.

Rutherford made some of the first such estimates in 1905, from the helium produced by uranium in minerals, and found ages of hundreds of millions of years. [11] In 1907 the American chemist Bertram Boltwood, realising that the final product of uranium's decay is lead, measured lead and uranium in minerals from different places and obtained ages from 410 million to 2.2 billion years. [12] At a stroke the Earth was at least twenty times older than Kelvin had allowed.

The half-lives needed for this work are enormous. Uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.47 billion years, roughly the age of the Earth itself, so about half of the Earth's original uranium-238 is still here. Uranium-235 has a half-life of about 704 million years. Because the two uranium isotopes decay into two different kinds of lead at different rates, measuring both gives two independent clocks in the same mineral, a powerful check.

10. Arthur Holmes and the Long Fight for Deep Time

The young British geologist Arthur Holmes devoted his life to radioactive dating. In 1913, at the age of 23, he published The Age of the Earth, arguing that the oldest rocks were at least 1.6 billion years old. [13] Many geologists, who had only just got used to a hundred million years, found this far too much, and it took decades for radioactive dating to be accepted. Holmes kept refining his methods. By the late 1940s, using the different kinds of lead produced by uranium and thorium, he and, independently, Fritz Houtermans estimated the Earth's age at a little over 3 billion years. [14, 16] The answer was getting closer, but there was a fundamental problem.

The Earth is geologically alive. Its earliest rocks have been melted, eroded, buried and recycled many times over by plate tectonics and erosion. No rock survives from the very beginning. So how could anyone date the formation of the Earth itself?

11. Patterson and the Meteorite

The answer came from space. Meteorites are pieces of asteroids, small bodies that formed at the same time as the planets from the same cloud of gas and dust, but that never melted and mixed like the Earth. They preserve the composition of the early solar system. If the Earth formed at the same time from the same material, its age should be the age of the meteorites.

The American geochemist Clair Patterson set out to measure the lead in meteorites, including fragments of the iron meteorite that made Meteor Crater in Arizona, which contain almost no uranium and so preserve the "primordial" lead of the early solar system. The measurements were extraordinarily difficult, because lead was everywhere: in the air, in dust, in the laboratory equipment. Patterson had to build one of the first ultra-clean laboratories, with filtered air and acid-washed everything, to keep stray lead out of his samples. In 1956, comparing meteorite lead with lead from sediments on the ocean floor, which represent an average of the Earth's crust, he announced the age of the Earth: 4.55 ± 0.07 billion years. [15] That value has barely changed since.

Patterson's struggle with contamination led him to a second discovery. He found that the lead pollution in the environment, much of it from lead added to petrol, was hundreds of times higher than natural levels. He spent the rest of his career campaigning against it, against fierce opposition from industry, and his work led to leaded petrol being phased out around the world, including in India in 2000. [24]

12. The Oldest Things We Can Hold

Since Patterson, many independent clocks have confirmed his answer and filled in the details. [16]

  • The first solids. Certain white grains in meteorites, rich in calcium and aluminium, were the first solid materials to condense in the solar system. They have been dated to 4,567 million years, with an uncertainty of only about 0.2 million years. [19]
  • The oldest crystal from Earth. Zircon is a tough mineral that traps uranium but almost no lead when it forms. A tiny zircon grain from the Jack Hills of Western Australia has been dated to 4.4 billion years; its chemistry hints that liquid water may already have existed on the Earth's surface. [17]
  • The oldest rocks. The Acasta Gneiss in northern Canada contains rocks about 4.0 billion years old. [18]
  • The Moon. Rocks brought back by the Apollo astronauts are up to about 4.4–4.5 billion years old, consistent with the Moon forming soon after the Earth.

Put together, these give a consistent picture: the solar system's first solids formed 4,567 million years ago, the Earth grew to its present size over the following few tens of millions of years, and the best estimate for its age is about 4.54 billion years, with an uncertainty of around 50 million years.

14. Try It Yourself: Half-Lives with Coins

Try it yourself: a radioactive sample you can hold

You need 100 coins (or dice, or sweets with a mark on one side) and a large tray.

  1. Toss all the coins onto the tray. Every coin that lands heads has "decayed": remove it.
  2. Count the coins that are left and write down the number.
  3. Toss the remaining coins again, remove the heads, and count. Repeat until no coins are left.
  4. Plot the number left against the number of tosses.

You should find that roughly half the coins disappear at each toss, even though you cannot predict which ones. That is a half-life: here, one toss. With dice, remove only the sixes, and the half-life becomes about four throws. Try the virtual version below, with 200 coins.

15. How Did They Know? Counting Atoms

How did they know? Weighing individual kinds of atoms

Radioactive dating requires measuring how many atoms of particular isotopes are in a mineral, often just a few millionths of a gram of lead. Chemistry alone cannot tell lead-206 from lead-207, because isotopes of the same element behave almost identically. The tool that made it possible is the mass spectrometer. [16]

The sample is turned into charged atoms or molecules, which are accelerated and sent through a magnetic field. The field bends their paths, and lighter ones bend more than heavier ones, so a stream of lead separates into separate beams for lead-204, 206, 207 and 208, each landing on its own detector. Counting the atoms in each beam gives the ratios needed for the clocks.

Modern instruments can date a single spot a few hundredths of a millimetre across on a zircon crystal, which is how the 4.4-billion-year Jack Hills grain was dated. The hardest part is often not the measurement but keeping the sample clean, as Patterson discovered: the lead in a fingerprint can be more than the lead in the sample.

16. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: did radioactivity prove Kelvin wrong?

The legend: Kelvin's age for the Earth was too small because he did not know about radioactivity. When radioactive heating was discovered, his calculation collapsed.

The record: radioactivity did two separate things. As a clock, it showed beyond doubt that the Earth is billions of years old. But as a source of heat, it does not by itself rescue the old Earth: when modern geophysicists put the known amount of radioactive heating into Kelvin's calculation, it still gives a young Earth. The main flaw in Kelvin's model was the one John Perry pointed out in 1895, before radioactivity was known: the Earth's interior is not a rigid solid but flows slowly, carrying heat upwards by convection. [9, 8]

The grain of truth: Kelvin did say that his estimate assumed no unknown source of heat, and radioactive heat is real and important for the Earth's interior. And Rutherford's story of the old man beaming was a gracious way to soften the blow.

A second legend: "Ussher said creation happened at 9 a.m." The 9 a.m. comes from an earlier writer, John Lightfoot, and the two have often been mixed up; Ussher placed it at nightfall before 23 October.

17. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: when many clocks agree

How confident should we be in 4.54 billion years? Each radioactive clock rests on assumptions: that the decay rate never changed, that the mineral kept all its atoms, that we know how much daughter it started with. Critics sometimes ask: what if one of these assumptions is wrong?

The answer lies in agreement between independent methods. Uranium-238 and uranium-235 decay at very different rates into different kinds of lead; rubidium, samarium and potassium decay into entirely different elements; meteorites, Moon rocks and Earth's oldest zircons are completely separate samples. If any of the assumptions were wrong, these clocks would not agree. Yet they do, to within about 1 per cent. Even models of how the Sun burns, based on entirely different physics, give a solar age of about 4.6 billion years. In 1840 the philosopher William Whewell called this kind of agreement between independent lines of evidence a consilience of inductions, and argued that it is one of the strongest kinds of evidence science has. [22]

The story also shows the limits of authority. Kelvin was the greatest physicist of his day and his mathematics was flawless, yet his answer was wrong by a factor of fifty, because of an assumption about the inside of the Earth that he did not question.

Questions to think about: Is agreement between methods enough to make a result certain? When an expert's calculation conflicts with evidence from another field, how should we decide who is right?
