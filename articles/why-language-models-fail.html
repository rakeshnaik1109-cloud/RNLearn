Why It Fails: Hallucination, Calibration and Measuring the Accuracy of Language Models

The machinery that makes language models so often right also makes them sometimes wrong in a peculiar way: fluent, specific and confident. Knowing where those errors come from tells you when an answer can be trusted.

At a glance

In this part: the other half of the puzzle. Why do language models sometimes state falsehoods fluently and confidently? We look at why “most probable” is not “true”; why rare facts are the weak spot, with a mathematical bound; calibration, or whether a model knows what it knows; blind spots with letters, numbers and reversed facts; how retrieval, tools and reasoning reduce errors; and how to measure accuracy honestly.

You need: Parts 1–5. Our article on null hypotheses and p-values helps with Section 8.

Try: four interactive figures: how often facts appear in training, a calibration chart, how tokens hide letters, and how sure a benchmark score really is.

1. Confidently Wrong

Part 5 explained why language models are so often right: predicting human text well requires learning much of what the text knows. This final part explains why they are sometimes wrong in a peculiar way, producing fluent, specific, confident statements that are simply false. Such errors are called hallucinations. [1] They are not random glitches. They follow from the same machinery that makes the models work, and understanding where they come from is the best guide to when an answer can be trusted.

3. Why Models Hallucinate

A language model is trained to continue text the way text usually continues. When it knows a fact well, the most probable continuation is the fact. When it does not, the most probable continuation is still fluent and plausible: a confident sentence with a name, a date or a reference of the right form. Nothing in next-word prediction distinguishes “what is true” from “what sounds like the kind of thing that would be said here”. Asked for a citation, the model knows what citations look like; if it does not recall a real one that fits, it may produce an invented one with realistic authors, journal and year.

This has had real consequences. In 2023 a US federal judge sanctioned two lawyers who had filed a brief citing court decisions that did not exist; the cases, complete with quotations, had been generated by a chatbot. [2] Part 4’s lesson applies: decoding chooses among the model’s probable continuations, and if none of them is true, no setting will make the answer true.

4. Rare Facts and the Limits of Memory

Which facts are at risk? Fame is extremely uneven: a few people, places and events are mentioned millions of times, and most are mentioned rarely or once. A model sees the famous facts again and again, in different wordings, and learns them reliably. A fact mentioned once is hard to learn; a fact never mentioned cannot be learned at all, yet the model will still produce a plausible answer if asked.

With 100,000 mentions and a typical skew, 83% of the facts never appear and 12% appear exactly once. Alan Turing and I. J. Good worked out, during the Second World War, that the share of observations that are of items seen exactly once estimates how likely the next observation is to be something never seen before. [3] In 2024 Adam Tauman Kalai and Santosh Vempala proved a striking consequence: for arbitrary facts such as birthdays, which follow no pattern, a language model that is well calibrated must hallucinate at a rate at least roughly equal to this “once-seen” share. [4] The only ways out are to see the fact more often, to look it up, or to abstain from answering.

5. Knowing What You Know: Calibration

A model would be far more useful if it signalled when it is guessing. A model is calibrated if, among all the answers it gives with 70% confidence, about 70% are right (Inside the Machine Part 7). Saurav Kadavath and colleagues found that large language models were well calibrated on multiple-choice questions, and could be trained to estimate the probability that their own answers were correct. [5] OpenAI reported that its pre-trained GPT-4 model was well calibrated on such questions, but that the tuning for helpfulness described in Part 5 reduced its calibration. [6]

Calibration in multiple-choice probabilities does not mean that the confident tone of a long written answer is meaningful: the wording is generated like any other text. Practical checks include asking the same question several times and seeing whether the answers agree (Part 4), asking for sources and verifying them, and preferring systems that are allowed, and encouraged, to say “I don’t know”.

6. Blind Spots: Letters, Numbers and Reversals

Some errors come from how models see text. Because they receive tokens rather than letters (Part 2), questions about spelling require knowledge the input hides. Numbers are cut into chunks that need not align with place value, which makes long arithmetic error-prone without help.

Other blind spots come from how knowledge is stored. Lukas Berglund and colleagues trained models on made-up facts of the form “A is B” and found that they could not answer the reverse question, “Who is B?”: a model that has learned “X is the composer of Y” does not automatically know who composed Y. They called this the reversal curse. [7] Knowledge in a language model is a set of learned associations in one direction, not a database that can be queried any way round.

7. Fixing Errors: Retrieval, Tools and Reasoning

  • Retrieval. Retrieval-augmented generation, introduced by Patrick Lewis and colleagues, first searches a collection of documents and gives the most relevant passages to the model, which then answers from them. [8] It addresses rare, recent and private facts, and makes sources checkable, though the model can still misread or ignore what it retrieves.
  • Tools. Timo Schick and colleagues taught a model to decide by itself when to call a calculator, a search engine or a calendar, and to use the results in its answer. [9] Arithmetic done by a calculator does not suffer from tokenisation.
  • Reasoning. Writing out steps (Part 5) improves accuracy on problems that need several steps. Reasoning models are now trained with reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as maths and code, so that they learn to produce long, self-correcting chains of thought; DeepSeek described one such training method in detail. [10] But a written chain of thought is not necessarily an honest account of how the answer was reached: Miles Turpin and colleagues showed that models’ explanations can leave out the factor that actually swayed them, such as a hint planted in the prompt. [11]

8. Measuring Accuracy Honestly

Claims about how accurate a model is usually rest on benchmarks: fixed sets of questions with known answers. Three cautions apply. First, a benchmark is a sample, so its score has sampling error; small differences between models may be noise. Second, a model may have seen the benchmark’s questions during training, contamination, which inflates its score; Yonatan Oren and colleagues developed statistical tests that can detect it. [12] Third, a benchmark measures what it measures. Stephanie Lin, Jacob Hilton and Owain Evans built TruthfulQA from questions that people often answer wrongly because of popular misconceptions, and found that larger models were often less truthful, because they were better at imitating common human errors. [13]

With 200 questions, accuracies of 82% and 78% have overlapping 95% intervals (76.1–86.7% and 71.8–83.2%), and the paired test gives p = 0.38: no evidence of a real difference. With 2,000 questions, the same accuracies give p = 0.002. Our article on null hypotheses, p-values and effect sizes explains these tests and how to compare AI models properly.

9. So How Accurate Are They?

Putting the series together gives a practical answer to the puzzle we started with:

  • Usually right on well-established knowledge that appears often and consistently in text, on language tasks such as summarising and rephrasing, and on common patterns of reasoning, because predicting text well required learning these (Part 5).
  • Often wrong on rare facts, recent events after the training data was collected, precise details such as citations, quotations, numbers and dates, and questions where a popular misconception is more common in text than the truth (Sections 3–4, 8).
  • Unreliable on tasks that clash with how text is represented, such as spelling and long arithmetic, unless tools or step-by-step working are used (Section 6).
  • More trustworthy when answers are grounded in retrieved sources, checked by tools, agreed on across several samples, and when the system may decline to answer (Sections 5, 7).

10. Open Questions

Open questions: can hallucination be eliminated?

Kalai and Vempala’s result shows that for arbitrary facts seen only once, a calibrated model must sometimes invent answers, unless it abstains. [4] So eliminating hallucination entirely means trading it for something else: more refusals, more retrieval, or less calibration. How to make models abstain exactly when they do not know, how to make their stated reasoning faithful to their actual computation, [11] and how to evaluate systems on questions they could not have seen in training, are among the most active research problems in AI.

11. Worked Example: The Unseen and the Uncertain

  1. The once-seen share. Suppose a training text contains 1,000,000 mentions of facts about people, and 27,000 people are mentioned exactly once. Good–Turing estimates that the chance a new question concerns someone never mentioned is about 27,000 / 1,000,000 = 2.7%, the value in Figure 3 for that setting. For such questions, a model that always answers will often be wrong.
  2. A benchmark interval. A model answers 164 of 200 questions correctly: 82%. The 95% Wilson interval is 76.1% to 86.7%: a rival scoring 78% is not clearly worse.
  3. The paired test. If model A alone gets 36 questions right and model B alone 28, the paired test asks whether a 36:28 split is surprising under equal ability: p = 0.38, so it is not.

13. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “bigger models are always more truthful”

The myth: as language models grow and score higher on benchmarks, their answers become more truthful across the board.

The reality: bigger models know more and are usually more accurate, but they are also better at imitating human text, including its errors. On TruthfulQA, built from questions that many people answer wrongly, the largest models of the time were often less truthful than smaller ones, because they reproduced popular misconceptions more fluently. [13] Tuning, retrieval and reasoning reduce such errors, but do not remove the underlying pull towards what is commonly said.

Why it matters: fluency and size are not proof of truth. The questions on which a model is most persuasive and most wrong are exactly those where human text is wrong too.

14. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Hallucinations follow from next-word prediction: a plausible continuation can be probable without being true.
  • Rare facts are the weak spot; for arbitrary facts seen once, a calibrated model must sometimes invent answers.
  • Models can be fairly well calibrated in their probabilities, but a confident tone in writing is not evidence.
  • Tokens hide letters and digits, and learned associations run one way, causing characteristic blind spots.
  • Retrieval, tools, reasoning and multiple samples reduce errors; written reasoning is not always a faithful explanation.
  • Benchmark scores are samples with uncertainty, may be inflated by contamination, and measure only what they test.
