Ramanujan, C. V. Raman and S. N. Bose: Intuition, Scattered Light and Counting Particles

A self-taught clerk who wrote formulas no one could have invented, an accountant who discovered a new kind of light scattering after office hours, and a lecturer whose new way of counting named half the particles in the universe.

1. Intuition, Light and Counting

In the first decades of the twentieth century, while India was a British colony with few research posts for Indians, three Indian scientists produced work that changed mathematics and physics. Srinivasa Ramanujan, a largely self-taught clerk from Madras, filled notebooks with thousands of formulas of breathtaking originality. C. V. Raman, working in his spare time while employed as a government accountant, discovered a new way in which light scatters from molecules and won India’s first science Nobel Prize. Satyendra Nath Bose, a young lecturer in Dhaka, found a new way to count particles of light, and gave his name to half the particles in the universe. [1, 2]

Their stories raise questions about where discovery comes from: what role intuition plays beside proof, and how science depends on access, recognition and the institutions that let talent flourish.

3. Ramanujan: A Clerk with Notebooks

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born in 1887 in Erode and grew up in Kumbakonam, in southern India, in a poor Brahmin family. At about sixteen he borrowed G. S. Carr’s Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure Mathematics, a compendium of some five thousand theorems stated with little or no proof, and taught himself mathematics from it. So absorbed was he that he neglected other subjects, lost his college scholarship and failed to complete a degree. For years he lived in poverty, filling notebooks with his own results, until in 1912 he found work as a clerk in the Madras Port Trust, whose officials encouraged his mathematics. [1]

4. A Letter to Cambridge

In January 1913 Ramanujan wrote to G. H. Hardy, a leading mathematician at Trinity College, Cambridge, enclosing pages of theorems on infinite series, continued fractions, integrals and prime numbers. Some were known, some were wrong, and many were unlike anything Hardy had seen. He recalled that some formulas “defeated me completely; I had never seen anything in the least like them before”, and concluded that they “must be true, because, if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them”. [3, 1] With his colleague J. E. Littlewood, Hardy decided that the writer was a genius and arranged for him to come to Cambridge, overcoming Ramanujan’s religious scruples about crossing the sea.

5. Ramanujan in Cambridge: Partitions

Ramanujan arrived in 1914. Hardy, an atheist and a stickler for rigorous proof, and Ramanujan, devout and guided by intuition, formed one of the great collaborations in mathematics. Their most famous joint work concerns partitions: the number of ways p(n) of writing n as a sum of positive integers, ignoring order. The numbers grow astonishingly fast, and there seemed no hope of a formula. In 1918 Hardy and Ramanujan published an asymptotic series using a new “circle method”; summed carefully, it gave p(200) = 3,972,999,029,388 exactly, matching a laborious hand computation by Percy MacMahon. [4] Ramanujan also discovered strange regularities, such as that p(5k + 4) is always divisible by 5, which opened whole fields of research.

In 1918 Ramanujan became a Fellow of the Royal Society, only the second Indian to be so honoured, and a Fellow of Trinity. But he was gravely ill, weakened by the English climate, wartime food shortages that made his strict vegetarian diet hard to keep, and isolation. Visiting him in hospital, Hardy remarked that his taxi’s number, 1729, seemed a dull one. “No,” Ramanujan replied, “it is a very interesting number; it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways” (1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³). [1]

6. Ramanujan’s Legacy

Ramanujan returned to India in 1919 and died in 1920, aged 32. In his last months he wrote to Hardy about new “mock theta functions”, whose meaning was understood only in the twenty-first century. In 1976 the mathematician George Andrews found a bundle of his late papers, the “lost notebook”, in a Trinity College library. Mathematicians have spent decades proving the results in his notebooks, nearly all of which turned out to be correct; some now appear in physics, in the theory of black holes and string theory. [1, 5] His life also exposed how much talent is lost when people lack education and opportunity: Hardy called his discovery of Ramanujan “the one romantic incident in my life”.

7. C. V. Raman: Science After Office Hours

Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman was born in 1888 in Tiruchirappalli, the son of a physics teacher. A brilliant student, he published a paper in the Philosophical Magazine at eighteen, but there were few research positions in India, so in 1907 he joined the Indian Finance Department in Calcutta. On his way home from work one evening he noticed the sign of the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science, and began doing experiments there before and after office hours, on the acoustics of musical instruments such as the violin and the Indian drums mridangam and tabla. In 1917 he became professor of physics at the University of Calcutta. [2]

8. Why Is the Sea Blue? The Raman Effect

Sailing to Europe in 1921, Raman studied the deep blue of the Mediterranean with a pocket spectroscope and a Nicol prism. Lord Rayleigh had suggested the colour was mainly reflected sky; Raman showed that water itself scatters light, as air does, and published a short note in Nature. [6] Back in Calcutta he and his students studied light scattering in liquids systematically. In early 1928, with K. S. Krishnan, he found that when a beam of sunlight filtered to a single colour passed through a liquid, a feeble trace of light of a different colour appeared in the scattered light. [7]

The explanation is quantum. Most scattered photons bounce off molecules with unchanged energy (Rayleigh scattering), but about one in a million exchanges energy with a molecular vibration, giving it a quantum of energy and emerging redder (a Stokes line), or taking one and emerging bluer (an anti-Stokes line). The shifts reveal the vibrational frequencies of the molecule, a unique fingerprint. Adolf Smekal had predicted such scattering in 1923, and Grigory Landsberg and Leonid Mandelstam observed it in quartz crystals at almost the same time in Moscow. [8] Raman’s rapid, clear publication won the credit; the effect is called Raman scattering, and in Russia also combinational scattering.

9. Raman’s Nobel Prize and Later Years

Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930, the first Asian to win it in a science; he was so confident that he had booked his passage to Stockholm months before the announcement. He later became director of the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore and founded the Raman Research Institute there, working until his death in 1970. [2] Raman spectroscopy became an important laboratory tool only after the invention of the laser in 1960 provided intense single-colour light; today it identifies drugs, minerals, pigments in paintings and molecules in living cells. India celebrates National Science Day on 28 February, the anniversary of the discovery.

10. Satyendra Nath Bose: Counting Photons

Satyendra Nath Bose was born in Calcutta in 1894 and studied at Presidency College with his friend Meghnad Saha, later famous for the theory of ionisation in stars. Together they translated Einstein’s papers on relativity into English. In 1921 Bose became a reader in physics at the new University of Dacca (Dhaka). Preparing lectures on Planck’s law of black-body radiation (Part 9’s glowing objects), he was dissatisfied with its derivation, which mixed classical and quantum ideas. In 1924 he found a new one: treat light as a gas of photons, divide the space of positions and momenta into cells of size h³, and count the ways indistinguishable photons can be distributed among them. Planck’s formula followed directly. [9]

A British journal rejected the paper. Bose sent it to Einstein, writing “Respected Sir, I have ventured to send you the accompanying article for your perusal and opinion.” Einstein translated it into German himself, had it published in the Zeitschrift für Physik, and added a note calling it an important step forward. [10]

11. Bosons and the Condensate

The crucial point was that photons are truly indistinguishable: swapping two of them does not produce a new state. Counted this way, identical particles are more likely to share a state than classical ones. Einstein immediately applied the same counting to atoms, and in 1925 predicted that a gas of such atoms, cooled enough, would suddenly collapse into a single quantum state. [11] Particles that obey these statistics, such as photons, helium-4 atoms and the Higgs particle, are now called bosons, in Bose’s honour; particles that obey Pauli’s exclusion principle, such as electrons, are fermions. In 1995 Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman cooled rubidium atoms to 170 billionths of a degree above absolute zero and created the first Bose–Einstein condensate; Wolfgang Ketterle did the same with sodium shortly afterwards. [12] They received the 2001 Nobel Prize. Bose himself never received one.

12. Science in a Colonial World

All three worked under colonial rule, with few laboratories, little funding and limited contact with European science. Raman’s experiments used simple, often home-made apparatus; Bose had to persuade a European physicist to read his paper; Ramanujan needed a foreign mentor to be taken seriously. Yet they also drew on strong local traditions of mathematics (Part 3) and on new Indian institutions, such as the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science and the University of Calcutta, built by Indian reformers. Their success helped inspire a generation of Indian scientists, including Raman’s nephew Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, who would win the Nobel Prize for the physics of stars in 1983.

13. Intuition and Proof

Ramanujan rarely gave proofs as mathematicians understood them; he stated results, often verified by extensive calculation, and sometimes, as Hardy noted, made striking errors where his intuition misled him, for instance about the distribution of primes. Hardy’s role was to translate this intuition into proofs and to teach Ramanujan when they were needed. Bose’s insight about counting came from dissatisfaction with an argument, not from new data; Raman’s discovery came from a hunch, pursued with simple instruments, that molecular scattering held more than had been seen. In each case, science needed both: the leap of insight, and the disciplined checking that turns it into knowledge.

14. Try It Yourself: Counting and Colour

Try it yourself: partitions, scattering and counting
  1. Partitions: list all partitions of 6. Did you get 11? Check that p(9) = 30 is divisible by 5, as Ramanujan’s rule for p(5k + 4) predicts.
  2. Taxicab numbers: find two different ways to write 4104 as a sum of two cubes.
  3. Scattering: shine a torch through a glass of water with a few drops of milk. Look from the side (bluish) and through it at the torch (orange). This is Rayleigh scattering, the elastic cousin of the Raman effect.
  4. Counting: toss two coins many times. How often do they match? Now imagine the coins were “identical” in Bose’s sense, so that HT and TH count as one outcome. How would the matching probability change?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: formulas from a goddess

The legend: Ramanujan’s formulas came to him in dreams from the goddess Namagiri, so they needed no proof.

The record: friends and family reported that Ramanujan was devoted to Namagiri, his family deity, and that he sometimes credited her with results that came to him in sleep. [1] But his notebooks show long calculations, special cases and repeated revisions, and many of his conjectures were refined over years of work. Hardy, who knew him best, said he had never been able to discover whether Ramanujan’s religious beliefs went deep.

The grain of truth: Ramanujan’s insights often arrived suddenly and in finished form, a phenomenon many mathematicians describe, including Poincaré (Part 10), who wrote of a solution appearing as he stepped onto a bus. Sudden insight usually follows long, conscious preparation.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: where does discovery come from?

Philosophers of science have long distinguished the context of discovery, how ideas arise, from the context of justification, how they are tested. Many argued that only the second can be studied rationally: discovery is psychology, perhaps even inspiration. Ramanujan’s visions, Bose’s dissatisfaction and Raman’s hunches seem to support this. Yet each discovery also depended on preparation, tools and community: Carr’s book, Hardy’s letters, the Calcutta laboratory, Einstein’s willingness to read a stranger’s paper.

Their stories also raise questions about recognition. Credit for the Raman effect went to the group that published first and most clearly; Bose’s name lives on in “boson”, but not on a Nobel medal. Science is a human institution, and who gets heard shapes what we know.

Questions to think about: Can intuition count as knowledge before it is proved? How many potential Ramanujans are lost today for lack of education or opportunity?
