Context and Attention: How Language Models Decide Which Words Matter

Whether the next word is “is” or “are” can depend on a word far back. Attention lets every word look back at every earlier word and decide which ones matter.

1. The Problem of Context

Parts 1 and 2 predicted the next word from the one or two words before it. Real language does not work like that. Whether the next word is “is” or “are” may depend on a noun many words back; whether “it” means the cat or the fish depends on the whole sentence; and answering a question depends on a document pasted in thousands of words earlier. A useful language model must decide, for every next word, which of the earlier words matter. The mechanism that does this in every modern LLM is attention. [1, 2]

3. Long-Distance Clues

Consider “The keys to the old wooden cabinet in the hall …”. The next word should be “are”, because the subject is “keys”; but the nearest nouns, “cabinet” and “hall”, are singular. Linguists call such misleading nouns attractors. In 2016 Tal Linzen, Emmanuel Dupoux and Yoav Goldberg used exactly this test on neural networks: recurrent networks usually chose the right verb, but their errors rose sharply when attractors intervened. [3]

4. Recurrent Networks and Their Bottleneck

The first neural answer to long contexts was the recurrent network: read one word at a time and carry a hidden state, a vector summarising everything so far, from each word to the next. [4] Plain recurrent networks forget quickly, because the signal from early words shrinks at every step; the LSTM of Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber added a gated memory cell that holds information much longer. [5] LSTMs powered speech recognition and translation for much of the 2010s.

But a recurrent network still squeezes the entire past into one fixed-size vector, and must process words one after another, which is slow to train. In 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le translated sentences by encoding the source into a single vector and decoding the translation from it; the longer the sentence, the more that vector had to hold. [6]

5. Attention: Looking Back at Everything

In 2015 Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho and Yoshua Bengio removed the bottleneck: for each word it wrote, their translator looked back at all the source words and took a weighted average, weighting each by its relevance. [7] In 2017 Ashish Vaswani and colleagues built an entire network from this operation, the transformer, in which every word attends to the words of its own text: self-attention. [1]

Each word’s vector is turned into three new vectors by three learned matrices: a query (what this word is looking for), a key (what it offers to others) and a value (the information it passes on). A word’s attention to another is the dot product of its query with the other’s key, scaled and passed through a softmax so the weights sum to 1; its output is the weighted average of the values.

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}} + M\Big)\,V$$

Here M is the causal mask: minus infinity for every future position, so that a word can attend only to itself and earlier words.

In this hand-built example, the verb “eats” looks for an animate noun and gives “cat” a weight of 0.87; the object “fish” looks for a verb and finds “eats”; “cat” looks for an adjective and finds “hungry”. After attention, the vector for “eats” carries information about its subject. In a trained model nobody writes the matrices: they are learned, and the features are not neat labels such as “noun” but directions in a space of thousands of dimensions.

6. Many Heads, Many Layers

One attention operation captures one kind of relationship. A transformer runs many in parallel, called heads, each with its own query, key and value matrices, and stacks dozens of layers. Each layer adds the output of its attention heads, and then of a feed-forward network applied to each word separately, to the word’s vector. Researchers call this running vector the residual stream: a shared workspace that every layer reads from and writes to. [8] The feed-forward layers act partly as a memory; Mor Geva and colleagues found that they behave like key–value stores, with particular units responding to particular textual patterns and promoting particular next words. [9]

Studies of trained models find heads with recognisable jobs. Kevin Clark and colleagues found heads in BERT that attend to the previous or next word, to punctuation, or from verbs to their objects. [10] Many heads park most of their attention on the very first token when nothing else is relevant; Guangxuan Xiao and colleagues called these “attention sinks” and showed that keeping the first tokens is essential when a model processes very long streams of text. [11]

7. Induction Heads and Learning in Context

One kind of head is especially important. An induction head looks for an earlier occurrence of the current word and attends to the word that came after it, so the model can predict that the pattern repeats. In Figure 5, at the second “visits” it attends to “Lumo”, which followed “visits” the first time. Catherine Olsson and colleagues found such heads in transformers of many sizes, forming suddenly during training at the same moment the models became much better at using their context. [12]

This links to one of the most surprising abilities of LLMs. GPT-3 could perform new tasks, such as translating words or correcting grammar, after seeing just a few examples in its prompt, without any change to its weights: in-context learning. [13] Copying and completing patterns found earlier in the context is one of its building blocks.

8. Predicting Every Position at Once

The causal mask has a second benefit. Because each position can see only earlier positions, a single pass over a text of 1,000 tokens gives 1,000 next-token predictions at once: position 1 predicts token 2, position 2 predicts token 3, and so on, each computed in parallel. A recurrent network has to work through the text step by step. This parallelism, on graphics processors, is what made it practical to train on trillions of tokens. Alec Radford and colleagues’ GPT of 2018 used exactly this recipe: a transformer pre-trained to predict the next token, then adapted to particular tasks. [14]

In code: one causal self-attention head in PyTorch 
import torch, math

n, d = 5, 4                                   # tokens, vector size
x = torch.randn(n, d)                         # token vectors
Wq, Wk, Wv = (torch.randn(d, d) for _ in range(3))
q, k, v = x @ Wq, x @ Wk, x @ Wv              # queries, keys, values

scores = q @ k.T / math.sqrt(d)               # every query against every key
mask = torch.triu(torch.ones(n, n), diagonal=1).bool()
scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, float("-inf"))   # no looking ahead
weights = torch.softmax(scores, dim=-1)       # each row sums to 1
out = weights @ v                             # weighted mix of values

9. The Cost of Context

Attention compares every word with every earlier word, so the number of comparisons grows with the square of the text length. During generation, the keys and values of all earlier tokens are kept in memory, the KV cache, so that they need not be recomputed for every new token; that memory grows in proportion to the length.

For a model with 32 layers and vectors of 4,096 numbers, stored at 2 bytes per number, each token’s keys and values take 512 KB: 2 GB for a 4,096-token context and over 60 GB for 128,000 tokens. That is why long contexts are expensive, and why much engineering goes into sharing keys and values between heads, compressing the cache and computing attention efficiently.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: what is each part of the network doing?

Individual heads such as induction heads are understood, but a large model has thousands of heads and millions of feed-forward units interacting across dozens of layers. Nelson Elhage and colleagues found that networks store more features than they have dimensions, by overlapping them, a phenomenon they called superposition, which makes individual units hard to interpret. [15] Working out the full set of mechanisms by which a model gets from a prompt to an answer, the goal of mechanistic interpretability, is still at an early stage.

11. Worked Example: Attention by Hand

Take the query word “eats” in Figure 4, with features (noun 0, animate 0, verb 1, adjective 0).

  1. Query: the query matrix maps the verb feature to “looking for noun 3, animate 3”: q = (3, 3, 0, 0).
  2. Keys: the = (0, 0, 0, 0), hungry = (0, 0, 0, 1), cat = (1, 1, 0, 0), eats = (0, 0, 1, 0); “fish” is in the future and masked.
  3. Scores q·k / √4: the 0, hungry 0, cat (3 + 3)/2 = 3, eats 0.
  4. Softmax: e3 = 20.1, so the weights are 20.1/23.1 = 0.87 for “cat” and 1/23.1 = 0.04 for each of the other three.
  5. Output: 0.87 × cat’s value + 0.04 × each other value: the vector for “eats” now strongly says “my subject is an animate noun”, which helps predict what a cat might eat.

13. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a model with a huge context window reads everything equally well”

The myth: if a model accepts a million tokens, it uses every part of that text as carefully as a human reading a short note.

The reality: attention can reach every token, but that does not mean the model uses them equally. Nelson Liu and colleagues placed the document containing the answer at different positions among many distractors, and found that models answered best when it was at the beginning or end of the context and noticeably worse when it was in the middle. [16]

Why it matters: a long context is an opportunity, not a guarantee. Putting the key information near the start or end of a prompt, or retrieving only the relevant passages, often gives better answers.

14. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • The right next word can depend on words far back; short windows miss them and can be misled by nearer words.
  • Recurrent networks carry a summary of the past, but squeeze it into one vector and process words one at a time.
  • Attention lets every word look back at every earlier word: queries meet keys, a softmax gives weights, and values are mixed.
  • Many heads in many layers each track different relationships, writing into a shared residual stream.
  • Induction heads copy patterns seen earlier in the context, one basis of learning from examples in the prompt.
  • The causal mask allows training on every position at once; the cost is memory that grows with the context and comparisons that grow with its square.
