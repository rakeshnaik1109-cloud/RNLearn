Newton and Leibniz: Calculus, Gravity and the Nature of Space

One wrote the laws that ruled physics for two centuries; the other gave us the notation every student still uses. They invented the same mathematics independently, then fought over it for the rest of their lives.

1. Two Inventors of the Calculus

Galileo showed that motion could be described mathematically (Part 5). But his mathematics was Euclid’s geometry of ratios, ill suited to quantities that change continuously, such as the speed of a falling body at an instant or the curved path of a planet. Within a generation, two men independently created the tool for the job: the calculus. Isaac Newton, a reclusive Cambridge professor, used it to write the laws of motion and gravity that would rule physics for two hundred years. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, a German diplomat, librarian and universal philosopher, gave it the notation we still use. [1, 2]

They then fought one of the bitterest priority disputes in the history of science, and argued about the nature of space, time and God. Their story asks what a law of nature explains, and whether a theory can be accepted when it describes a force without saying what causes it.

3. Newton: A Boy from Woolsthorpe

Isaac Newton was born on Christmas Day 1642 (by the old calendar) at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire, three months after his father’s death. His mother remarried and left him with his grandmother, an experience that may have shaped his famously suspicious, solitary character. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661, and read Descartes, Galileo and Kepler outside the official curriculum. In 1665–66, when plague closed the university, he returned home and, by his own later account, began in those two years his method of fluxions, his theory of colours and his ideas on gravity. “All this was in the two plague years,” he wrote, “for in those days I was in the prime of my age for invention.” [1] He became Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge in 1669, aged 26.

4. Leibniz: A Universal Genius

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646, the son of a professor of moral philosophy, and taught himself Latin from his father’s library as a child. Trained in law, he served the Elector of Mainz and later the Dukes of Hanover as diplomat, historian and librarian. His interests were boundless: he designed a calculating machine that could multiply, studied binary arithmetic, proposed a universal logical language, worked on mining engineering and theology, and wrote thousands of letters. [2] On a diplomatic mission to Paris from 1672 to 1676, he learned modern mathematics from Christiaan Huygens, and in the autumn of 1675 invented his calculus, introducing the symbols ∫ for “summa” and d for difference.

5. The Calculus: Fluxions and Differentials

The calculus has two halves. Differentiation finds rates of change: the slope of a curve, or the speed of a moving body, at each instant. Integration finds totals: areas under curves, distances travelled from changing speeds. The deep discovery, made by both Newton and Leibniz (building on their teachers, such as Isaac Barrow), is the fundamental theorem: the two operations are inverses of each other. [3]

Newton thought physically, of quantities that flow in time; he called the rate of change of a “fluent” x its fluxion, written ẋ. Leibniz thought of curves as made of infinitely many infinitely small pieces; the slope was the ratio of tiny differences, dy/dx, and the area was a sum of thin strips, ∫y dx. Leibniz published first, in 1684, with brief rules for differentiating sums, products and quotients. [4] His notation proved so well designed, almost doing the thinking for you, that continental mathematicians such as the Bernoulli brothers and Euler raced ahead, while British mathematicians, loyal to Newton’s dots, fell behind for a century.

6. The Principia: Laws of Motion and Universal Gravitation

In 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton to ask what orbit a planet would follow if attracted to the Sun by a force falling off as the inverse square of distance. Newton replied at once that it would be an ellipse; he had calculated it. Halley persuaded him to write it up, and paid for the printing. The result, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), is arguably the most important scientific book ever written. [5] It begins with three laws of motion:

  • A body remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless a force acts on it (Galileo’s inertia, Part 5, made general).
  • The change of motion is proportional to the force and in its direction (in modern form, F = ma).
  • To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.

To these he added the law of universal gravitation: every particle attracts every other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their distance. From these few principles he derived Kepler’s three laws of planetary motion, the tides, the flattening of the Earth at the poles, the slow wobble of its axis that causes the precession of the equinoxes, and the orbits of comets. Heaven and Earth, which Aristotle had separated (Part 1), now obeyed the same laws.

7. The Moon Test

How did Newton know that the force holding the Moon in orbit is the same as the one that makes apples fall? He compared numbers. The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away; its orbit requires it to accelerate towards the Earth by about 0.0027 m/s², roughly 3,600 = 60² times less than the 9.8 m/s² of a falling apple. The inverse-square law predicts exactly this. Newton later said he found it to “answer pretty nearly” in the plague years, though an inaccurate value for the Earth’s size may have delayed his confidence. [1, 5] It is a model of how a physical theory is tested: a bold, general law predicts a precise number in a completely different situation.

8. Light, Colours and Opticks

Newton’s first public work was on light. In 1672 he described to the Royal Society how a prism spreads sunlight into a band of colours, and how, in his experimentum crucis (“crucial experiment”), a second prism refracted a single colour without changing it further. White light, he concluded, is a mixture of rays of different colours, each refracted by its own amount. [6] The paper provoked a fierce reply from Robert Hooke, beginning a lifelong enmity, and Newton withdrew from public controversy for years. He also built the first practical reflecting telescope, avoiding the coloured fringes that refraction produces in lenses. His Opticks (1704), written in English and full of experiments, ends with famous “Queries” speculating about the nature of light, matter and forces, which inspired experimenters through the eighteenth century. [7]

9. The Priority Dispute

Newton had developed his calculus by 1666 but published almost nothing about it until 1704. Leibniz developed his independently in 1675 and published in 1684. Leibniz had visited London in 1673 and 1676, and in 1676 Newton sent him two letters, through the Royal Society’s secretary, describing some results while hiding his method in an anagram. Years later, Newton’s supporters accused Leibniz of plagiarism. In 1712 a Royal Society committee published the Commercium Epistolicum, finding for Newton. Newton, the Society’s president, had chosen the committee, drafted much of the report, and then anonymously reviewed it. [8]

Historians now agree that the two men invented the calculus independently, Newton first, Leibniz first to publish and with the better notation. The dispute poisoned relations between British and continental mathematicians for decades, and Leibniz died in 1716 with his reputation in England damaged and his court position in Hanover neglected; his funeral was poorly attended.

10. Absolute Space and the Leibniz–Clarke Correspondence

The two men also disagreed about the foundations of physics. Newton held that space and time are absolute: a fixed, infinite container and a uniform flow, existing independently of anything in them. He argued for absolute space with a spinning bucket of water: as the water starts to rotate with the bucket, its surface becomes concave, revealing rotation that is not merely relative to the bucket. [5] Leibniz held that space is nothing but the system of relations between bodies, and time the order of events. If the whole universe were shifted three metres east, nothing observable would change, so, by his principle of sufficient reason, such a shift is meaningless.

In 1715–16 Leibniz debated Newton’s ally Samuel Clarke in letters that ranged over space, time, miracles and God. [9] Leibniz also attacked Newton’s gravity as an occult quality, an action at a distance with no mechanism. Newton had admitted, in the General Scholium added to the Principia in 1713, that he could not find the cause of gravity: “hypotheses non fingo”, “I feign no hypotheses”. The laws described how bodies move; why, he left open. The debate about absolute space was revived by Ernst Mach (Part 9) and transformed by Einstein (Part 10).

11. The Newtonian Legacy

Newton spent his later decades as Warden and then Master of the Royal Mint, prosecuting counterfeiters, and as president of the Royal Society. He also devoted enormous effort to alchemy and biblical chronology, writing more on theology than on physics; John Maynard Keynes, who bought many of these papers, called him “not the first of the age of reason” but “the last of the magicians”. [10] He died in 1727 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. In 1798 Henry Cavendish measured the gravitational attraction between lead balls in the laboratory, “weighing the Earth”, and in 1846 astronomers found Neptune where Newtonian calculations of the disturbances in Uranus’s orbit predicted it. [11] For two centuries, Newton’s physics was the model of what successful science looks like.

12. Leibniz’s Other Legacies

Leibniz left more than notation. His study of binary arithmetic, published in 1703, which he related to the ancient Chinese Yijing, underlies every digital computer. His dream of a characteristica universalis, a symbolic language in which disputes could be settled by calculation (“Let us calculate!”), anticipated symbolic logic and artificial intelligence (Part 12). His metaphysics of “monads”, simple substances without parts, was strange even to his contemporaries, and Voltaire mocked his claim that this is the best of all possible worlds in Candide. Yet his relational view of space, his principle of sufficient reason, and his work on energy (vis viva, proportional to mv²) proved deeply influential. [2]

13. Reason, Experiment and the New Physics

Newton fused the two methods of Part 5: Galileo’s mathematical idealisation and Bacon’s experimental care. His “Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy” in the Principia include: admit no more causes than are true and sufficient to explain the phenomena; and regard propositions inferred by induction from phenomena as accurate or very nearly true until other phenomena make them more accurate or liable to exceptions. [5] The second rule is a powerful statement of the provisional, correctable character of scientific knowledge, and it raised the question that Hume would press next: what justifies induction at all? (Part 7.)

14. Try It Yourself: Calculus and Gravity

Try it yourself: think like Newton and Leibniz
  1. Slopes: work out [(x + h)² − x²]/h by algebra. What happens as h gets very small? You have just differentiated x².
  2. Areas: estimate the area under y = x² from 0 to 1 by adding ten thin rectangles. Compare with 1/3, the value calculus gives.
  3. Moon test: how far does an apple fall in the first second? Divide by 3,600. Is the result close to the 1.4 mm the Moon “falls” towards the Earth each second?
  4. Prism: use a glass of water or a CD in sunlight to make a spectrum. Can you isolate one colour with a slit in a card and try Newton’s second step?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: the apple

The legend: an apple fell on Newton’s head as he sat under a tree, and in a flash he discovered gravity.

The record: Newton himself, in old age, told the story to several people. His acquaintance William Stukeley recorded that in 1726, while they drank tea under apple trees, Newton said the notion of gravitation had come to him as he sat in a contemplative mood and an apple fell, and he wondered why it should always fall straight down towards the centre of the Earth. [12] Nothing about it hitting his head; that embellishment came later. The event, if it happened, was some sixty years earlier, and the full theory took twenty more years of work.

The grain of truth: the essential insight, that the force pulling the apple might reach as far as the Moon, is exactly Newton’s Moon test. A tree at Woolsthorpe, said to be the original, still stands.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: does a law explain, if it gives no cause?

Newton’s law of gravitation predicted with astonishing precision, but said nothing about how one body pulls another across empty space. Newton privately called action at a distance an absurdity that no competent thinker could accept; Leibniz called it an occult quality. Yet the law worked, and over time scientists stopped asking for a mechanism. Is a mathematical law that predicts everything a complete explanation, or only a description? Einstein later replaced the force with curved spacetime (Part 10), but quantum physics raised similar puzzles again.

The Leibniz–Clarke debate also asks whether space is a thing or a set of relations. If all motion is relative, what makes Newton’s bucket of water curve? Mach answered: the distant stars (Part 9). Einstein was inspired by that answer, though his final theory did not fully embody it.

Questions to think about: Should we accept a theory that predicts perfectly but whose central entity we cannot picture? If everything in the universe moved together, would anything have happened?
