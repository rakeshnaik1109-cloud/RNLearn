Counts and Categories: Chi-Square, Exact Tests and Overdispersion

Seeds germinate or not; plates carry so many colonies; raters say yes or no. Here is how to analyse counts and categories, from Pearson’s chi-square and Mendel’s peas to exact tests, odds ratios and kappa.

1. Counting Things

Not every measurement is a number on a scale. Seeds germinate or they do not. Cells are classified as normal, damaged or dead. Pea plants have round or wrinkled seeds. A detector counts photons; a microbiologist counts colonies on a plate. Such data are counts and categories, and they need their own methods.

This part covers them: the χ² test for whether counts fit a theory and whether two classifications are related, Fisher’s exact test for small samples, McNemar’s test for paired yes/no data, the risk ratio and odds ratio, Poisson counts and the common trap of overdispersion, and Cohen’s kappa for agreement between raters. Proportions and their confidence intervals were introduced in Part 4; here we compare and test them. [1]

3. Kinds of Categorical Data

  • Nominal categories have no order: blood group, species, colour, which of four catalysts was used.
  • Ordinal categories have a natural order but no fixed spacing: mild, moderate or severe; a damage score from 0 to 4; a five-point rating.
  • Binary (dichotomous) data have just two categories: yes or no, alive or dead, pass or fail. They are summarised by a proportion.
  • Counts record how many events happen in a fixed time, area or volume: decays per minute, colonies per plate, photons per pixel.

Categorical data are usually summarised in a frequency table or, for two variables, a contingency table (cross-tabulation), which counts how many observations fall into each combination of categories. Means and standard deviations make little sense for nominal categories; proportions and counts do.

4. Does It Fit? The Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test

Suppose a theory predicts how counts should be shared among categories. Do the observed counts fit? In 1900 Karl Pearson proposed comparing each observed count O with the count E expected under the theory: [2]

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}.$$

If the theory is right, χ² approximately follows a chi-square distribution with (number of categories − 1) degrees of freedom; a large value means the observed counts are further from the expected ones than chance would usually produce. Dividing by E makes sense because counts with larger expected values naturally vary more: for Poisson-like counts the variance equals the mean (Part 2).

A classic test is Gregor Mendel’s 1866 experiment on pea plants. [3] Crossing plants that differed in seed shape and seed colour, he obtained 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green seeds, 556 in all. His theory predicted a ratio of 9:3:3:1, or expected counts of 312.75, 104.25, 104.25 and 34.75. The χ² statistic is 0.47 with 3 degrees of freedom, p = 0.93: an excellent fit.

For 60 rolls of a die giving 8, 9, 12, 7, 10 and 14 of each face, χ² = 3.4 with 5 degrees of freedom, p = 0.64: uneven-looking counts, but no evidence of bias. For 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 20, χ² = 13.0, p = 0.023: too many sixes to blame on chance. If any parameters of the expected distribution are estimated from the data (such as the mean of a Poisson distribution), subtract one more degree of freedom for each.

5. Contingency Tables and the Test of Independence

The most common question about categorical data is whether two classifications are related: does germination depend on treatment? Does the type of defect depend on the production line? Arrange the counts in a contingency table. If the two variables were independent, the expected count in each cell would be

$$E = \frac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{\text{grand total}}.$$

The χ² statistic is calculated over all cells as before. For a table with r rows and c columns it has (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom, a result Ronald Fisher established in 1922, correcting Pearson’s original count. [4] For a 2 × 2 table that is 1 degree of freedom.

A significant χ² says only that the variables are associated, not how strongly or in what way. For larger tables, look at the standardised residuals, (O − E)/√E, to see which cells deviate most, and report an effect size: for 2 × 2 tables the difference in proportions, the risk ratio or the odds ratio (Section 8).

6. Small Samples: Fisher’s Exact Test and Yates’s Correction

The χ² distribution is only an approximation to the true distribution of the statistic, and it fails when expected counts are small. The usual guidance, from William Cochran in 1954, is that no expected count should be below 1 and no more than 20% should be below 5. [5]

For small 2 × 2 tables, Fisher’s exact test avoids the approximation altogether. Given the row and column totals, it calculates the exact probability of every possible table (from the hypergeometric distribution) and adds up those at least as extreme as the one observed. Fisher described it in 1935 with the example of a lady who claimed she could tell whether milk had been poured into the cup before or after the tea. [6] For a table with 7 of 8 successes in one group and 2 of 8 in the other, the χ² test gives p = 0.012 but Fisher’s exact test gives p = 0.041: with expected counts of 3.5 and 4.5, the approximation exaggerates the evidence.

An older fix is Yates’s continuity correction (1934), which reduces each |O − E| by 0.5 before squaring. [7] It brings the χ² test closer to the exact test but tends to be conservative, and with modern computers Fisher’s exact test (or the N − 1 version of the χ² test recommended in some comparisons) is usually preferred for small tables. [8] For large tables all of these give very similar answers.

7. Paired Categories: McNemar’s Test

If the same subjects are classified twice, for example the same 100 samples tested by an old and a new detection method, the two classifications are not independent, and the ordinary χ² test is wrong. Arrange the data by pairs:

New method positiveNew method negative
Old method positive4012
Old method negative345

The 85 samples on which the methods agree say nothing about which method detects more. McNemar’s test (1947) uses only the discordant pairs: 12 detected by the old method alone and 3 by the new method alone. [9] If the methods were equally sensitive, these 15 should split roughly evenly. An exact binomial test gives p = 0.035; the χ² version, (|12 − 3| − 1)²/(12 + 3) = 4.27, gives p = 0.039. The old method detects more samples.

8. Risk, Odds and Confounders

For a 2 × 2 table comparing two groups, three effect sizes are common. Suppose 75% of treated seeds and 50% of control seeds germinate:

  • The difference in proportions (risk difference): 75% − 50% = 25 percentage points. Its reciprocal, 1/0.25 = 4, is the number needed to treat: on average, treating 4 seeds produces one extra germination.
  • The risk ratio (relative risk): 0.75/0.50 = 1.5. Treated seeds are 1.5 times as likely to germinate.
  • The odds ratio: the odds of germination are 0.75/0.25 = 3 for treated seeds and 0.50/0.50 = 1 for control seeds, so the odds ratio is 3.0. [10]

The odds ratio and risk ratio are similar when the outcome is rare, but diverge when it is common, as here: an odds ratio of 3 does not mean “three times as likely”. Odds ratios are nevertheless widely used, because they are what logistic regression estimates (Part 7) and because they can be estimated from case–control studies, where risks cannot. Confidence intervals for both ratios are calculated on the log scale.

Associations in tables are vulnerable to confounding and to Simpson’s paradox (Part 7). When the data fall into strata, such as several batches, sites or age groups, the Mantel–Haenszel method (1959) combines the 2 × 2 tables from each stratum into a single adjusted odds ratio and test. [11]

9. Counts and Rates: Poisson and Overdispersion

Counts of independent events in a fixed interval, such as radioactive decays, photons, colonies or cells in a counting chamber, often follow the Poisson distribution, for which the variance equals the mean. In 1907 Gosset showed that counts of yeast cells in the squares of a haemacytometer behave this way, giving brewers a way to judge the precision of their counts. [12] For a count x, the standard error is about √x, so counting 100 events gives a relative uncertainty of about 10%, and 10,000 events about 1%.

Two rates measured over equal times can be compared simply: if 30 events occur in one sample and 50 in another, then under equal rates each of the 80 events is equally likely to come from either sample, and a binomial test gives p = 0.033. With unequal times or covariates, Poisson regression models the logarithm of the rate as a linear function of predictors. It belongs, with linear and logistic regression, to the family of generalised linear models introduced by John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn in 1972. [13]

The Poisson assumption often fails in biology and the laboratory. Plates, animals or samples differ for reasons beyond counting noise, so the counts vary more than the mean predicts: overdispersion. Ten plates with counts 12, 9, 15, 11, 8, 14, 10, 13, 9 and 11 have mean 11.2 and variance 5.3, consistent with Poisson noise. Ten others with counts 3, 22, 7, 15, 1, 30, 9, 4, 18 and 6 have a similar mean, 11.5, but variance 89. Treating them as Poisson would make standard errors far too small and p-values far too optimistic. A dispersion test compares Σ(x − x̄)²/x̄ with a χ² distribution on n − 1 degrees of freedom; remedies include negative binomial or quasi-Poisson models.

10. Ordered Categories

When categories have a natural order, such as increasing dose or severity, the ordinary χ² test throws that information away: it would give the same answer if the categories were shuffled. A test for trend uses the order. For proportions across ordered groups (for instance, the share of seeds germinating at four increasing doses), the Cochran–Armitage trend test asks whether the proportion rises or falls steadily, and it is much more powerful than the general χ² test when it does. [5, 14] For an ordinal outcome compared between groups, rank-based tests such as the Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests (Part 5) are natural choices, and ordinal logistic regression offers a full model.

11. Agreement Between Raters: Cohen’s Kappa

Many classifications involve judgement: is this cell damaged? Is this X-ray abnormal? Is this image in focus? To check reliability, two raters classify the same samples, and their agreement is measured. Raw agreement is misleading, because two raters guessing at random would still agree some of the time. Cohen’s kappa (1960) corrects for this: [15]

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},$$

where po is the observed agreement and pe the agreement expected by chance from each rater’s overall proportions. κ = 1 is perfect agreement, κ = 0 is no better than chance. Kappa has a well-known paradox: when one category is very common, chance agreement is high, so excellent raw agreement can give a modest kappa. [16] Two raters who agree on 90% of samples have κ = 0.80 if half the samples are positive, but only κ = 0.44 if 90% are positive.

The same chance-correction idea appears in machine learning, where kappa and the related Matthews correlation coefficient are used to judge classifiers on unbalanced data; see our article on classification and regression metrics.

12. Reporting

  • Give the full table of counts, not just percentages; readers can then recompute any statistic.
  • Report an effect size with a confidence interval: the difference in proportions, the risk ratio or the odds ratio, saying which.
  • Name the test (χ², with or without correction, Fisher’s exact, McNemar’s) with its degrees of freedom and p-value.
  • For counts, check for overdispersion before trusting Poisson standard errors.
  • Make sure each observation is counted once and is independent: one seed, one sample, one patient, not repeated readings of the same unit.

13. Worked Example: Seed Germination

Sixty seeds are soaked in a growth solution and sixty in water, assigned at random; after a week, 45 treated and 30 control seeds have germinated.

  1. Table. Treated: 45 germinated, 15 not (75%). Control: 30 germinated, 30 not (50%).
  2. Expected counts under independence: 75 germinated in total out of 120, so 37.5 expected in each group, and 22.5 not germinated. All are well above 5.
  3. χ² test: χ² = (45 − 37.5)²/37.5 + (15 − 22.5)²/22.5 + (30 − 37.5)²/37.5 + (30 − 22.5)²/22.5 = 8.0, with 1 degree of freedom, p = 0.005. With Yates’s correction, p = 0.008; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.008.
  4. Effect sizes. Difference 25 percentage points (95% CI 8 to 42); risk ratio 1.50 (95% CI 1.12 to 2.01); odds ratio 3.0 (95% CI 1.38 to 6.50); number needed to treat 4.
  5. Report. “Germination was higher with the growth solution (45/60, 75%) than with water (30/60, 50%); risk ratio 1.50, 95% CI 1.12 to 2.01; χ²(1) = 8.0, p = 0.005.”
Try it in Python: χ², Fisher, McNemar and kappa 
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from statsmodels.stats.contingency_tables import Table2x2, mcnemar

print(stats.chisquare([315, 108, 101, 32], f_exp=np.array([9, 3, 3, 1]) * 556 / 16))
# Mendel: chi2 = 0.47, p = 0.93

t = np.array([[45, 15], [30, 30]])
print(stats.chi2_contingency(t, correction=False))   # chi2 = 8.0, p = 0.0047
print(stats.fisher_exact(t))                         # p = 0.0080
tab = Table2x2(t)
print(tab.riskratio, tab.riskratio_confint())        # 1.5 (1.12, 2.01)
print(tab.oddsratio, tab.oddsratio_confint())        # 3.0 (1.38, 6.50)

print(mcnemar([[40, 12], [3, 45]], exact=True))      # p = 0.035

14. Try It Yourself: Is the Bag Fair?

Try it yourself: a goodness-of-fit test with sweets or dice
  1. Open a bag of coloured sweets (or roll a die 60 times) and count each colour (or face).
  2. Write down what you expect: equal numbers of each colour, or the proportions the manufacturer states, if it does.
  3. Compute χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E and compare it with the 5% critical value for (categories − 1) degrees of freedom: 9.49 for 4, 11.07 for 5.
  4. Pool the counts of several bags or several friends. Does the conclusion change with more data? Which colours contribute most to χ²?

A bonus: if everyone in a class tests a fair die, about one in twenty should get p < 0.05 by chance. Collect everyone’s p-values: are they spread evenly between 0 and 1, as they should be when the null hypothesis is true?

15. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: were Mendel’s results too good to be true?

The legend: Gregor Mendel faked his pea data.

The record: in 1936 Ronald Fisher re-analysed all of Mendel’s experiments and found that, taken together, the counts agreed with Mendel’s expected ratios more closely than chance would usually allow: the total χ² was so small that Fisher put the chance of agreement this good at roughly 7 in 100,000. [17] Fisher did not accuse Mendel of fraud; he suggested that an assistant who knew what was expected might have influenced the counts. Later scholars have proposed other explanations, including unconscious bias in classifying ambiguous seeds, selective reporting of experiments, or stopping counts when the ratio looked right, and the debate has never been fully settled. [18]

The grain of truth: Mendel’s theory was right, and his experiments were real. But the episode carries a statistical lesson: a χ² value that is too small is suspicious too. Real data are noisy, and results that match theory too perfectly suggest that something other than chance has shaped them.

16. Philosophy Corner

Philosophy Corner: who decides the categories?

Some categories are given by nature: a seed has germinated or it has not. Many others are made by us. Continuous measurements are often cut into groups: “high” and “low” expression, “responders” and “non-responders”, “obese” and “not obese”. This makes results easy to tabulate, but it throws information away. Statisticians have long warned that dichotomising a continuous variable loses power, treats values just either side of the cut-off as completely different and values far apart within a group as identical, and invites choosing the cut-off that gives the best result. [19]

Categories also shape what we see. A classification scheme decides which differences count and which are ignored, and two raters can disagree not because either is careless but because the boundary between categories is genuinely fuzzy, which is exactly what kappa measures.

Questions to think about: When does splitting a measurement into categories help understanding, and when does it hide it? If a cut-off is chosen after seeing the data, what happens to the meaning of the p-value?
