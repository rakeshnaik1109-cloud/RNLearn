Learning from Data: Models, Loss, Overfitting and Honest Evaluation

A model that fits its training data perfectly can be useless. The first part of the series lays the foundations of machine learning: what learning is, why generalisation is the real goal, and how to measure it without fooling yourself.

1. What Does It Mean for a Machine to Learn?

A traditional program follows rules written by a person: if the temperature is above 30 °C, switch on the fan. A machine learning program is different. It is given examples, and it works out the rules itself. Show it thousands of photographs labelled “cat” or “dog”, and it learns to label new photographs. Show it past house sales, and it learns to estimate the price of a house it has never seen.

In 1950 Alan Turing suggested that rather than programming an adult mind, we might build a “child machine” and educate it. [1] In the 1950s Arthur Samuel wrote a checkers program that improved by playing against itself, and his 1959 paper popularised the phrase “machine learning”. [2] The definition most often quoted today comes from Tom Mitchell’s 1997 textbook: a program learns from experience E with respect to a task T and a performance measure P, if its performance at T, as measured by P, improves with experience E. [3]

Every part of this series is about one or more pieces of that definition: the task (classification or regression), the experience (data), the performance measure (loss and metrics), and the machinery that turns experience into improvement (the algorithm). This first part lays the foundation that all the others build on.

3. The Vocabulary of Machine Learning

A few words appear in every part of this series:

  • An example (or sample, or instance) is one item: one house, one image, one patient.
  • Its features are the measurements that describe it: floor area, number of rooms, location. Together they form a feature vector x.
  • Its label or target y is the answer we want to predict: the price, or “cat”.
  • A data set is a collection of examples, usually arranged as a table with one row per example and one column per feature.

Machine learning problems come in several kinds:

  • Supervised learning: every training example has a label, and the goal is to predict labels for new examples. If the label is a category (spam or not spam, which of ten digits), the task is classification. If it is a number (price, temperature, yield), the task is regression. These are the focus of this series.
  • Unsupervised learning: there are no labels, and the goal is to find structure, such as clusters of similar customers or a simpler description of the data.
  • Self-supervised learning: the labels are made from the data itself, for example by hiding a word in a sentence and asking the model to predict it. This is how large language models are pre-trained.
  • Reinforcement learning: an agent learns by trial and error from rewards, as in game-playing programs.
The running example

To make comparisons easy, the series reuses the same small data sets. For regression: 15 training points and 60 test points drawn from the curve y = sin(2πx), with random noise of standard deviation 0.25 added to each. We know the truth, so we can see exactly how well each method recovers it. Even a perfect model would have an average squared error of 0.25² = 0.0625 on new points, because the noise cannot be predicted. A classification data set joins in Part 2.

Check yourself: classification or regression?

Predicting tomorrow’s maximum temperature: regression (a number). Predicting whether it will rain tomorrow: classification (yes or no). Predicting how many millimetres of rain will fall: regression again. The same situation can give either kind of problem, depending on the question.

4. A Model Is a Function with Knobs

A model is a function that turns features into a prediction: ŷ = f(x; θ). The symbols θ stand for its parameters, the adjustable knobs that learning will set. For a straight line ŷ = θ0 + θ1x, there are two knobs: the intercept and the slope. A polynomial of degree 9 has ten. A modern neural network has millions or billions.

Some settings are not learned but chosen before learning starts: the degree of the polynomial, the depth of a decision tree, the number of layers in a network, how fast to learn. These are hyperparameters. Choosing them well is a central practical problem, and Sections 9–10 show how.

Every choice of model type restricts what can be learned. A straight line can never capture a curve; a model that assumes smoothness will struggle with sudden jumps. These built-in assumptions are the model’s inductive bias. Far from being a flaw, some inductive bias is essential: without assumptions, there is no reason to prefer any prediction for an unseen case over any other (Section 13). Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron of 1958, an early learning machine with adjustable connection weights, built in the assumption that classes can be separated by a straight boundary. [4]

5. Loss: Measuring How Wrong a Model Is

To improve, a model needs a way to measure its mistakes. A loss function compares a prediction with the true answer and returns a number: zero when the prediction is perfect, larger when it is worse. For regression, the most common is the squared error, (ŷ − y)²: big mistakes count much more than small ones. Averaged over the training examples, it is the mean squared error (MSE).

Learning is then an optimisation problem: find the parameters that make the average loss over the training data as small as possible. This principle, called empirical risk minimisation, was put on a mathematical footing by Vladimir Vapnik, who also studied when the training loss is a reliable guide to the loss on new data. [5] Part 7 is devoted to loss functions: why squared error, when to use absolute error or cross-entropy, and how the choice changes what a model learns.

The maths: empirical risk

The quantity we really care about is the expected loss on new data drawn from the same distribution, the risk:

$$R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\big[\,L(f(x;\theta),\,y)\,\big].$$

We cannot compute it, because we do not know the distribution. Instead we minimise the average over the n training examples, the empirical risk:

$$\hat{R}(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L(f(x_i;\theta),\,y_i), \qquad \hat\theta = \arg\min_\theta \hat{R}(\theta).$$

The gap between R and R̂ is the generalisation gap. Everything in Sections 7–12 is about keeping it small, or at least measuring it honestly.

6. Fitting the Knobs

How are the best parameters found? For some models, such as a straight line with squared error, there is an exact formula: the method of least squares (Part 7 of Making Sense of Data). For most models there is not, and the parameters are found step by step: start with a guess, see which direction reduces the loss, take a small step that way, and repeat. This is gradient descent, the engine behind almost all modern machine learning, introduced properly in Part 2 and refined in Part 8.

In code: fitting a model with scikit-learn 
import numpy as np
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 1, 15)); y = np.sin(2*np.pi*x) + rng.normal(0, 0.25, 15)

model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree=3), LinearRegression())
model.fit(x.reshape(-1, 1), y)          # learn the parameters
print(model.predict([[0.25]]))           # predict for a new x

Almost every library follows this pattern: create a model, call fit on training data, call predict on new data. The rest of this part is about the question that matters most: how do we know whether the predictions will be any good?

7. Generalisation, Overfitting and Underfitting

The goal of learning is not to do well on the training data, which we already have, but to generalise: to do well on new data. These are different. A model can fit its training examples perfectly by memorising them, noise and all, and still predict new cases badly. This is overfitting. A model that is too simple to capture the real pattern does badly on both, which is underfitting.

For the running example, a straight line (degree 1) has a training error of 0.20 and a test error of 0.23: underfitting. A cubic (degree 3) brings both down, to 0.022 and 0.075, close to the noise floor of 0.0625. Beyond that, the training error keeps falling, to 0.0006 at degree 12, but the test error explodes, to millions, because the curve swings wildly between and beyond the training points.

Watch out: training error is not a measure of quality

Training error almost always falls as a model grows more complex, so it cannot tell you when to stop. Always judge a model on data it has not seen.

8. The Bias–Variance Trade-off

Why does test error fall and then rise? Imagine collecting many different training sets from the same source and fitting the same kind of model to each. Two things can go wrong:

  • Bias: the models are wrong on average, because the model family cannot represent the truth. A straight line fitted to a sine curve is biased however much data you have.
  • Variance: the models differ a lot from one training set to another, because they are flexible enough to follow the quirks of each particular sample.

Simple models have high bias and low variance; flexible models the reverse. The best model balances the two. Stuart Geman, Elie Bienenstock and René Doursat called this the bias–variance dilemma in a 1992 analysis of neural networks. [6]

The maths: the bias–variance decomposition

For squared-error loss, with data y = f(x) + ε where the noise ε has variance σ², the expected error of a fitted model f̂ at a point x, averaged over training sets, splits exactly into three parts:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat f(x)-\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.$$

The noise term sets a floor that no model can beat. For other losses, such as classification error, the decomposition is less tidy, but the intuition carries over.

Check yourself: which is it?

Your model’s predictions change completely when you retrain it on a slightly different sample. Is that bias or variance? Variance. Your model gives almost the same predictions on any sample, but they are consistently too low for large houses. Bias.

9. Train, Validation and Test

To measure generalisation honestly, we hold back data the model never learns from. The standard arrangement uses three parts:

  • The training set, used to fit the parameters.
  • The validation set, used to compare models and choose hyperparameters, such as the polynomial degree.
  • The test set, locked away until the very end and used once, to estimate how the final model will perform on new data.

Why three? Because every time we use data to make a choice, we fit that data a little. If we try twenty models and pick the one with the best validation score, that score is optimistic: we picked it partly for its luck. The test set, untouched by any choice, gives an unbiased estimate.

How to split matters as much as how much. Shuffle at random only if examples are independent. If they come in time order, train on the past and test on the future, or the model will “predict” using information from later. If several examples come from the same patient, machine or batch, keep each group entirely in one split, or the model will be tested on near-copies of what it trained on (the same issue as pseudoreplication in Making Sense of Data Part 6).

10. Cross-Validation

With small data sets, setting aside a validation set wastes precious examples, and the estimate depends heavily on which examples happen to land in it. k-fold cross-validation solves both problems. Split the training data into k equal folds. Train on k − 1 folds and validate on the one left out; repeat k times, so that every example is used for validation exactly once; and average the k validation errors. Mervyn Stone set out the method for choosing and assessing predictions in 1974. [7]

For the running example, 5-fold cross-validation picks degree 3, with an estimated error of 0.046, the same choice the hidden test set would have made. Common choices are k = 5 or 10; with k equal to the number of examples, it is called leave-one-out cross-validation. After choosing the hyperparameters, the final model is refitted on all the training data, and only then evaluated once on the test set.

In code: choosing the degree by cross-validation 
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, KFold

search = GridSearchCV(
    make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(), LinearRegression()),
    param_grid={"polynomialfeatures__degree": range(0, 9)},
    cv=KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0),
    scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
search.fit(x.reshape(-1, 1), y)
print(search.best_params_)               # refits on all training data

11. Data Leakage: How to Fool Yourself

Data leakage happens when information that would not be available at prediction time, or information from the test data, finds its way into training. The model then looks excellent in evaluation and fails in use. A study of data-mining competitions described many forms of it, [8] and a 2023 review found leakage affecting hundreds of published scientific papers that used machine learning, across more than a dozen fields. [9]

A classic trap is selecting features before cross-validation. Suppose you have 50 samples and thousands of measurements, say gene expression levels, and you first pick the 20 measurements most associated with the outcome using all the data, then cross-validate a classifier on those 20. The selection step has already seen the validation folds. In 2002 Christophe Ambroise and Geoffrey McLachlan showed that this selection bias made published classifiers based on microarray data look far more accurate than they were. [10, 11]

In simulations like Figure 6, the leaky analysis reports about 93% accuracy on pure noise, while the honest one gives about 49%. Other common leaks: scaling or filling in missing values using statistics from the whole data set; features that are recorded only after the outcome is known; duplicates or near-duplicates that land in both training and test sets; and testing on the past after training on the future.

Watch out: every learning step belongs inside the fold

Anything that learns from data (feature selection, scaling, imputation, resampling, tuning) must be fitted on the training folds only. In scikit-learn, putting all these steps in a Pipeline and cross-validating the whole pipeline does this automatically.

12. Learning Curves: More Data or a Better Model?

When a model disappoints, should you collect more data or change the model? A learning curve, which plots training and validation error against the number of training examples, often answers the question.

  • If the two curves meet quickly at a high error, the model has high bias: more data will not help, but a richer model or better features will.
  • If there is a large gap that narrows slowly, the model has high variance: more data or regularisation will help.
  • If both approach the noise floor, you are near the best that is possible with these features.

13. No Free Lunch, and Open Questions

Is there a best learning algorithm? In 1996 David Wolpert proved that, averaged over all possible problems, every learning algorithm performs equally well: for any problem where one algorithm wins, there is another where it loses. [12] This no-free-lunch theorem does not mean all algorithms are equal on the problems we actually meet, which are far from random. It means that an algorithm succeeds when its inductive bias matches the structure of the problem. Choosing well, the subject of Part 10, means knowing what each method assumes.

Theory also asks when good training performance guarantees good test performance. Leslie Valiant’s 1984 framework of “probably approximately correct” (PAC) learning, and Vapnik’s statistical learning theory, give guarantees that depend on how flexible the model family is. [13, 5]

Open questions: why do huge networks generalise?

Classical theory says that a model with far more parameters than training examples should overfit badly. Yet modern neural networks, with millions of parameters trained on fewer examples, often generalise well. In 2017 researchers showed that such networks can even fit completely random labels perfectly, so their capacity alone cannot explain their success. [14] In 2019 Mikhail Belkin and colleagues described double descent: as model size grows, test error first follows the classical U-shape, peaks when the model is just big enough to fit the training data exactly, and then falls again as the model grows further. [15] Why training methods such as gradient descent find solutions that generalise remains an active research question, revisited in Part 8.

14. Worked Example: Choosing a Model Honestly

Putting the pieces together on the running example:

  1. Split. Keep the 60 test points locked away. Use only the 15 training points for every decision.
  2. Choose a model family and a loss. Polynomials of degree 0 to 8, with squared error.
  3. Cross-validate. 5-fold cross-validated errors: 0.43 (degree 0), 0.23 (1), 0.29 (2), 0.046 (3), 0.063 (4), 0.17 (5), 0.40 (6), and enormous beyond. Choose degree 3.
  4. Refit the degree-3 model on all 15 training points.
  5. Test once. Error on the 60 test points: 0.075, close to the noise floor of 0.0625 and in line with the cross-validated estimate. Report this number.

Had we instead chosen the degree by training error, we would have picked degree 12 and reported a training error of 0.0006, while the model’s real error on new data was millions of times larger.

16. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “machine learning just lets the data speak”

The myth: machine learning needs no assumptions. Given enough data, it discovers the truth objectively.

The reality: data alone never determine a prediction for a case not in the data. Every learner adds assumptions: that the relationship is smooth, or linear, or that nearby examples behave alike, or that simpler explanations are better. Tom Mitchell argued in 1980 that these biases are what make generalisation possible at all, [16] and the no-free-lunch theorem makes the point precise. The data also carry the assumptions and blind spots of whoever collected and labelled them.

Why it matters: understanding a model’s assumptions tells you when to trust it and when it will fail, which is why every later part of this series spells them out.

17. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • A model is a function with parameters; learning means choosing the parameters that minimise a loss on training data.
  • The goal is generalisation to new data, which training error cannot measure.
  • Too simple means high bias (underfitting); too flexible means high variance (overfitting).
  • Use a training set to fit, a validation set or cross-validation to choose, and a test set, once, to report.
  • Every data-dependent step must happen inside the training folds, or leakage will flatter your results.
  • Learning curves tell you whether to get more data or a better model.
  • No algorithm is best for everything: success depends on matching assumptions to the problem.
