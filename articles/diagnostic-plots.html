1. Plots That Check Your Assumptions

Most of the plots in this series are for exploring data or presenting results. This part is about a different family: diagnostic plots, which check whether an analysis is trustworthy. Is the distribution close enough to normal for a t-test? Does the straight line really fit? Is one data point steering the whole result? Do two instruments agree? Are studies missing from a literature review? Which of thousands of genes really changed?

Diagnostic plots are rarely published, but every careful analyst makes them. Learning to read them is also the best way to read other people’s analyses critically.

3. The Q–Q Plot

Many statistical methods assume that data, or a model’s errors, follow a normal distribution. A histogram is a poor judge of this, because it depends on the bin width (Part 4) and the eye is bad at comparing curves. A quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plot, set out by Martin Wilk and Ram Gnanadesikan in 1968, is better: it sorts the data and plots each value against where it would fall if the data were perfectly normal. [1] Normal data lie on a straight line; deviations show exactly how the shape differs.

How to read a Q–Q plot
  • Straight line: consistent with the reference distribution.
  • Curve bending up (convex): right skew; bending down (concave): left skew.
  • S-shape, steep at the ends: heavy tails; flat at the ends: light tails.
  • Steps or a flat stretch: rounding, ties or a gap between clusters.
  • A few points off at the very ends: often just chance, especially in small samples.
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
samples = {
    "Normal": rng.normal(0, 1, 200),
    "Right-skewed": rng.lognormal(0, 0.6, 200),
    "Heavy-tailed (t, 3 df)": rng.standard_t(3, 200),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(12, 6.5))
for j, (name, x) in enumerate(samples.items()):
    axes[0, j].hist(x, bins=30, color="#0072B2", edgecolor="white")
    axes[0, j].set_title(name)
    stats.probplot(x, dist="norm", plot=axes[1, j])        # sample quantiles vs normal quantiles
    axes[1, j].set_title("Normal Q–Q plot")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

4. Residual Plots

After fitting a model, the residuals, the differences between the observed values and the model’s predictions, contain everything the model failed to explain. Plotting them against the fitted values magnifies problems that are hard to see in the original data. Frank Anscombe and John Tukey made the examination of residuals a standard practice in 1963. [2]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, 100)
cases = {
    "Good fit": 2 + 1.0 * x + rng.normal(0, 1, 100),
    "Missed curve": 2 + 0.15 * x**2 + rng.normal(0, 1, 100),
    "Growing spread": 2 + 1.0 * x + rng.normal(0, 0.25 * x + 0.2, 100),
}

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(12, 6.5))
for j, (name, y) in enumerate(cases.items()):
    b, a = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)                             # straight-line fit
    fitted = a + b * x
    resid = y - fitted
    axes[0, j].scatter(x, y, s=10)
    axes[0, j].plot(np.sort(x), a + b * np.sort(x), color="#D55E00")
    axes[0, j].set_title(name)
    axes[1, j].scatter(fitted, resid, s=10, color="#009E73")
    axes[1, j].axhline(0, color="gray", lw=1)
    axes[1, j].set_xlabel("Fitted value")
    axes[1, j].set_ylabel("Residual")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

A good residual plot is boring: a horizontal band of random scatter around zero. Patterns mean the model is wrong in a specific way: a curve (wrong shape), a fan (non-constant spread), clusters (a missing grouping variable) or isolated points (outliers).

5. Influential Points

Not all points matter equally to a fitted line. A point far out along the x-axis has high leverage: like a long lever, it can tip the line. Whether it does depends on whether it agrees with the rest. Cook’s distance, proposed by R. Dennis Cook in 1977, measures how much the fitted values would change if a point were removed. [3]

# pip install statsmodels
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
x = rng.uniform(0, 10, 30)
y = 3 + 0.5 * x + rng.normal(0, 1, 30)
x = np.append(x, 20)                                       # one far-out point...
y = np.append(y, 2)                                        # ...that disagrees with the rest

model = sm.OLS(y, sm.add_constant(x)).fit()
cooks = model.get_influence().cooks_distance[0]
print("Largest Cook's distance:", cooks.max().round(2), "at point", cooks.argmax())

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
ax1.scatter(x, y, c=cooks, cmap="viridis", s=30)
grid = np.array([0, 20])
ax1.plot(grid, model.params[0] + model.params[1] * grid, color="#D55E00", label="with the point")
m2 = sm.OLS(y[:-1], sm.add_constant(x[:-1])).fit()
ax1.plot(grid, m2.params[0] + m2.params[1] * grid, color="gray", ls="--", label="without it")
ax1.legend()
sm.graphics.influence_plot(model, ax=ax2, criterion="cooks")   # leverage vs studentised residual
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

An influential point is not necessarily wrong. It may be a data error, or the most informative observation in the study. The right response is to check it and to report results with and without it, not to delete it quietly.

6. Bland–Altman Plots

When a new instrument is compared with an established one, researchers often report a high correlation as proof that they agree. In 1986 Martin Bland and Douglas Altman showed in The Lancet why this is wrong: correlation measures whether two methods rise and fall together, not whether they give the same numbers. [4] Their plot of the difference between methods against their average is now standard in medicine and measurement science, and their paper became one of the most cited in the medical literature.

How to read a Bland–Altman plot
  1. The solid line is the mean difference: the average bias of one method relative to the other.
  2. The dashed lines are the 95% limits of agreement: most individual differences fall between them. Are they narrow enough for practical use?
  3. A slope in the points means the bias depends on the size of the measurement.
  4. A spread that widens to the right suggests the error is proportional; analyse percentage differences instead.
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
true = rng.uniform(80, 180, 60)                           # e.g. blood pressure (synthetic)
method_a = true + rng.normal(0, 4, 60)
method_b = 1.12 * true - 14 + rng.normal(0, 4, 60)        # biased, and more so at high values

mean_ab = (method_a + method_b) / 2
diff = method_b - method_a
bias, sd = diff.mean(), diff.std(ddof=1)
print(f"r = {np.corrcoef(method_a, method_b)[0, 1]:.3f} (high, but says nothing about agreement)")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
ax.scatter(mean_ab, diff, s=18, color="#0072B2")
ax.axhline(bias, color="black", label=f"mean difference (bias) = {bias:.1f}")
for k in (-1.96, 1.96):
    ax.axhline(bias + k * sd, color="#D55E00", ls="--")
ax.axhline(0, color="gray", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlabel("Mean of the two methods")
ax.set_ylabel("Difference (B − A)")
ax.set_title("Bland–Altman plot with 95% limits of agreement")
ax.legend(loc="upper left")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

7. Funnel Plots

A meta-analysis (Part 7) can only combine the studies it can find. If studies with disappointing results are less likely to be published, the published evidence is biased. Richard Light and David Pillemer suggested in 1984 plotting each study’s estimate against its size or precision. [5] Small studies scatter widely and large ones cluster near the truth, so the points should form a symmetrical, inverted funnel; a missing corner suggests missing studies. Matthias Egger and colleagues gave a statistical test for this asymmetry in 1997. [6]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
true_effect = 0.2
se = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.5, 80)                           # small studies have large standard errors
est = rng.normal(true_effect, se)
published = (est / se > 1.0) | (se < 0.15)                # small, unimpressive studies stay in the drawer

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 5))
ax.scatter(est[~published], se[~published], facecolors="none", edgecolors="gray", label="unpublished")
ax.scatter(est[published], se[published], color="#0072B2", label="published")
s = np.linspace(0, 0.55, 50)
ax.plot(true_effect - 1.96 * s, s, color="black", lw=0.8)  # funnel: 95% of studies expected inside
ax.plot(true_effect + 1.96 * s, s, color="black", lw=0.8)
ax.axvline(true_effect, color="black", ls="--")
ax.invert_yaxis()                                         # most precise studies at the top
ax.set_xlabel("Estimated effect")
ax.set_ylabel("Standard error")
ax.set_title("Funnel plot: missing studies make it lopsided")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

8. Volcano Plots

Modern biology often tests thousands of genes or proteins at once. A volcano plot shows every test in one picture: the size of the change on the horizontal axis (usually log₂ fold change) and the strength of the evidence on the vertical axis (−log₁₀ of the p-value), so that the most interesting results erupt into the upper corners. [7]

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n_genes, n = 3000, 6
effect = np.where(rng.random(n_genes) < 0.05, rng.normal(0, 2, n_genes), 0)   # 5% truly change
a = rng.normal(0, 1, (n_genes, n))
b = rng.normal(effect[:, None], 1, (n_genes, n))
log2fc = b.mean(1) - a.mean(1)                            # log2 fold change (synthetic)
p = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, axis=1).pvalue

hit = (np.abs(log2fc) > 1) & (p < 0.01)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 5))
ax.scatter(log2fc[~hit], -np.log10(p[~hit]), s=6, color="lightgray")
ax.scatter(log2fc[hit], -np.log10(p[hit]), s=10, color=np.where(log2fc[hit] > 0, "#D55E00", "#0072B2"))
ax.axhline(-np.log10(0.01), color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
for v in (-1, 1):
    ax.axvline(v, color="gray", ls="--", lw=1)
ax.set_xlabel("log₂ fold change")
ax.set_ylabel("−log₁₀ p-value")
ax.set_title(f"Volcano plot: {hit.sum()} genes pass both thresholds")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

A related plot, the Manhattan plot, shows −log₁₀ p against position along the genome in genome-wide association studies, so that strong signals rise like skyscrapers.

9. Common Misreadings

  • Demanding perfect Q–Q lines. Small samples wobble; many methods are robust to mild non-normality, especially with large samples.
  • Checking the normality of the raw data instead of the residuals. Regression assumes normal errors, not normal variables.
  • Deleting influential points to tidy a result. Investigate, then report with and without them.
  • Treating high correlation as agreement. Use Bland–Altman limits of agreement.
  • Reading every funnel asymmetry as fraud or bias. Small studies may genuinely differ, for example in their patients or methods.
  • Trusting every dot above a volcano threshold. With thousands of tests, many “hits” are false positives unless the p-values are corrected.

10. Legend and Record

Legend and Record: statistics in measurement

The legend: if two methods correlate at r = 0.99, they can be used interchangeably.

The record: Bland and Altman pointed out that correlation depends on the range of the samples: measure a wide enough range of people and almost any two methods correlate highly, even if they disagree by clinically important amounts for individuals. A method that always reads twice the true value correlates perfectly with the truth. Their 1986 paper was written to stop this misuse. [4]

A second legend: if a Q–Q plot is not straight, a test cannot be used. The record: the t-test and regression are fairly robust to non-normality, especially with large, similar-sized groups, thanks to the central limit theorem. What matters more is often independence of the observations and outliers, which residual and influence plots reveal.
