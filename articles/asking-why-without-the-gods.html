Asking "Why" Without the Gods: The Birth of Natural Philosophy

Along the maritime trade routes of Ionia, the river valleys of northern India, and the warring states of China, thinkers made a radical conceptual leap: nature is not a plaything of capricious deities, but an orderly, intelligible cosmos governed by universal principles that human reason can deduce.

1. A Harbour Town and a New Kind of Answer

In the 6th century BCE, Miletus was a busy Greek port on the west coast of what is now Turkey. Ships came in from Egypt, from the Phoenician cities of the Levant and from the Black Sea. In the market, traders swapped olive oil and dyed wool for Egyptian papyrus, Babylonian goods and the first coins, from nearby Lydia.

In a town like this, people heard many different stories about the gods, and no single story could claim to be the only one. Here lived Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BCE), remembered as a merchant, engineer and the first Greek philosopher [1, 2]. When an earthquake shook the coast, everyone knew the explanation: Poseidon, the earth-shaker, was angry and had struck the ground with his trident. When thunder rolled, Zeus had thrown a lightning bolt.

Thales suggested something startlingly simple: the Earth is a flat slab floating on water, and when the water heaves, the land shakes [1, 2].

As science, this is completely wrong. But look at what has gone missing: no angry god, no sacrifice, no prayer. Water moving land is explained by water. Nature is explained by nature. The question has changed from "Who did this?" to "What natural process made this happen?" That change of question is where this part of the story begins.

2. What Is Everything Made Of?

Thales and the thinkers who followed him in Miletus asked a big question: is there one basic stuff that everything else is made from? They called it the arche, "the beginning" [1, 2]. Thales said it was water. He wrote nothing down, so we only have later guesses at his reasons. Aristotle thought it was because food and seeds are always moist. Water is also the one substance everyone saw as solid, liquid and vapour, and rivers seem to turn into mud and new land at their mouths.

His younger neighbour Anaximander (c. 610–546 BCE) spotted a problem. If everything is really water, how does fire ever come about? Water puts fire out. So, he argued, the basic stuff can't be any one thing we can see. It must be something without limits or fixed qualities, out of which hot and cold, wet and dry separate. He called it the apeiron, "the boundless" [1, 2].

Anaximander had two other remarkable ideas:

  • The Earth needs no support. Thales had the Earth resting on water, but what holds up the water? Anaximander said the Earth simply hangs in the middle of everything. It is the same distance from the edges in every direction, so it has no reason to fall one way rather than another. It is an early example of a powerful style of argument: if nothing favours one outcome over another, neither happens [2].
  • Humans came from earlier creatures. Human babies are helpless for years, so the first humans could not have survived on their own as babies. He suggested that our ancestors began as fish-like creatures in the water and came onto land only when they could fend for themselves [1, 2]. It is nothing like modern evolution, but it is a natural explanation for where people came from.

The third thinker from Miletus, Anaximenes (c. 586–526 BCE), went back to a substance you can feel: air. His big step was to explain how one thing turns into another: by thinning out and packing together [1, 2]. Thin air out and it becomes fire. Pack it together and it becomes wind, then cloud, water, earth and finally stone. For the first time, the differences between substances were put down to a matter of amount, how tightly packed the stuff is, rather than mysterious qualities.

3. Does Anything Really Change?

By the 5th century BCE these thinkers hit a puzzle that sounds strange today but was taken very seriously: if everything is always changing, how can we know anything for certain? And can change even make sense?

In Ephesus, Heraclitus (c. 535–475 BCE) said change is the basic fact of the world [1, 2]:

"You cannot step twice into the same river, for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you." (The popular summary panta rhei, "everything flows", is a later phrase, not his.)
For Heraclitus the basic element was fire, always moving and changing. But underneath the change he saw a steady rule, which he called the Logos: opposites such as hot and cold or life and death keep each other in balance, like the two pulls on a tightened bowstring [2].

In Elea, a Greek town in southern Italy, Parmenides (c. 515–450 BCE) argued the exact opposite, using pure logic [1, 2]. His reasoning went roughly like this:

  1. What exists, exists; what doesn't exist, doesn't.
  2. You can't truly think about "nothing", because as soon as you think about it, it is something.
  3. So "nothing" (empty space, a void) can't exist.
  4. But to change, a thing must stop being what it was and become what it wasn't, and to come into being, it must come from nothing. Both need "nothing" to exist.
  5. So real change is impossible. Reality must be one single, solid, unchanging whole [1, 2].

His follower Zeno of Elea backed him up with famous puzzles about motion, such as Achilles and the Tortoise and the Flying Arrow. The Arrow goes like this: at any single instant, a flying arrow takes up a space exactly its own size, so at that instant it isn't moving. But the flight is made of instants, so when does it ever move [1, 2]?

This left a real challenge: our senses tell us things move and change, but this logic says they can't. If studying nature was going to work, someone had to find a way to accept change while keeping something underneath that doesn't change.

4. Atoms and Empty Space

The clever way out came from Abdera in northern Greece, from Leucippus and his student Democritus (c. 460–370 BCE) [1, 2].

Democritus agreed with Parmenides that real things can't pop out of nothing or vanish into nothing. So, he said, at the bottom of everything there must be tiny, unchanging pieces that can't be split: atoms, from the Greek atomos, "uncuttable" [1, 2].

Where he broke with Parmenides was over empty space. Democritus said "nothing" does exist, as the void: empty space in which atoms move, bump into each other and rearrange [1, 2]. The atoms themselves never change, which satisfies Parmenides. But the arrangements change all the time, which is the change we see.

Democritus's picture of the world was strikingly modern:

  • Only atoms and empty space. Nothing else is real. There is no plan or purpose behind events; everything happens because atoms collide.
  • Atoms have only shape, order and position. A single atom has no colour, taste, smell or temperature [1, 2]. Honey tastes sweet because its atoms are smooth and round; vinegar tastes sharp because its atoms are jagged and prick the tongue.
  • Colour and taste are in us, not in things. About two thousand years before Galileo and John Locke made the same point, Democritus wrote:
    "By convention sweet, by convention bitter, by convention hot, by convention cold, by convention colour; but in reality atoms and the void." [2]

5. Atoms in India: Kaṇāda

Separately from the Greeks, thinkers in northern India developed their own theory of atoms. It belongs to the Vaiśeṣika school, traced back to a sage called Kaṇāda, author of the Vaiśeṣika Sūtra. His dates are very uncertain, somewhere between the 6th and 2nd centuries BCE [4, 5].

The starting point is a thought experiment. Take a lump of clay, cut it in half, then cut a half in half, and so on. Can you go on forever? The Vaiśeṣika thinkers said no. If you could, then a mustard seed and a mountain would both contain an endless number of parts, and there would be no way to explain why one is so much bigger than the other [4, 5]. So cutting must stop at a smallest piece that can't be divided: the paramāṇu, the ultimate atom.

Democritus's atoms were all the same stuff in different shapes. Kaṇāda's came in four kinds, one for each of four elements; a fifth element, space or ether (ākāśa), was not made of atoms [4, 5]:

  1. Earth (pṛthvī): solid and heavy; it carries smell.
  2. Water (jala): flowing and cool; it carries taste.
  3. Fire (tejas): hot and bright; it carries colour.
  4. Air (vāyu): always moving; it carries touch.

How do invisible atoms make visible things? A single atom is too small to see. Set moving by an unseen force (adṛṣṭa, linked to the moral order of the world), two atoms of the same element join to make a pair (dvyaṇuka) [4, 5]. Three pairs join to make a triad (tryaṇuka), the smallest thing big enough to see, like a speck of dust floating in a sunbeam. Changes such as a grey clay pot turning red in the kiln were explained by heat getting in between the particles, breaking them apart, changing their qualities and letting them recombine. This was called pīlupāka, "cooking of the atoms" [4, 5].

6. How Do We Know? The Nyāya School

Indian philosophers argued about such theories in public debates, and to argue well they needed clear rules for what counts as good evidence. The Nyāya school worked these out in the Nyāya Sūtra, traditionally credited to Akṣapāda Gautama and put together by about the 2nd century CE (its date is debated) [4].

Their central question is still the central question of science: what are the reliable ways of getting to know something? They accepted four, called pramāṇas [4]:

  • Perception (pratyakṣa): seeing, hearing, touching and so on, when the senses are working properly and not being fooled.
  • Inference (anumāna): reasoning from what you can observe to what you can't, using a rule you know holds.
  • Comparison (upamāna): learning about something new by comparing it with something you already know ("a wild ox looks like a cow").
  • Trustworthy testimony (śabda): the word of a reliable expert who knows the subject first-hand and has no reason to deceive [4].

Nyāya's best-known tool is a five-step argument [4]. Aristotle's famous pattern in Greece had three steps (all humans are mortal; Socrates is a human; so Socrates is mortal). The Nyāya version adds a real-world example, so the argument stays tied to experience:

  1. Claim: "There is fire on that hill."
  2. Reason: "Because there is smoke on it."
  3. Rule, with an example: "Wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen hearth (and unlike a lake, which has neither)."
  4. Applying the rule: "This hill has smoke, which always goes with fire."
  5. Conclusion: "So there is fire on the hill." [4]

Everything depends on the rule in step 3 being a link that never fails. The Nyāya called this vyāpti, "pervasion". It only works in one direction. Suppose someone argues, "That red-hot iron ball must be smoking, because it is hot like fire." Wrong: red-hot iron gives off no smoke. Smoke always goes with fire, but fire doesn't always go with smoke. Spotting a broken link like this was called finding a hetvābhāsa, a "false reason" [4]. For the Nyāya, logic wasn't word games; it was a tool for separating truth from mistakes.

7. China: Pinhole Images and the Five Phases

During China's Warring States period (about 475–221 BCE), many schools of thought argued about how to live and how to rule. One group stood out: the Mohists, followers of Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE), who did some of the earliest experimental physics in East Asia [6].

Unlike Confucian scholars, who looked down on manual work, many Mohists were craftsmen and military engineers who built city defences. In a section of the book Mozi called the Mohist Canon, written by later members of the school in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE, they recorded careful findings about light and mirrors, and about levers and balances [6].

The Mohist Canon contains the oldest surviving description of a pinhole image (a camera obscura, "dark room") [6]:

The Mohist Canon on pinhole images (paraphrased)

"The image is upside down because the light crosses at the small hole... Light from the top of the person shines down onto the lower part of the screen; light from the feet shines up onto the higher part. Because the rays cross at the hole, the image is upside down." [6]

The key idea is that light travels in straight lines. The Mohists also studied flat, curved-in and curved-out mirrors, and described how a curved-in (concave) mirror can give either an upside-down image or a larger upright one, depending on how far away the object is [6].

Around the same time, the thinker Zou Yan (305–240 BCE) and the "Naturalist" school organised Chinese ideas about nature into the Five Phases (wuxing): Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water [6]. These weren't fixed building blocks like Greek elements. They were stages in endless cycles of change: Wood feeds Fire; Fire makes Earth (ash); Earth produces Metal; Metal collects Water (as dew); Water feeds Wood. Nature was pictured not as a fixed structure but as a web of cycles.

8. Aristotle the Biologist

Back in Greece, all these strands came together in one person: Aristotle (384–322 BCE), from Stagira in northern Greece [1, 3].

Aristotle studied for twenty years at Plato's Academy in Athens. Plato distrusted the senses. For him the most real things were perfect, unchanging "Forms", such as the perfect circle or perfect justice, which we grasp with the mind, and the world we see is only a shadowy copy (like shadows on the wall of a cave, in his famous story). After Plato died, Aristotle went his own way. The form of a thing, he said, isn't somewhere else; it is in the thing itself, and we learn about it by looking closely at the world.

Between about 345 and 343 BCE Aristotle lived on the island of Lesbos, by the lagoon at Pyrrha [1, 3]. There he became the first great field biologist. He cut open and studied cuttlefish, octopuses, sea urchins, small sharks and chicken eggs at different stages. In his books on animals he described about 500 kinds of animal, often in remarkable detail [1, 3].

Some examples: he described the special arm a male octopus uses to mate, which nobody confirmed until the 1800s. He saw that dolphins and whales breathe air, give birth to live young and feed them milk, unlike fish. He watched the heart of a chick beating as a tiny red dot after about three days in the egg. And he described the jaws of the sea urchin, which zoologists still call "Aristotle's lantern" [1, 3]. His careful observations shaped biology for about two thousand years.

9. Aristotle's Universe

Aristotle didn't stop at animals. He built a complete explanation of how everything in the universe moves [1, 3].

He split the universe into two very different parts:

  1. Below the Moon: our changing world, where things are born, decay and turn into one another. It is made of four elements, each a mix of two basic qualities:
    • Earth: cold and dry; heavy, it heads for the centre.
    • Water: cold and wet; heavy, it settles on top of earth.
    • Air: hot and wet; light, it rises above water.
    • Fire: hot and dry; lightest, it rises up towards the Moon [1, 3].
  2. From the Moon outwards: the heavens, which never change or decay. The Sun, Moon, planets and stars are made of a fifth element, aether (later called the "quintessence", the fifth essence), whose natural motion is to go round in perfect circles forever [1, 3].

For Aristotle, every motion on Earth needed a cause:

  • Natural motion: each element tends to go to its proper place, by the straightest route. A stone falls because it is mostly earth heading for the centre of the universe; smoke rises because it is mostly fire heading up [1, 3].
  • Forced motion: anything moving away from its natural place, such as a thrown spear, needs something pushing it all the time. But once the spear leaves the hand, what pushes it? Aristotle's answer was that the air, set moving by the throw, keeps pushing it along [1, 3]. This weak point would bother thinkers for nearly two thousand years.

Later readers summed up Aristotle's ideas about speed with a simple rule (he never wrote it as a formula): speed goes up with the push and down with the resistance of whatever the object moves through, $$\text{speed} \propto \frac{\text{push}}{\text{resistance}}$$ [1]. This led him to say that empty space, a vacuum, can't exist. In a vacuum there would be no resistance at all, so any push would make an object move infinitely fast, which is absurd. For Aristotle the universe was completely full, with no gaps.

10. Measuring the Earth with a Shadow

In the 3rd century BCE the centre of Greek learning moved to Alexandria in Egypt, with its royally funded Museum and great Library. Around 240 BCE the head of the Library, Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–194 BCE), did something extraordinary: he worked out the size of the whole Earth with a shadow, one known distance and some simple geometry [1, 8].

He had heard that at Syene (today's Aswan), far to the south, the Sun at noon on midsummer's day stood straight overhead: an upright post cast no shadow, and sunlight lit up the bottom of a deep well [8].

Alexandria lies roughly due north of Syene. At noon on the same day in Alexandria, an upright post did cast a shadow. Measuring it showed the Sun was 7.2° away from straight overhead, and 7.2° is exactly one-fiftieth of a full circle (360° ÷ 50) [8].

Why does that tell you the size of the Earth? The Sun is so far away that its rays arrive at Alexandria and Syene as parallel lines. Draw a line from each city down to the centre of the Earth. Simple geometry then shows that the shadow angle at Alexandria is the same as the angle between the two cities measured at the Earth's centre [8]. So the distance from Alexandria to Syene must be one-fiftieth of the way round the whole Earth.

That distance was taken to be 5,000 stadia (we don't know how it was measured). So: $$\text{distance round the Earth} = 50 \times 5{,}000 = 250{,}000 \text{ stadia}$$ (Later writers give 252,000 stadia, perhaps rounded to make exactly 700 stadia per degree.)

How much is that in kilometres? Here is a real uncertainty: we don't know exactly how long Eratosthenes' stadion was. Suggestions range from about 157.5 to about 185 metres [8]. With the shorter one: $$250{,}000 \times 0.1575 \text{ km} \approx 39{,}375 \text{ km}$$ The real distance round the Earth through the poles is about 40,008 km. With the short stadion he was within about 2%; with the longer one, about 16% too big. Either way, the method was sound and the answer was the right size [8].

How Did They Know the Earth Is Round?

A popular myth says ancient people thought the Earth was flat. In fact, from the 4th century BCE onward educated Greeks generally accepted that it is a sphere. How could they tell, with no spacecraft? In his book On the Heavens, Aristotle gave the first two reasons below; the third comes from later writers such as Strabo [1, 3].

1. The Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved

In an eclipse of the Moon, the Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and its shadow falls across the Moon. Aristotle noticed that the edge of this shadow is always a curve, whatever the time of night or year and wherever the Moon is in the sky. A flat disc would sometimes cast a narrow oval or even a straight-edged shadow, when seen edge-on. Only a ball casts a round shadow from every direction.

2. Different stars as you travel

Aristotle noted that if you travel even a modest distance south, for example from Greece to Egypt, the sky changes. Stars that never set in Greece now dip below the horizon, and new stars, such as the bright star Canopus, rise above the southern horizon. Travel north and southern stars disappear. On a flat Earth everyone would see the same stars. That the view changes so quickly showed not only that the Earth is curved, but that it isn't very large [1, 3].

3. Ships disappear bottom first

A ship sailing away doesn't just shrink to a dot. Its hull disappears first, then the lower sails, until only the top of the mast is left before it too drops out of sight. And a lookout up the mast sees land before the sailors on deck. The curve of the sea is in the way (an argument found in Strabo's Geography, not in Aristotle).

Legend and Record: Archimedes, "Eureka!" and the Golden Crown

The popular story: The Roman architect Vitruvius, writing about two hundred years later, tells this tale. King Hiero II of Syracuse gave a goldsmith pure gold to make a crown. He suspected the goldsmith had kept some gold and mixed in cheaper silver, and asked Archimedes (c. 287–212 BCE) to find out without damaging the crown. Getting into a full bath, Archimedes saw the water spill over and realised that the spilled water matched the volume of his body. He leapt out and ran naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!" ("I've found it!"). Then he dunked the crown in a bucket of water and measured the overflow.

What the evidence shows: In 1586, in a short early essay called La Bilancetta ("The Little Balance"), Galileo pointed out that the bucket method doesn't really work. Water clings to the rim and sides of a container, so a small overflow is hard to measure. And the difference is tiny: a 1 kg crown that is 30% silver would push out only about 13 cubic centimetres more water than pure gold, about a tablespoon. Trying to measure a tablespoon of spilled water from a sloshing bucket gives errors bigger than the thing you are trying to find.

Archimedes was one of the greatest mathematicians who ever lived, and in his surviving book On Floating Bodies he set out the law now named after him: an object in water is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of the water it pushes aside [7].

We don't know how, or whether, Archimedes really tested a crown. Galileo suggested he would have used a balance in water, a method that follows from On Floating Bodies and is described in a Latin poem from about 400 CE:

  1. Hang the crown on one side of a balance and an equal weight of pure gold on the other. In air, the balance is level.
  2. Now lower both into water.
  3. Silver is much less dense than gold (10.5 g per cm³ against 19.3), so a crown with silver in it takes up more space than the same weight of pure gold.
  4. Taking up more space, it pushes aside more water, so the water pushes it up harder.
  5. The balance tips: the pure gold goes down and the crown rises [7].

Philosophy Corner: Explaining Nature by Nature

What changed between about 600 and 200 BCE was a new habit: explaining the world using only natural causes, observation and reasoning, without bringing in the gods. Philosophers call this naturalism [10]. It comes in two quite different kinds:

  • Naturalism as a working method: when a bridge falls down or an eclipse happens, look for a natural cause, such as cracked supports or the positions of the Sun and Moon. Scientists who believe in God work this way in the lab too.
  • Naturalism as a belief about everything: the view that nothing exists except the natural world: no soul, no God, no higher purpose. Democritus and later Epicurus held this view [10].

Aristotle added a famous checklist for understanding anything, his four causes, or four kinds of "because" [1, 3]. To understand a thing, ask:

  1. What is it made of? (the material cause): bronze for a statue; bone, muscle and feathers for a bird.
  2. What is its form or design? (the formal cause): the shape of the statue; the layout of a bird's wing.
  3. What made it? (the efficient cause): the sculptor with a chisel; the parent birds.
  4. What is it for? (the final cause): to honour a hero; to let the bird fly [1, 3].

For Aristotle, nature was full of purpose: "nature does nothing in vain". An acorn exists to become an oak; an eye exists to see. When modern science arose in the 1600s with Galileo, Descartes and Newton, it made huge progress by dropping the "what is it for?" question from physics and astronomy. Planets don't orbit because they want to; stones don't fall because they long for the centre of the Earth. Physics kept only "what is it made of?" and "what pushed it?". Yet in biology, talk of purpose (what a heart is for, how a feature helps survival) is still a useful shorthand, now explained by evolution.

14. People in This Part

These are the thinkers, from the Mediterranean to India and China, whose ideas fill this chapter:

Person Dates Where Why they matter
Thales of Miletus c. 624–546 BCE Miletus (western Turkey) Remembered as the first Greek philosopher; said everything comes from water and explained earthquakes without gods [1, 2].
Anaximander c. 610–546 BCE Miletus Proposed "the boundless" as the source of everything, an Earth hanging unsupported in space, and humans descended from fish-like creatures [1, 2].
Parmenides of Elea c. 515–450 BCE Elea (southern Italy) Argued by pure logic that change and empty space are impossible, setting a puzzle later thinkers had to solve [1, 2].
Democritus c. 460–370 BCE Abdera (northern Greece) With Leucippus, founded atomism: everything is atoms moving in empty space; colour and taste are in us, not in the atoms [1, 2].
Kaṇāda Uncertain (between the 6th and 2nd centuries BCE) India Traditional author of the Vaiśeṣika Sūtra; Indian atomism with four kinds of atom joining in pairs and threes [4, 5].
Akṣapāda Gautama Uncertain (the Nyāya Sūtra was compiled by about the 2nd century CE) India Traditional author of the Nyāya Sūtra: the ways of knowing (pramāṇas) and the five-step argument [4].
Mozi c. 470–391 BCE China Philosopher of care for all people; his later followers wrote the Mohist Canon, with the oldest surviving description of a pinhole image [6].
Aristotle 384–322 BCE Stagira, Athens and Lesbos Pulled together ancient knowledge of nature; described about 500 kinds of animal; taught a universe split into the changing world below the Moon and the unchanging heavens [1, 3].
Eratosthenes c. 276–194 BCE Cyrene and Alexandria Head of the Library of Alexandria; measured the Earth as 250,000 stadia round, within about 2–16% of the true value depending on the stadion used [8].
Archimedes c. 287–212 BCE Syracuse (Sicily) Perhaps the greatest mathematician of the ancient world; worked out the law of the lever, centres of gravity and the law of floating bodies [7].

15. A World That Makes Sense

By the 3rd century BCE something important had happened across Greece, India and China.

The world was no longer seen as the playground of moody gods, to be handled with sacrifices and omens. It had become a world that follows rules. Whether people pictured atoms colliding in empty space, elements heading for their natural places, the cycles of the Five Phases, or careful reasoning from smoke to fire, they had come to believe that the world could be understood by human reason.

But there was a new problem. Arguments in words could go round in circles forever: change or no change, four elements or atoms. To settle questions for good, people needed a language where an argument, once made, could not be wriggled out of: the language of mathematical proof.

In the next part we follow that story, from notches on bones to the certainty of geometry: Euclid's Elements, Archimedes' levers, the invention of zero in India and the birth of algebra in Baghdad.
