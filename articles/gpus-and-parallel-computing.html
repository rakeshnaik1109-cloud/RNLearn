GPUs and Parallel Computing: Warps, Rooflines and Tensor Cores

When clock speeds stopped rising, computers had to do many things at once. A chip designed to draw video-game pixels turned out to be the ideal engine for science and artificial intelligence.

1. Many Hands Make Light Work

When clock speeds stopped rising in the mid-2000s, the only way to keep computers getting faster was to do more things at once. In 2005 Herb Sutter warned programmers that “the free lunch is over”: software would no longer speed up by itself on each new processor unless it was written to run in parallel. [1] Some problems, such as drawing millions of pixels or multiplying large matrices, are naturally parallel. For them a different kind of processor, with thousands of simple arithmetic units instead of a few complex cores, wins by a wide margin. That processor is the GPU, now the engine of scientific computing and artificial intelligence. [2]

3. The Limits of Parallelism: Amdahl and Gustafson

In 1967 Gene Amdahl, chief architect of IBM’s System/360, argued that the part of a job that cannot be parallelised limits the benefit of adding processors. [3] If a fraction p of a program’s run time can be spread over N processors and the rest is serial, the speedup is

S = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/N) ≤ 1/(1 − p)

With 95% parallel work, no number of processors gives more than 20×. In 1988 John Gustafson pointed out that this assumes a fixed problem. In practice, scientists use bigger machines to solve bigger problems, whose parallel part grows while the serial part stays roughly the same; measured this way, the scaled speedup is (1 − p) + pN, close to linear. [4] Both views matter: Amdahl’s explains why serial bottlenecks must be hunted down, Gustafson’s why supercomputers with millions of cores are useful.

4. Kinds of Parallelism

In 1966 Michael Flynn classified computers by how many instruction streams and data streams they handle. [5] A classic processor is SISD (single instruction, single data). SIMD machines apply one instruction to many data elements at once; MIMD machines run many independent instruction streams, as multicore processors and clusters do.

  • Vector processors such as the Cray-1 of 1976 applied each instruction to whole arrays streamed through deeply pipelined arithmetic units. [6] Their descendants are the SIMD extensions (such as SSE, AVX and Arm’s NEON and SVE) inside every modern CPU.
  • Multicore processors, which reached desktops in 2005, put several complete cores on one chip, sharing caches and memory.
  • GPUs combine both ideas on a massive scale: dozens to hundreds of cores, each running many threads on wide SIMD units.

5. From Graphics to General-Purpose GPUs

Drawing a 3D scene means transforming millions of triangle corners and computing the colour of millions of pixels, each independently of the others: a perfect parallel workload. Graphics chips of the 1990s did this with fixed-function hardware; in 1999 Nvidia marketed its GeForce 256 as a “graphics processing unit”. From about 2001, the per-pixel and per-vertex stages became programmable “shaders”, and researchers began disguising scientific calculations as graphics to exploit them. [7]

The turning point came in 2006–2007. Nvidia’s G80 architecture replaced separate vertex and pixel units with an array of unified, general-purpose processors, and its CUDA platform let programmers write ordinary C-like code for them. [8, 9] In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet, trained on two consumer GPUs, won the ImageNet image-recognition competition by a large margin, and deep learning took off on GPU hardware. [10]

6. How a GPU Works: SIMT, Warps and Latency Hiding

A GPU program launches a kernel, the same function run by many thousands of threads, each working on a different piece of data. The hardware groups threads into warps (32 threads on Nvidia GPUs) that execute the same instruction together on a wide SIMD unit, a style Nvidia calls SIMT: single instruction, multiple threads. [8] Each streaming multiprocessor holds dozens of warps at once.

Two consequences follow. First, if threads in a warp take different branches of an if-statement, the warp runs both paths one after the other with some threads switched off: branch divergence wastes throughput. Second, a GPU does not rely on big caches and branch prediction to keep its units busy. It hides memory latency by keeping many warps ready and switching to another warp, at no cost, whenever one is waiting for data. A CPU core is a sports car designed to finish one task quickly; a GPU is a fleet of buses designed to move as many passengers as possible.

7. Memory Bandwidth and the Roofline

Thousands of arithmetic units are useless if data cannot reach them. A kernel’s arithmetic intensity is the number of operations it performs per byte read from or written to memory. In 2009 Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson proposed the roofline model: attainable performance = min(peak compute, memory bandwidth × arithmetic intensity). [11] Kernels with low intensity, such as adding two arrays or multiplying a sparse matrix by a vector, are limited by bandwidth; those with high intensity, such as multiplying large dense matrices, can approach peak compute. To feed their arithmetic, high-end GPUs use stacks of DRAM chips mounted beside the processor (high-bandwidth memory), delivering several terabytes per second.

8. Tensor Cores and AI Accelerators

Neural networks spend most of their time multiplying matrices. In 2017 Nvidia’s Volta GPUs added tensor cores, units that perform a small matrix multiply–accumulate (such as 4 × 4 matrices of 16-bit numbers) in one operation, many times faster than ordinary arithmetic units. [12] The same year Google described its Tensor Processing Unit, a chip built around a 256 × 256 array of 8-bit multipliers through which data flows in a systolic pattern; Google reported it was much faster and more energy-efficient than contemporary CPUs and GPUs for neural-network inference. [13] Later generations of both use ever lower precision, such as 8-bit and even 4-bit floating-point formats, because neural networks tolerate it and narrower numbers need less energy and bandwidth.

The deepest reason for specialised hardware is energy. Mark Horowitz’s widely quoted figures for a 45 nm process show a 32-bit floating-point addition costing about 0.9 pJ, while reading 32 bits from DRAM costs about 640 pJ, hundreds of times more. [14] Computation is cheap; moving data is expensive. Accelerators therefore keep data close to the arithmetic, reuse each value as many times as possible, and use the narrowest numbers that give acceptable results.

9. Worked Problems

Problem 1 (easy): Amdahl’s law

A program is 95% parallelisable. What speedup does it get on 64 processors, and what is the maximum possible?

Solution

S = 1/(0.05 + 0.95/64) = 1/(0.05 + 0.0148) ≈ 15.4×. The limit is 1/0.05 = 20×. Already at 64 processors the serial 5% takes more than three-quarters of the run time.

Problem 2 (easy): peak throughput of a GPU

A GPU has 100 streaming multiprocessors, each with 128 single-precision arithmetic lanes, running at 1.8 GHz. Each lane can do one fused multiply–add (counted as 2 floating-point operations) per cycle. What is its peak rate?

Solution

100 × 128 × 2 × 1.8 × 109 ≈ 4.6 × 1013 FLOP/s = 46 TFLOP/s. A CPU core with two 512-bit FMA units at 4 GHz manages 2 × 16 × 2 × 4 × 109 = 256 GFLOP/s, so the GPU equals about 180 such cores.

Problem 3 (medium): roofline analysis

On a GPU with 50 TFLOP/s peak and 2 TB/s bandwidth, estimate the attainable performance of (a) adding two arrays of 32-bit floats, c[i] = a[i] + b[i], and (b) multiplying two 4096 × 4096 matrices of 32-bit floats, assuming each matrix crosses the memory bus once.

Solution

(a) One addition per 12 bytes (two reads, one write): intensity ≈ 0.083 FLOP/B, so performance ≈ 2 × 1012 × 0.083 ≈ 0.17 TFLOP/s, 0.3% of peak. (b) 2n³ = 1.4 × 1011 FLOPs for 3n² × 4 bytes = 2.0 × 108 bytes: intensity ≈ n/6 ≈ 680 FLOP/B, above the ridge point of 25 FLOP/B, so it is compute-bound and can approach 50 TFLOP/s (in practice, with careful use of on-chip memory).

Problem 4 (medium): why GPUs need so many threads

Little’s law says that the data in flight equals bandwidth × latency. With 2 TB/s of bandwidth and a memory latency of 500 ns, how many bytes must be in flight, and how many outstanding 128-byte requests is that?

Solution

2 × 1012 B/s × 5 × 10−7 s = 106 bytes, about 1 MB in flight at all times. That is 106/128 ≈ 7,800 requests outstanding. Only tens of thousands of threads, each waiting on its own loads, can generate that many, which is exactly what GPUs are designed to hold.

Problem 5 (harder): Amdahl or Gustafson?

A simulation spends 5% of its time on one processor in serial set-up and 95% in parallel work. On a 64-processor machine, scientists enlarge the grid so that the run still takes the same time. Compute Gustafson’s scaled speedup and compare it with Amdahl’s. Which describes the scientists’ experience?

Solution

Taking the 5% serial fraction as measured on the parallel run, the scaled speedup is S = 0.05 + 0.95 × 64 = 60.9×: the 64-processor run does as much work as 61 processor-runs would on one machine. Amdahl’s fixed-size speedup is 15.4× (Problem 1). The scientists experience the Gustafson figure, because they used the machine to solve a 61-times larger problem in the same time, not the same problem faster. Someone who needs the original problem solved sooner experiences Amdahl.

10. Open Questions

Open questions: specialisation versus flexibility

The largest AI systems are trained on tens of thousands of accelerators, and their speed is often limited not by arithmetic but by memory bandwidth and by the networks connecting the chips. How far specialisation should go is unsettled: highly specialised chips are efficient for today’s algorithms but risk being stranded if algorithms change, while general-purpose GPUs adapt but spend energy on flexibility. Lower numerical precision, sparsity, in-memory computing and optical interconnects are all being explored, and the right balance between programmability and efficiency remains an open design question.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “a GPU is just a faster CPU”

The myth: GPUs are simply more powerful processors, so everything runs faster on them.

The reality: a GPU core is slower and simpler than a CPU core, with weak branch prediction and small caches per thread. It wins only when a task can be split into thousands of similar, independent pieces with regular memory access. Serial code, branchy logic and pointer-chasing run better on a CPU. Real systems pair both, with the CPU orchestrating and the GPU doing the bulk arithmetic.

Why it matters: choosing the right processor, and restructuring algorithms to suit it, matters more than raw peak numbers.

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Amdahl: speedup ≤ 1/(1 − p) for a fixed problem; Gustafson: scaled speedup ≈ (1 − p) + pN when problems grow.
  • SIMD applies one instruction to many data; MIMD runs many instruction streams; GPUs combine both at massive scale.
  • GPUs run warps of threads in SIMT fashion and hide memory latency by switching between many warps.
  • The roofline model: performance = min(peak compute, bandwidth × arithmetic intensity).
  • Tensor cores and TPUs accelerate matrix multiplication at low precision; moving data costs far more energy than computing on it.
