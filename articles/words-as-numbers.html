Words as Numbers: Tokens, Embeddings and the First Neural Language Models

To a counting model, “cat” and “dog” are unrelated symbols. Turn every word into a list of numbers learned from the company it keeps, and similar words become neighbours, letting a model predict what it has never seen.

1. From Symbols to Numbers

Part 1 ended with a counting model that could not see that “cat” and “dog” are alike. To it, every word was an unrelated symbol, and nothing learned about one transferred to another. Modern language models solve this in two steps. First, text is cut into tokens, the units the model actually reads and writes. Second, each token is turned into a list of numbers, a vector, learned so that tokens used in similar ways get similar vectors. Arithmetic on these vectors is what lets a model generalise. [1, 2]

3. Tokens: The Pieces a Model Reads

Should a model read whole words or single letters? Whole words make the vocabulary enormous, and any new word, name or typo is unknown. Letters make every text very long, and each letter carries little meaning. The compromise is subword tokens. The most common method, byte-pair encoding (BPE), began as a data-compression trick described by Philip Gage in 1994; [3] Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch adapted it in 2016 to split rare words for machine translation. [4] It starts from single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token.

With no merges, the toy words average 4.8 tokens each, one per character plus an end-of-word marker. After 30 merges they average 2.3, and after 120 merges every word of the toy world is a single token. The unseen word “teachers” is still handled: it becomes “tea”, “che”, “r”, “s▁”, pieces learned from other words. Real tokenisers learn vocabularies of tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand tokens; GPT-2’s had 50,257. [5] Most common English words are a single token, and longer or rarer words are split into two or more.

4. Why Tokens Matter

Tokenisation looks like a technical detail, but it shapes what a model can do and what it costs:

  • Cost and length. Models are priced, and their context windows measured, in tokens. A tokeniser trained mostly on English splits other languages into more pieces. Aleksandar Petrov and colleagues found that the same text can need more than ten times as many tokens in some languages as in English, making those users pay more and fit less text into the model. [6]
  • Hidden letters. The model sees token numbers, not letters. Questions about spelling, such as counting the letters in a word, require knowledge the tokens hide. Part 6 returns to this.
  • Numbers. A number such as 1,234,567 may be split into arbitrary chunks, which makes arithmetic harder than it looks.

Modern tokenisers such as SentencePiece, by Taku Kudo and John Richardson, work directly on raw text, including spaces, in any language. [7] From here on, “word” and “token” are used loosely as synonyms; strictly, a language model predicts the next token.

5. Know a Word by Its Company

How can a machine learn that “milk” and “water” are similar without being told? In 1954 the linguist Zellig Harris argued that words that occur in similar environments tend to have similar meanings, the distributional hypothesis; [8] in 1957 John Rupert Firth put it memorably: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” [9] Counting the company each word keeps gives it a profile, and comparing profiles measures similarity.

“Milk” and “water” appear in identical company, after “drinks the” and before a full stop, and their profiles have a similarity of 1.00. “Cat” and “dog” score 0.88: they share most company but differ in what they eat. “Cat” and “tea” score 0.02. To sharpen the comparison, raw counts are converted to pointwise mutual information, which asks how much more often two words occur together than chance would predict, so that ubiquitous words such as “the” count for little.

6. Words as Vectors

A profile over every possible neighbour is long and mostly zeros. Compressing it to a few dozen or hundred numbers keeps the main patterns and discards noise. Latent semantic analysis, by Scott Deerwester and colleagues in 1990, compressed word–document counts this way; [10] in 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues’ word2vec learned such vectors directly, by training a small network to predict neighbouring words, on billions of words in hours, [11] and GloVe, by Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher and Christopher Manning, fitted them to global co-occurrence counts. [12] Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg later showed that word2vec is implicitly compressing the same kind of mutual-information table. [13] The resulting vectors are called word embeddings.

The nearest neighbours of “horse” are “bird” and “cat” (similarity 0.99) and “dog” (0.98); those of “fish” are “bread”, “cheese” and “apple”; those of “teacher” are the other people. Animals, people, foods, drinks and places form separate groups, although the text never labels them. The geometry of embedding spaces can be surprisingly rich: Mikolov and colleagues found that the vector from “man” to “woman” roughly matches the one from “king” to “queen”, so that king − man + woman lands near queen. [14]

The maths: PMI and cosine similarity

For a word w and a context c,

$$\text{PMI}(w, c) = \log \frac{P(w, c)}{P(w)\,P(c)}, \qquad \cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}.$$

PMI is positive when w and c occur together more often than if they were independent. Negative values are set to zero (positive PMI). 7. A Neural Network Predicts the Next Word

In 2003 Yoshua Bengio and colleagues put the two ideas together: a neural language model that looks up a learned vector for each previous word and feeds those vectors through a neural network to produce a probability for every possible next word. [15] The vectors and the network are trained together, by gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss (Inside the Machine Parts 7 and 8). Because similar words get similar vectors, evidence about one word automatically informs predictions for its neighbours.

The count model gives “the horse drinks” a smoothed probability of about 0.0001. A neural model with 2-number word vectors gives it 0.079, after 400 training steps: “horse” shares the region of vector space where cats, dogs and birds live, and inherits their habit of drinking. With 8-number vectors, the model has room to give the horse its own corner and memorises the exception (0.0001 again). Whether that is right depends on the world: perhaps horses really never drink in this world, or perhaps the text simply never mentioned it. A model trained on text cannot tell the difference, a fact that matters greatly in Part 6.

In code: an embedding layer and a next-word predictor in PyTorch 
import torch
from torch import nn

V, d = 39, 2                                  # vocabulary size, vector size
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Embedding(V, d),     # one learned vector per token
                      nn.Linear(d, V))        # scores for every next token
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.05)

prev = torch.tensor([3, 3, 7])                # previous-token ids (a batch)
nxt  = torch.tensor([5, 9, 5])                # the tokens that followed
for step in range(400):
    loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(model(prev), nxt)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
probs = torch.softmax(model(torch.tensor([3])), dim=-1)   # P(next | token 3)

8. Embeddings in Real Models

Every modern LLM begins with an embedding table: one learned vector per token. These vectors are long. GPT-3, for example, used vectors of 12,288 numbers for a vocabulary of about 50,000 tokens, so its embedding table alone held over 600 million numbers. [16] In a large model, the embedding is only the starting point. A word such as “bank” gets the same initial vector whether it means a river bank or a savings bank; the layers that follow, described in Part 3, mix in information from the surrounding words, producing contextual vectors that differ from one sentence to the next.

9. Open Questions

Open questions: is usage enough for meaning?

Embeddings capture how words are used, not what they refer to. Emily Bender and Alexander Koller argued in 2020 that a system trained only on text, on form, cannot in principle learn meaning, which connects words to the world and to speakers’ intentions. [17] Others point out that text contains a great deal of information about the world, and that later parts of this series show models acquiring internal representations of things such as space and time. How much meaning can be extracted from the patterns of words alone is one of the deepest open questions about language models.

10. Worked Example: Similarity by Numbers

Suppose three words have 3-number vectors: cat = (0.9, 0.8, 0.1), dog = (0.8, 0.9, 0.2) and tea = (0.1, 0.2, 0.9).

  1. cat · dog = 0.72 + 0.72 + 0.02 = 1.46; |cat| = √(0.81 + 0.64 + 0.01) = 1.208; |dog| = √(0.64 + 0.81 + 0.04) = 1.221.
  2. cos(cat, dog) = 1.46 / (1.208 × 1.221) = 0.99: nearly the same direction.
  3. cat · tea = 0.09 + 0.16 + 0.09 = 0.34; |tea| = √(0.01 + 0.04 + 0.81) = 0.927; cos(cat, tea) = 0.34 / (1.208 × 0.927) = 0.30.
  4. A model that has learned that cats drink milk will, through the similar vector, expect the same of dogs, but not of tea.

12. Myth vs Reality

Myth vs Reality: “word vectors are neutral mathematics”

The myth: embeddings are computed objectively from data, so they carry no opinions.

The reality: embeddings faithfully absorb the associations in their training text, including social stereotypes. Tolga Bolukbasi and colleagues found that word2vec vectors trained on news articles completed the analogy “man is to computer programmer as woman is to …” with “homemaker”. [18] Aylin Caliskan, Joanna Bryson and Arvind Narayanan showed that standard embeddings reproduce a wide range of human biases measured by psychologists, for example associating European-American names more strongly than African-American names with pleasant words. [19]

Why it matters: “the company a word keeps” includes the prejudices of the people who wrote the text. Large language models inherit such patterns too, which is one reason they are tested and adjusted after training (Part 5).

13. Key Takeaways

Key takeaways
  • Language models read and write tokens: words or pieces of words learned by methods such as byte-pair encoding.
  • Subword tokens handle any new word, but they cost different amounts in different languages and hide individual letters.
  • Words used in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings: the distributional hypothesis.
  • Embeddings represent each token as a vector; similar tokens get similar vectors, measured by cosine similarity.
  • A neural language model predicts the next token from these vectors, so knowledge about one word transfers to similar words.
  • Too little room forces generalisation, too much allows memorisation; and embeddings absorb the biases of their text.
